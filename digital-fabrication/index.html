What Is Digital Fabrication

Turning digital designs into physical objects. Digital fabrication combines CAD (Computer Aided Design) and CAM (Computer Aided Manufacturing).

Examples: CNC mills, 3D printers, laser cutters, waterjets, vinyl cutters.

Digital design → real object

CAD
Draw the part's geometry. Fusion 360, Onshape, SolidWorks.
→
CAM
Turn geometry into machine paths. Fusion CAM, MasterCAM, a slicer, laser software.
→
Machine
Follows the instructions (usually G-code) to move and cut, print, or burn.
→
Part
The physical object you hold, check, and fix in CAD if needed.

Three ways machines make parts

Additive
Builds material up layer by layer. 3D printers.
Subtractive
Removes material from a solid block. CNC mills and routers.
2D cutting
Cuts profiles out of flat sheets. Laser cutters, waterjets, vinyl cutters.

CNC & Numerical Control

What is CNC?

  • CNC = Computer Numerical Control
  • Machines follow coded instructions to move precisely.
  • Automates cutting, milling, drilling, and additive processes.
  • Usually referring to CNC mills, but can be routers, welders, benders, wire EDM machines, etc.

Before CNC: Numerical Control

  • Early systems used punched cards/tape.
  • Machines followed pre-set paths mechanically.
  • No digital computers — limited flexibility.
  • Multiple spindles

A tracer mill follows a physical template with a stylus, and the cutting spindles copy that motion. With several spindles, it can cut several copies of the same part at once. Changing the part means making a new template, which is the flexibility CNC later solved: changing a part became editing a program.

NC machines appeared in the 1950s. Adding a computer to the controller (CNC) became common in the 1970s.

Manual Machining
  • Fast(er) setup
  • Easy(er) changes
  • Great for prototypes
Manual: human guides each move
CNC Machining
  • Automated repeatability
  • Ideal(er) for production
  • Precise (or at least as precise as the machine)
CNC: program repeats same path
Part 1 · Part 2 · Part 3
Key tradeoff
Flexibility vs repeatability. One part tomorrow is often faster on a manual machine. Twenty identical parts favor CNC, because programming time is paid once.

  • Machine language for CNC.
  • Commands include: Motion (X, Y, Z), Speed & Tool Changes.
  • Shared across printers, mills, and cutters.
  • CAM (MasterCAM, Fusion 360, Slicers) writes it for you.

What Is 3D Printing

  • Additive process: builds objects layer by layer.
  • Turns digital models into real parts.
  • Expiration of patents mid 2000s to 2010s (2009).
MakerBot & the Open-Source Revolution
  • Founded in 2009 by former RepRap community members.
  • Early printers like the Cupcake CNC and Thing-O-Matic were open-source kits.
  • Helped popularize FDM/FFF printing for hobbyists.
  • Later moved toward closed-source designs with the Replicator series.
Formlabs & Desktop Resin Printing
  • Founded in 2011 from MIT Media Lab.
  • Form 1 introduced affordable SLA resin printing to consumers.
  • Brought high-resolution printing from industrial labs to desktops.

Printing Technologies

Common 3D printing technologies

  • FDM/FFF: Fused Deposition Modeling™ / Fused Filament Fabrication. (FDM is Stratasys's trademark, which is why the open community says FFF.)
  • SLA/MSLA: Stereolithography or Masked Stereolithography (curing resin by UV)
  • SLS: Selective Laser Sintering (Powder Printing)
  • MJF: HP Multi Jet Fusion (powder + inkjet agents, fused by heat)
  • Binder Jet: powder + inkjet-printed liquid binder (glue)
  • Emerging: Volumetric & Gel Printing

MJF vs Binder Jet

Both spread a thin layer of powder and use inkjet heads, like a paper printer, to mark each layer. What the ink does is different.

MJF · HP Multi Jet Fusion
Inkjet heads print a fusing agent where the part goes, so that powder absorbs the heat from an infrared lamp and melts together. A detailing agent printed along the edges keeps the neighboring powder from fusing, for crisp edges. Usually nylon (PA12).
Binder Jet
Inkjet heads print a liquid binder that glues powder together. No heat while printing, so parts come out fragile ("green"). Metal parts are then sintered in a furnace, where they shrink. Also used for sand molds and full-color models.

Advantages & disadvantages

Supports in FDM

  • Prevent overhangs and sagging.
  • Avoid supports: design ≤45° angles.
Supports hold up steep overhangs
Aim for ≤45° when possible
At 45° each new layer still sits about half on the layer below. Steeper than that, the plastic hangs in the air and droops. Chamfers instead of flat ceilings, and short bridges between two posts, often avoid supports entirely.

Types

Breakaway
Same material as the part, snapped off by hand. Leaves marks where it touched.
Dissolvable
PVA, BVOH, PETG/PLA for each other. PVA and BVOH dissolve in water. PETG and PLA barely stick to each other, so each one peels cleanly off the other.
Interface layers
Denser layers at the top of the support, so the underside of the part comes out smoother.
09

Infill

What is infill?

  • The internal structure of a 3D print between outer walls.
  • Balances strength, weight, and print time.

General tips

  • Higher infill % = stronger but heavier. (with exceptions)
  • 15–25% is typical for general use.
  • Solid (100%) infill is uncommon on consumer 3D printers due to inconsistent extrusion.
Why 100% infill goes wrong
With a solid inside, every small over-extrusion has nowhere to go. The extra plastic piles up into blobs, the nozzle scrapes the print, and top surfaces come out rough or bulging. Time and filament also climb fast, while the strength gain is small, because the walls and top and bottom layers carry most of the load.

Infill tradeoffs

Infill changes weight, print time, and internal support — but walls and orientation often matter more.

Teaching cue
Ask students when "more infill" is actually the wrong answer. Good answers: adding walls (perimeters) beats adding infill for bending strength and screw holes; extra infill on a robot or drone part is just extra mass; flexible TPU parts get stiffer as infill goes up.

Common patterns

Grid / Rectilinear
Fast, simple default. Fine for most parts.
Gyroid
Wavy 3D pattern, similar strength in every direction, prints smoothly.
Triangles
Stiff in the layer plane. Good for flat panels.
Honeycomb
Stiff and good-looking, but slower because of all the direction changes.

Orientation & Layer Strength

Print orientation

  • Affects surface finish, strength, and supports.
  • Choose orientation based on function and appearance.

Layer direction & strength

FDM prints behave like laminated material: the part is usually weaker between layers than within a layer.

Example: a hook printed standing up can snap along a layer line. The same hook printed lying flat carries the load along its layers and holds much more.

Orient for load path
Run layers along the direction the part gets pulled or bent.
Minimize supports
Put large flat faces on the bed and keep overhangs under 45°.
Hide layer lines on show faces
Faces that point up or sideways look different. Put the face people see where it prints cleanest.
Reduce print time
Shorter parts in Z usually print faster, since fewer layers means fewer layer changes.
Design decision
Function first, appearance second.

Motion Systems & Printers

Motion systems in FDM

Cartesian
Simple, reliable, Z wobble.
CoreXY
Fast, rigid.
Delta
Tall prints, complex.

Common printers

Cartesian / Bedslinger printers

  • Examples: Prusa MK3, Ender 3
  • Build plate (bed) moves in Y-axis while the toolhead moves in X and Z.
  • Simple, affordable, and easy to maintain.
  • The Prusa lineage traces back to the RepRap Project — an open-source movement to create self-replicating 3D printers.

CoreXY printers

  • Examples: Bambu P1S, Voron 2.4
  • The bed moves only in Z, while two belts control X and Y together.
  • Allows faster, more rigid motion with less vibration.
  • Popular for speed, accuracy, and compact frames.
Ender 3
// Cartesian · bedslinger
Low-cost workhorse. Easy to repair and modify.
Prusa MK3
// Cartesian · bedslinger
RepRap descendant, known for reliability and open designs.
Bambu P1S
// CoreXY · enclosed
Fast out of the box, enclosure helps with ABS and ASA.
Voron 2.4
// CoreXY · open-source kit
Built from a kit by the owner. Very fast and rigid.
Acceleration ≠ print speed
High acceleration helps with cornering and quick direction changes, but print speed also depends on flow rate, cooling, and stability. Faster movement isn't always faster printing — balance is key.

CoreXY kinematics

This design allows both motors to remain stationary on the frame, reducing moving mass and improving acceleration response.

Because no motor moves with the toolhead, inertia is greatly reduced — allowing faster, smoother, and more precise printing.

SLA vs MSLA

SLA
Laser traces each layer. Slower, excellent quality.
MSLA
LCD projects whole layer. Faster, depends on screen resolution.
Full plate, same time
An MSLA printer exposes a whole layer at once, so print time depends on the number of layers, not how much is on the plate. Ten small parts take about as long as one. On SLA, the laser has to trace every part, so more parts means more time.

Introduction to Laser Cutters

  • Use focused light to cut or engrave materials.
  • Motion is CNC-controlled, like 3D printers.

A laser cutter is a two-axis CNC machine whose tool is a beam of light. The head moves in X and Y, the laser switches on and off along the path, and Z only sets the focus height. Everything from section 05 about toolpaths still applies.

The Electromagnetic Spectrum

Radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays are all the same thing: electromagnetic waves. The only difference is wavelength. Long waves (radio) carry little energy per photon; short waves (UV, X-ray) carry a lot.

Zoom in: where the lasers and resin printers sit

Log scale: each step to the right is a longer wavelength. The visible band is a sliver even at this zoom.

BandWavelengthEveryday examples
Radioover 1 mAM and FM radio (FM is about 3 m), TV broadcast
Microwave1 mm – 1 mMicrowave ovens (about 12 cm), Wi-Fi, radar
Infrared700 nm – 1 mmTV remotes (about 940 nm), heat, fiber and CO₂ lasers
Visible380 – 700 nmLight bulbs, screen pixels (red, green, blue), diode lasers (blue)
Ultraviolet10 – 380 nmSunburn, black lights. Resin printers use 405 nm, at the violet edge of visible, usually called UV
X-ray0.01 – 10 nmMedical and security imaging
Gammaunder 0.01 nmRadioactive decay
Why wavelength picks the material
Each material absorbs some wavelengths and reflects or passes others. CO₂ light (10.6 µm) soaks into wood, acrylic, and leather but bounces off bare metal. Fiber light (1064 nm) is absorbed by metal but passes through clear acrylic. Blue diode light is absorbed by dark materials and passes through clear or blue ones.
Safety glasses are wavelength-specific
Laser glasses are rated for certain wavelengths. Glasses made for a blue diode do not protect against a fiber or CO₂ laser, and the reverse. Check the rating printed on the lens.

Different lasers interact with different materials. Section 15 shows why.

How laser cutters work

  • Focused beam vaporizes or burns material.
  • Controlled by mirrors, lenses, and stepper motors.
  • Engraving = shallow burn, Cutting = full penetration.
Beam path in a CO₂ laser
Focus sets the cut
The lens brings the beam to its smallest spot at the focal distance. Set the material surface at that distance for the narrowest cut (kerf, often around 0.1–0.2 mm on a CO₂). Out of focus, the spot spreads out, so cuts get wider and may not go through.

Diode and fiber machines usually put the laser source right in the head or deliver it through a fiber cable, so they skip the mirror chain.

Cut, Engrave & Machine Needs

What they can cut

  • CO₂: Wood, acrylic, leather, cardboard.
  • Fiber: Metals.
  • Diode: Thin dark materials, paper, leather.

WoodAcrylicLeatherCardboardMetal*

*with fiber laser or marking coating

What they can engrave

Wood, acrylic, anodized aluminum, coated metals, leather, glass (with coatings).

Engraving changes the surface. Engrave = surface mark, not full cut.

CO₂ lasers frost bare glass directly. Diode and fiber lasers need a coating or marking paste to mark glass.

What laser cutters need

Exhaust
Ventilation/exhaust system. Pulls smoke and fumes out of the machine and the room.
Air assist
Air assist for clean cuts. Blows smoke and flame away from the cut, so there is less charring and fewer flare-ups.
Clean optics
Optics cleaning and focus alignment. A dirty lens absorbs power, heats up, and can crack.
Focus + settings
Proper power settings and calibration. Run a small test grid on new material before the real job.

Toxic Materials & Safety

Toxic or unsafe materials
Never cut PVC, vinyl, ABS, polycarbonate, fiberglass, or unknown plastics. Polycarbonate melts instead of vaporizing. They release toxic gases or damage optics.
MaterialWhat goes wrong
PVC / vinylReleases chlorine compounds (hydrochloric acid in the air). Harmful to breathe and corrodes the machine's metal parts and optics.
ABSMelts into gooey edges and gives off toxic fumes, including hydrogen cyanide.

When in doubt
Do not cut until identified. Polycarbonate (Lexan) is common in robotics, so it shows up in scrap bins. Cut it with a router, saw, or shear instead.

Laser Cut Lines & Color

Defining laser cut lines

  • Set line width to hairline for laser cutting.
  • When you print from CorelDRAW or Illustrator to our Universal Laser Systems driver (and Epilog's), a hairline stroke tells the laser it's a vector cut, not an engraving.
  • Use color to define operations. Different colors = different power/speed passes.
Hairline + color = laser operation

Red for cut and blue for score is a common shop convention, not a rule. Your shop's color chart is what counts.

Hairline depends on the software

Understanding color spaces

  • RGB color space: Red, Green, Blue (light-based).
  • For full red: R = 255, G = 0, B = 0 → Hex: #FF0000
  • Hexadecimal system: base-16 counting (0–9, A–F).
  • #FF0000 = 255 red intensity, 0 green, 0 blue.
  • 8-bit color depth: each RGB channel uses 8 bits (0–255 values).
  • 256³ = 16,777,216 possible colors.
  • RGB vs CMYK: RGB = additive (used for screens, light). CMYK = subtractive (used for print).
CMYK files break color mapping
An Illustrator document set to CMYK can turn pure red into something like #ED1C24 when it prints, which no longer matches the laser's red setting. Set laser files to RGB color mode. Sections 20 and 21 cover what these hex numbers mean.

Binary, Decimal & Hex

Hex codes like #FF0000 are numbers written in base 16. To read them, start with how counting works in any base.

The rollover rule
When a position goes past its largest digit, it resets to 0 and bumps the position on its left by one. It's the same rule as a car odometer and the printed binary counter. Only the largest digit changes between bases, so in every base, "10" means the base itself.
1 + 1 = 10

Hex · base 16

Each position is worth the base times the position to its right. In binary that's 1, 2, 4, 8 … 128. In hex it's 1, 16, 256. One binary digit is a bit; eight bits make a byte, which holds 0 to 255. Four bits make exactly one hex digit, so a byte is always two hex digits.

Color Is Three Bytes

A screen color stores red, green, and blue as one byte each. A hex code is those three bytes written as six hex digits: #FF0000 is FF red, 00 green, 00 blue, the same as rgb(255, 0, 0).

// Color mixer · drag the picker, slide a channel, or click a wheel
24-bit color · 8 bits per channel
Hex code
RGB
All 24 bits
Presets
Send odometer value 0 to

Why 8 bits per channel

One channel is one byte
8 bits give 28 = 256 levels, numbered 0 to 255. The same byte the odometer rolls through.
One byte is two hex digits
16 × 16 = 256, so FF is 255. Six hex digits hold all three channels.
Three channels, 24 bits
256 × 256 × 256 = 16,777,216 colors.
Why stop at 8?
Computers store data in bytes, and 256 steps is fine enough that neighboring shades are hard to tell apart. Smooth gradients can still show bands, which is why HDR video uses 10 bits (1,024 levels) per channel.
Light adds up
All three at 255 is white, all at 0 is black. Red plus green light makes yellow.
Naming trap
"24-bit color" and "8 bits per channel" mean the same thing. Old "8-bit color" used one byte for the whole color: only 256 colors total.

Laser check: one bit off

#FF0000rgb(255, 0, 0)red byte 11111111
#FE0000rgb(254, 0, 0)red byte 11111110

These differ by one bit and look identical on screen. Software that assigns settings by exact color, like a print driver's color mapping, treats #FE0000 as a different color, so that line can be skipped or cut with the wrong power. Some programs snap to the nearest color instead, so check what yours does.
