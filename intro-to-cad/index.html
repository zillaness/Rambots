01

What is CAD?

CAD stands for Computer-Aided Design. It's the use of software to create precise, mathematically defined models of physical objects. Before CAD, engineers worked on paper — hand-drawing blueprints, calculating dimensions manually, and building physical prototypes for every change. CAD replaced that process with digital models that can be measured, simulated, modified, and shared instantly.

A CAD model isn't just a picture of something — it's a precise description of an object's geometry. Every edge has an exact length. Every surface has an exact curvature. Every part has exact mass properties based on its material. This precision is what makes CAD useful for manufacturing: send a CAD file to a CNC machine, a 3D printer, or a laser cutter and get a physical part back that matches your design exactly.

Why it matters for FRC

In FIRST Robotics, CAD lets your team design the entire robot before cutting a single piece of metal. Check that parts fit together, run stress simulations, generate cut lists, and hand off manufacturing-ready drawings to sponsors — all from a laptop.

What CAD gives you

Precision. Dimensions are exact. A hole is 0.250" in diameter, not "about a quarter inch."

Iteration speed. Changing one dimension updates the whole model. Change a tube length and every dependent part adjusts automatically.

Simulation. Run stress analysis, motion simulation, fluid dynamics, and thermal analysis before building anything physical.

Collaboration. Multiple team members can work on different subsystems simultaneously and assemble them digitally to check for collisions.

Manufacturing output. G-code for CNC machines, DXF for laser cutters, STL for 3D printers, and formal engineering drawings for machinists.

02

Parametric Modeling

Parametric modeling is the dominant approach to mechanical CAD. Instead of defining just the shape of an object, you define the rules and relationships that create that shape — driven by named values called parameters like dimensions, angles, and distances. A plate with holes always 10mm from each edge: widen the plate and the holes follow automatically.

Feature history: tree vs. timeline

Parametric modelers record every operation — sketch, extrude, fillet, hole — as a step you can revisit. How that history is presented differs between tools.

Feature Tree (Onshape, SOLIDWORKS, Creo) — operations appear as a vertical list on the side panel. Every feature is a node. Click any node to go back and edit it; the model rebuilds from that point forward. The tree is always visible and always sequential. In Onshape, this is the left panel of a Part Studio.

Timeline (Fusion 360 default) — operations appear as a horizontal strip along the bottom of the screen, ordered left to right chronologically. You can drag features to reorder them, and scrub backward in time by dragging the marker. Fusion 360 also offers a Model History view that resembles a traditional feature tree — you can enable this in preferences. The timeline is particularly useful for complex assemblies where you want to see the chronological sequence of a design, but the tree format is more familiar to engineers coming from SOLIDWORKS or Creo.

Onshape — Part Studios

Onshape organizes work into Part Studios and Assemblies. A Part Studio is where you model parts — multiple parts can live in one Part Studio and reference each other's geometry directly. This is more flexible than SOLIDWORKS's one-part-per-file convention. An Assembly is where you bring parts together with mates. A single Onshape Document can contain multiple Part Studios, Assemblies, and Drawings, all version-controlled together.

Sketches and constraints

Most parametric modeling starts with a 2D sketch — a flat drawing of lines, arcs, and curves on a plane. That sketch is constrained: geometric rules ("this line is horizontal," "these arcs are tangent") and dimensional rules ("this length is 45mm") lock the geometry. When a sketch is fully constrained, every point is locked and can only change when you explicitly edit a constraint or dimension.

03

Constraints

Constraints are the rules that define relationships in a sketch or assembly. Without them, geometry floats freely. With them, your design has intent — the sketch knows that two lines should always be parallel, that a hole should always sit at the center of a face, that a bolt pattern should always stay equidistant from an edge. When you change one dimension, constraints ensure everything adjusts in a way that makes engineering sense.

Constraints come in three families: geometric (shape relationships), dimensional (exact numeric values), and assembly mates (how 3D parts connect to each other).

Geometric constraint symbols

Every CAD tool represents geometric constraints with small icons on sketch entities. Learn to read these symbols — they tell you at a glance exactly why your geometry is positioned the way it is.

Best practices

Apply geometric constraints before dimensional ones. Define the shape first (horizontal, tangent, perpendicular), then lock the sizes.

Avoid over-constraining. Applying a Perpendicular constraint AND a 90° angular dimension to the same two lines is redundant — the solver will flag it as an error.

Constrain to model geometry, not to space. Wherever possible, constrain sketch elements to existing edges or faces rather than fixed coordinates, so if the part changes your sketch follows it.

04

Variables & Equations

Dimensional constraints lock sketch geometry to exact numbers. But what happens when you want a hundred holes all at the same diameter, or when you need the wall thickness of a bracket to drive the size of a corresponding slot? Variables (also called parameters or global variables) let you define a named value once and reference it everywhere, so changing one number updates everything that depends on it simultaneously.

This is what separates a model with good design intent from a frozen snapshot. A model driven by well-named variables adapts to changes predictably and deliberately.

// Onshape-style variable definitions
tubeWidth = 2 in // 2x1 aluminum tube width
wallThickness = 0.125 in // 1/8" wall
mountHoleDia = 0.201 in // #10 clearance hole

// Variables can reference other variables with equations
innerWidth = tubeWidth - (2 * wallThickness) // 1.75 in
boltCircleR = mountHoleSpacing * 2

Now every mounting hole in the Part Studio uses mountHoleDia. Switch from a #10 to a #8 screw by changing one variable — every hole updates instantly. In Onshape, variables are defined with the Variables feature in a Part Studio. In Fusion 360, this is the Parameters manager. In SOLIDWORKS, it's the Equations dialog.

FRC example

Define frameWidth = 28 in in a master variables Part Studio. Drive your bellypan sketch, bumper profile, and drivetrain layout from that same variable. When the team decides to change the frame to 26" wide, change one number — the entire model updates rather than hunting through 12 sketches for every hardcoded "28".

Naming Conventions

CAD tools let you name almost everything: sketches, features, variables, parts, assemblies. Most beginners leave everything as Sketch 1, Extrude 2, d3. This works for a 5-feature part you built in 20 minutes. It's a disaster for a complex robot subsystem with 80 features built by four people over six weeks.

camelCase vs. snake_case

camelCase
tubeWallThickness
First word lowercase, each subsequent word capitalized. Common in Onshape FeatureScript.
snake_case
tube_wall_thickness
All lowercase, words separated by underscores. Common in Python, easy to read.
PascalCase
TubeWallThickness
Every word capitalized. Common for class names in programming.
kebab-case
tube-wall-thickness
Avoid in CAD — solvers may interpret hyphens as minus signs.

For Onshape and FRC work, camelCase is the most common convention — it matches FeatureScript's own style. The most important thing is consistency within a project.

Sketch Planes & Construction Geometry

Every 2D sketch lives on a plane. Before drawing a single line, you need a flat reference surface. The plane defines the orientation of your sketch in 3D space and which direction extrusions, revolves, and cuts will travel.

Default origin planes

Every Onshape Part Studio starts with three mutually perpendicular origin planes, each corresponding to a standard engineering view:

Front Plane
XY · Z = 0
The vertical plane you see face-on in the Front view. Use this as your primary profile plane for parts that are primarily viewed from the front — arms, plates, gussets.
Top Plane
XZ · Y = 0
The horizontal ground plane you see in the Top view. Good for footprint sketches of frames, bellypans, and anything with a horizontal primary cross-section.
Right Plane
YZ · X = 0
The vertical side plane you see in the Right view. Good for side-profile sketches — wheel arches, chassis side rails, cam profile shapes.

Creating custom planes

Real parts rarely sketch cleanly onto the three origin planes. Use Plane features to define new reference planes anywhere in space.

Custom Plane Types
Offset Plane
A plane parallel to an existing face or plane, moved a specified distance. Most common. Use for building stepped geometry or sketching on a face that isn't at the origin.
Inputs: face or plane + offset distance
Through 3 Points
Defined by three non-collinear points in space. Use when you need a plane that passes through specific vertices or sketch points on different faces.
Inputs: 3 vertices/sketch points
Angle to Plane
Rotated relative to an existing plane about a specified edge or axis. Use for angled cuts, tapered surfaces, or chamfer sketches.
Inputs: reference plane + axis/edge + angle
Midplane
A plane equidistant between two parallel faces. Excellent for driving symmetric geometry — define both sides as equal distances from a center plane rather than absolute coordinates.
Inputs: 2 parallel faces or planes
Through Line + Point
Defined by an axis (line or edge) and an additional point. Useful for angled ribs, tabs, or features that need to pass through a specific edge.
Inputs: 1 edge/axis + 1 point
Tangent to Surface
Tangent to a curved surface at a specified point. Used for sketching on cylinders or curved faces — placing a flat feature that lines up exactly with a surface normal.
Inputs: curved face + point

Sketching on existing faces — the most common workflow in FRC is skipping explicit planes entirely and just clicking a flat face of an existing solid to start a sketch there. Onshape places the sketch on that face automatically. This is fast for additive features (boss, pad, hole) that build on existing geometry.

Construction geometry

Inside a sketch, individual entities can be toggled to construction geometry — they display as dashed lines and do not form the closed profile that gets extruded or revolved. They still participate in constraints and can be dimensioned.

In Onshape, toggle the selected entity with Q. Common uses:

Centerlines for revolve. The axis of a revolve must be a construction line in the sketch. Draw a vertical line at x=0, toggle it to construction, and Onshape will recognize it as the revolve axis.

Reference geometry. Draw a construction circle to establish a bolt-circle radius, then place coincident point-constraints on real geometry at specific angles around it — cleaner than constraining each hole location independently.

Driven dimensions. A construction line between two points with a driven dimension shows you a measurement without constraining the geometry — useful for checking clearances while modeling.

FRC workflow tip

For tube-based FRC robots, start with a Top-plane sketch of your frame footprint. Every tube runs as a line constrained to exact position and length. This sketch becomes the master reference from which all tube length dimensions are driven — change the frame footprint and all tube lengths update automatically.

Geometric Primitives

A geometric primitive is a fundamental 3D shape — one of the basic building blocks from which more complex geometry is assembled. Classical solid geometry identifies a small set: box, cylinder, sphere, cone, torus (donut), and wedge/pyramid. Everything you design can be understood as combinations and subtractions of these forms.

In most parametric solid modelers, you don't insert primitives directly. Instead, you create them naturally from sketch+feature workflows. Understanding the sketch-to-primitive mapping demystifies many operations:

Box / Cuboid
Rectangle → Extrude (Blind)
Cylinder
Circle → Extrude (Blind)
Sphere
Semicircle → Revolve 360°
Cone
Right triangle → Revolve 360°
Torus
Circle (offset from axis) → Revolve 360°
Pyramid / Wedge
Triangle/polygon → Extrude with taper or Loft to point

Tools like TinkerCAD make the primitive-boolean model completely explicit: you drag in primitives, position them, and combine or subtract them to make parts. It's a valid introduction to 3D thinking, and it's exactly how many FRC students first learn spatial reasoning before moving to parametric CAD.

In OpenSCAD, primitives are code: cube([20,10,5]), cylinder(h=30, r=8), sphere(r=15). Everything else follows from boolean operations on these.

Recognizing primitives in mechanical parts

Develop the habit of decomposing any part you see into its constituent primitives. A motor bracket is a box with cylinders subtracted (holes) and a partial cylinder added (boss). An intake roller is a cylinder. A sprocket is a cylinder with many cylinders subtracted around its perimeter. This decomposition skill makes modeling new parts much faster.

08

Boolean Operations

Boolean operations combine or subtract solid bodies using set theory: union (add), difference (subtract), and intersection (overlap). These three operations — combined with primitives — are theoretically sufficient to model any manufacturable solid. In practice, they're embedded in nearly every CAD feature you use daily.

Union
Add · Join · Merge
Both bodies become one. All volume from both shapes is kept. Internal faces where they overlapped are removed. One solid result.
Difference
Subtract · Remove · Cut
Body B is removed from body A. A hole or cavity the shape of B is left behind in A. B is consumed. Used for every hole, pocket, and slot in mechanical design.
Intersection
Intersect · Keep Overlap
Only the volume shared by both bodies is kept. Everything outside the overlap is removed. Useful for creating complex clip shapes and trim operations.

Booleans in parametric CAD — New vs. Add vs. Remove vs. Intersect

In Onshape, boolean logic is embedded directly in feature operations. When you create an Extrude, you choose a merge scope — the bodies the extrusion will interact with — and an operation type:

New. Creates a completely independent body in the Part Studio. No interaction with existing bodies. Use this when designing a second part that will eventually be a separate component — or when you want to Boolean them explicitly later.

Add. Union with selected existing bodies. The extruded volume merges into the target bodies. Any face where the new volume intersects the existing body is dissolved — one continuous solid.

Remove. Difference from selected existing bodies. The extrusion's volume is cut away from targets. Creates holes, pockets, slots, and any subtracted geometry. A sketch on the face of a block with Remove = a pocket.

Intersect. Keeps only the volume common to both the extrusion and the target bodies. Everything outside the overlap is removed from both. Rarely used but powerful for mask operations.

Multi-body Part Studios and merge scope

In Onshape, a Part Studio can contain multiple independent bodies simultaneously. When you extrude with Add or Remove, a merge scope selector appears — you pick which bodies are affected. Bodies not in the scope are untouched. This lets you operate on specific parts in a multi-body setup without accidentally modifying others.

Explicit Boolean feature

Beyond embedding booleans in Extrude/Revolve/etc., Onshape also has a dedicated Boolean feature in the feature toolbar. This lets you perform union, subtract, or intersect operations on bodies you've already created in earlier features — useful when you want to delay the boolean to a specific step in the feature history.

Sketch Tools

A 2D sketch is built from a toolkit of geometry creation and manipulation tools. Mastering these tools — knowing exactly when to use each one and what makes them fully defined — is the core skill of parametric CAD. The tools listed here are Onshape-centric but are nearly identical in every parametric modeler.

Line tools

Lines & Curves
Line L
The fundamental sketch entity. Click-click mode chains lines continuously. Click-drag mode creates a single segment. Hovering the end of a line shows a tangent arc option. Lines infer horizontal/vertical and snap to existing geometry.
Fully defined by: 2 points, or 1 point + length + angle
Spline S
A smooth curve through or near control points. Onshape offers through-point spline (curve passes through each clicked point — more intuitive) and control-point spline (points attract the curve without touching — more controllable). Tangent handles at endpoints control how the spline enters and exits.
Fully defined by: all point positions + endpoint tangent directions
Construction Line
Any sketch entity toggled to construction mode (Q) becomes dashed and invisible to the solid body. Essential for revolve axes, reference geometry, and helping the constraint solver without adding profile complexity.
Toggle: select entity + Q

Rectangles and polygons

Rectangles & Polygons
Corner Rectangle R
Click two diagonally opposite corners. Creates four lines forming a closed rectangle. The two clicked points are the defining corners — constrain their positions to fully define the rectangle. Width and height are implicit from corner positions.
Fully defined by: 2 corner positions (or 1 corner + width + height)
Center Rectangle
Click the center point, then a corner. Automatically creates symmetric constraints so the rectangle stays centered. Much better than a corner rectangle when symmetry about an origin or centerline is intended — fewer constraints needed.
Fully defined by: center position + half-width + half-height
Inscribed Polygon
All vertices lie on a defined circle. The circle is the circumradius — corners touch the reference circle. Use when vertex-to-vertex distance across flats is what matters — e.g., hex bolt heads defined by their widest dimension (corner to corner).
Defined by: center + circumradius + rotation + number of sides
Circumscribed Polygon
The midpoints of all sides lie on a defined circle. The circle is the inradius — flat faces are tangent to the reference circle. Use when wrench size matters — hex head where the flat-to-flat distance is specified. Same hex bolt is different by this vs inscribed.
Defined by: center + inradius + rotation + number of sides
Inscribed vs. Circumscribed
Inscribed: vertices on circle (corners touch) · Circumscribed: sides tangent to circle (flats touch)

Circles and arcs

Circles & Arcs
Circle (Center) C
Click center, drag radius. The most common circle method. Center + radius fully defines it — apply coincident to center and a dimension to radius, or apply concentric + diameter.
Fully defined by: center position + radius/diameter
Circle (3-Point)
Click three points the circle must pass through. Useful when you know three points on the circumference but don't yet know the center — for example, fitting a circle to existing geometry or creating a clearance circle through three fixed points.
Fully defined by: 3 points on circumference
Arc (3-Point) A
Click start, end, then a through-point that lies on the arc. Intuitive — you define where the arc starts, ends, and how far it bulges. The midpoint controls the radius and arc extent simultaneously.
Fully defined by: 2 endpoints + 1 on-arc point
Arc (Center)
Click center, then click start and end points. The radius is determined by the center-to-start distance, and the arc sweeps from start to end. Useful when you know the center and want precise control over where the arc begins and ends.
Fully defined by: center + start point + end angle
Arc (Tangent)
Begins tangent to the last line or arc segment at its endpoint — no corner formed. In Onshape, hover the endpoint of an existing line during the Line tool and drag to spawn a tangent arc automatically. Creates G1 continuity at the junction.
Fully defined by: start point/tangent + end point or radius

Slots

A slot is an enclosed profile defined by its center-to-center length and width — two parallel lines capped by semicircles. Onshape provides four slot variants, each defined differently:

Slot Types
Straight Slot
Two endpoint-clicks define the centerline. The slot width is then specified. The slot runs straight between the two center points with semicircular end caps. The most common type — for sliding bolts, keyways, elongated holes.
Defined by: 2 center endpoints + width
Center-Point Straight Slot
Click the slot's overall center point, then drag to set length direction and length, then set width. Useful when you need the slot centered on an origin or constraint point rather than specifying both ends.
Defined by: overall center + length + angle + width
Center-Point Arc Slot
Click the arc center, click start and end of the arc centerline, then set width. The slot curves — both rails are concentric arcs and the end caps are radial. Common for curved channels where a bolt must slide through an arc of travel.
Defined by: arc center + start/end angles + radius + width
3-Point Arc Slot
Click two endpoints of the arc centerline and a through-point to set the arc's curvature, then specify width. More intuitive than center-point arc slot when you know the arc path but not the center.
Defined by: 2 endpoints + 1 arc through-point + width

Sketch manipulation tools

Manipulation & Reference
Mirror
Select entities to mirror, then select a mirror line. Applies symmetric constraints automatically — move one side and the mirror updates. Far better than manually placing symmetric geometry — use a construction centerline as the mirror line.
Inputs: entities + mirror line (can be construction)
Sketch Fillet F
Creates a tangent arc between two intersecting lines — rounds a corner within the sketch. The fillet is a proper arc entity constrained to be tangent to both lines. Different from a 3D fillet feature — this one lives in the sketch and affects the 2D profile shape.
Inputs: click corner intersection + radius dimension
Trim T
Click a segment to remove the portion inside an intersection. Cleans up overlapping geometry after extending or offsetting. Think of it as erasing from one intersection to the next. Essential for turning crossed rectangles into clean L or T profiles.
Mode: click to trim individual segment, or drag to trim all crossed
Extend
Extends a line or arc to meet the nearest intersecting entity. Click the entity near the end you want to extend. Useful for closing gaps after boolean-sketching or when building from independently placed lines.
Inputs: click near the end to extend
Offset O
Creates a new set of entities at a uniform distance from selected entities. Offset a rectangle inward to make a wall, offset a spline outward for a clearance path, offset a closed loop to create a pocket profile.
Inputs: entities + offset distance + direction
Sketch Pattern
Linear or circular repetition of sketch entities. Linear: rows and columns with spacing. Circular: count and angle around a center point. Creates separate entity copies (not linked like features patterns) — each copy can be individually constrained.
Types: Linear (X/Y count + spacing) · Circular (count + full or partial)
Use / Project
Projects edges of existing 3D bodies onto the active sketch plane. Creates reference lines you can constrain to — no need to manually redraw the existing geometry. In Onshape, select edges and "Use" them as sketch entities. Critical for in-context modeling where sketches build on previous features.
Result: linked construction or real entities from 3D edges
Split X
Splits a curve into two separate entities at a selected point. Use when you need to apply different constraints or properties to parts of what was one continuous entity — for example, splitting a circle to create an arc.
Inputs: entity + split point
Text
Places text as sketch geometry — fully constrainable profile curves. Used for laser-engraved labels, embossed or debossed letters, and decorative geometry. The text profile can be extruded, cut, or swept like any other sketch. Set font, size, and position.
Inputs: text string + font + size + position
Tips for efficient sketching

Start every sketch by placing the most critical anchor point (usually at the origin, a known face, or a reference vertex) with a coincident constraint first. Apply geometric constraints before dimensional ones. Trim aggressively to keep profiles clean and unambiguous. If the solver turns your geometry red, undo and look for which constraint is conflicting rather than adding more constraints hoping something works.

3D Feature Operations

3D features are the operations that turn 2D sketches (or existing 3D faces) into solid geometry. Each feature appears as a node in the feature tree. Understanding the full toolkit — what inputs each feature needs, what options it exposes, and when to reach for one over another — is the core of parametric mechanical modeling.

Profile-based operations — turning sketches into solids

Profile → Solid
Extrude
Pushes a closed sketch profile along a straight line perpendicular to the sketch plane. The workhorse of mechanical CAD — the majority of manufactured parts start here.
Direction: Blind · Symmetric · Up to vertex/face · Up to next · Through all
Operation: New body · Add · Remove · Intersect
Options: Draft angle · Second end condition · Merge scope (which bodies to affect)
Note: Multiple separate closed regions in one sketch can be extruded simultaneously — each becomes a separate boss or cut.
Revolve
Rotates a sketch profile around a specified axis. Creates rotationally symmetric parts — shafts, flanges, pulleys, bowls, wheels. The axis must be a construction line or an existing model edge. The profile must be on one side of the axis (no crossing).
Angle: Full 360° · One direction · Symmetric · Up to entity
Operation: New · Add · Remove · Intersect
Axis: Construction line in sketch or existing edge
Loft
Blends a solid between two or more profile sketches on different planes. The shape transitions smoothly from one profile to the next. Optional guide curves control how the surface flows between profiles — without guides the loft takes the "shortest path."
Profiles: 2 or more sketches on parallel or angled planes
Guide curves: Splines that profiles must pass through
Start/end conditions: Tangent or curvature matching to adjacent faces
Sweep
Moves a profile sketch along a 3D path curve. The cross-section shape stays constant (or can be twisted) as it travels. Useful for creating curved tubes, handrails, cable guides, and any part with a constant cross-section along a complex path.
Profile: any closed sketch
Path: any 3D sketch curve or chain of edges
Orientation: Keep normal constant · Follow path (auto-rotate) · Follow path and guide curve
Pipe
Sweeps a circular cross-section along a path — a simplified Sweep where you specify radius and wall thickness rather than drawing a cross-section sketch. Faster for round tubes and conduits. Hollow or solid, with configurable outer diameter and optional wall thickness.
Path: sketch path or model edges
Cross-section: outer radius + optional inner radius (hollow)
Operation: New · Add · Remove
Offset Surface / Thicken
Offsets a surface outward to give it thickness, converting a surface model to a solid. Also used to create thin-walled parts from a reference surface — import a surface and thicken it to get a solid panel or shell.
Inputs: surface + thickness value
Direction: one side or symmetric

Subtractive and hollow operations

Subtractive
Hole
Dedicated hole feature beyond a simple Remove extrude. Specifies standard hole types with engineering-correct geometry: simple drill, threaded (tap), counterbore, and countersink. Inputs use standard hardware sizes (M5, ¼-20, #10-32) and the tool computes the correct geometry automatically.
Types: Simple · Counterbore (CBore) · Countersink (CSink) · Threaded
Depth: Blind · Through · Up to face
Standard: ISO metric or inch/fractional hardware sizes
Shell
Hollows out a solid body to a uniform wall thickness, removing selected faces to create openings. The classic example: a solid box becomes a hollow box with open top. Shell first, then add features — shelves, ribs, bosses — to the hollow.
Wall thickness: single value
Faces to remove: open faces left as holes
Note: Shell before adding internal geometry — shelling a complex body often fails
Draft
Adds a taper angle to a face relative to a pull direction. Required for injection-molded and die-cast parts so they release from the mold. Even 1–3° of draft on tall sidewalls is sufficient. In FRC, relevant when designing custom-molded parts or checking that 3D-printed parts will release from molds.
Draft angle: 1–3° typical for plastics
Neutral plane: where the face pivots from
Pull direction: along mold separation axis

Edge operations

Edge Operations
Fillet F
Rounds one or more edges with a specified radius, creating a smooth tangent transition between adjacent faces. Reduces stress concentrations at internal corners (fillets are stronger than sharp corners), removes sharp edges for safety, and improves flow around parts. Applied as one of the last features — fillets interact with each other and downstream features.
Types: Constant radius · Variable radius (specify per-vertex) · G2 curvature-continuous · Face blend
Propagation: Tangent (rounds the whole rounded edge chain) · Minimal
Note: Internal fillets add material (boss); external fillets remove material (soften corner)
Chamfer
Creates a flat angled bevel between two faces — a cut-corner rather than a rounded one. Faster to machine than a fillet (straight cut vs. radius) and useful when you need a flat lead-in surface. Common for: deburring, entry lead-in for pins/shafts, and joining surfaces at specific angles.
Types: Equal distance · Distance + angle · Two distances
Note: A 45° equal chamfer is by far the most common in FRC mechanical work

Copy and pattern operations

Copy & Pattern
Mirror
Creates a mirror copy of features, bodies, or faces about a mirror plane. Onshape's Mirror feature can mirror at the feature level (adds mirrored features to the tree) or the body/face level. Use for symmetric parts to design half and mirror — a single change then updates both sides.
Scope: Features (replays features mirrored) · Faces · Bodies
Plane: Any flat face, sketch plane, or reference plane
Tip: Mirror bodies is faster but less flexible than mirroring features
Rectangular Pattern
Repeats features, faces, or bodies in a 1D or 2D grid. Define direction 1 (count + spacing), optionally direction 2 (count + spacing). Individual instances can be suppressed (removed from the pattern). Used for bolt hole grids, venting slot patterns, and structural rib arrays.
Scope: Features · Faces · Bodies
Direction: Linear edges, sketch lines, or axes

Push/Pull — direct modeling

The Move Face feature in Onshape is the closest equivalent to the "push/pull" tool familiar from SketchUp and Fusion 360. Faster than editing the underlying sketch for simple geometry adjustments, but it doesn't add a parametric sketch to your history.

Feature order strategy

Build the main body shape first with Extrude and Revolve. Add holes and cuts second. Apply Fillets and Chamfers last — rounding operations are the most likely to fail if the geometry changes, so keep them at the end of the feature tree. Patterns and Mirrors go before final edge treatments. Shell immediately follows the base shape if you need a hollow body.

Bézier Curves

Bézier curves are the foundational building blocks that NURBS generalizes. They were independently developed in the 1960s by Pierre Bézier at Renault and Paul de Casteljau at Citroën, both trying to mathematically describe car body panels for numerical machining. A Bézier curve is defined by a set of control points. The curve is pulled toward those points without necessarily passing through them (except at the endpoints).

Linear (Degree 1)
Straight line between two anchor points
Quadratic (Degree 2)
3 pts — one off-curve handle
Cubic (Degree 3)
4 pts — most common in CAD

The de Casteljau algorithm finds any point on a Bézier curve through repeated linear interpolation — no trigonometry, just weighted midpoints applied recursively. Complex shapes are built by chaining multiple Bézier segments together — this is called a B-spline. NURBS is the powerful generalization of B-splines that drives all professional CAD.

FRC relevance

WPILib's autonomous trajectory generation uses cubic Hermite splines — close cousins to cubic Béziers — to plan smooth robot paths. Understanding that "smooth path = mathematically defined handles" helps you tune trajectories intentionally rather than by feel.

NURBS

NURBS — Non-Uniform Rational B-Splines — is the mathematical language that most professional CAD software uses to represent curved geometry. NURBS takes B-splines and adds two capabilities: weights on each control point (the "Rational" part) and a non-uniform knot vector that controls local influence. This makes it flexible enough to represent everything from a perfect circle (which Béziers cannot do exactly) to a compound-curved hood panel — within one consistent framework.

FRC context

When you download a COTS part — a REV motor, a WCP gearbox — as a STEP file, it often has no feature history. Direct modeling tools let you modify those files without rebuilding from scratch.

Watertight Models & Mesh Export

When you export a 3D model for printing, slicing, or simulation, it usually needs to be watertight (also called airtight or manifold). A watertight model is a closed, solid volume with no gaps, holes, or ambiguous geometry — if you could fill it with water, none would leak out. In solid modeling (Onshape, SOLIDWORKS, Fusion), the software enforces watertightness automatically. In surface modeling (Rhino, Blender in mesh mode), you're working with open patches — nothing forces them to be closed. A model may look solid on screen but have tiny gaps. If you export that to STL and send it to a printer, the slicer will fail or produce garbage.

What "manifold" means geometrically

In mesh geometry, a manifold model requires every edge to be shared by exactly two faces — no more, no less.

Manifold Edge ✓
Each edge shared by exactly 2 faces. Unambiguous inside/outside.
Open Edge ✗
An edge with only 1 face — a "hole" in the mesh. Slicers fail here.
Non-Manifold Edge ✗
An edge shared by 3+ faces. Ambiguous — causes broken slices and failed booleans.

Other failure sources: zero-thickness walls (a flat surface with no volume), internal faces (hidden faces from uncleaned boolean operations), T-junctions (vertices sitting on the middle of an edge without splitting it), self-intersecting geometry, and flipped normals (faces pointing inward instead of outward).

From solid to STL — tessellation

When you export to STL, smooth NURBS surfaces are tessellated — approximated by flat triangles. This is a one-way, lossy conversion. The STL format stores only triangle vertices and face normals — no curves, no parameters, no units. Quality is controlled by chord tolerance (max distance between true surface and approximating triangle; smaller = smoother = larger file) and angular tolerance (max angle between adjacent triangle normals).

Curve Continuity

When two curve or surface segments meet at a boundary, continuity describes how smooth that transition is. CAD uses standardized levels G0 through G3, each adding one more requirement.

G₀
Position
Segments touch at endpoints only — may form a sharp corner. Most basic connection.
G₁
Tangent
Segments share the same tangent direction at the junction. No sharp corner, but curvature can change abruptly.
G₂
Curvature
Segments share position, tangent, and radius of curvature. Transitions appear seamless — standard for automotive design.
G₃
Acceleration
Rate of curvature change is also continuous. Completely imperceptible to the human eye even under dynamic lighting.

A standard fillet in most mechanical CAD produces G1 continuity. For smooth aesthetic blends, variable-radius fillets or explicit surface patches achieve G2. For FRC structural parts, G1 is entirely sufficient.

Class A Surfaces

A Class A surface is engineered to the absolute highest quality — mathematically precise, visually perfect, optimized for how it reflects light. The term originates in automotive design: every surface a customer can see, touch, or feel on a finished vehicle.

Class A Requirements

  • G₂ or G₃ continuity minimum — Adjacent patches share position, tangent, and curvature at every boundary
  • Flawless reflections — Zebra stripes flow across patch boundaries with zero kinks or distortions
  • Tight tolerances — Position deviations within 0.001 mm. Tangency under 0.05°
  • Minimalist patch layout — Single-span NURBS patches, degree 5–7, to eliminate surface ripple
  • Manufacturing constraints respected — Mold draft angles, tooling access, material behavior baked in

Class A surfacing is practiced in CATIA, Autodesk Alias, and Siemens NX by engineers with years of dedicated training. Beyond FRC scope — but knowing it exists explains why automotive CAD looks so different from mechanical part modeling.

Generative Design & Lightweighting

One of the most visually striking things you'll see in modern CAD — particularly Fusion 360 — is generative design: parts that look like they grew rather than were drawn. Bone-like lattices, organic branching structures arranged with eerie efficiency. These aren't artistic choices. They're the result of algorithms finding the most structurally efficient way to carry load using as little material as possible.

step 01
Define Loads
Specify forces, moments, and fixed constraint points on your design space
step 02
Set Constraints
Define preserved regions (bolt holes), obstacle regions to avoid, material, and manufacturing method
step 03
Run Algorithm
The solver iteratively removes underloaded material while maintaining structural targets
step 04
Evaluate Results
Multiple organic design candidates returned, each optimized for a different manufacturing method

Topology optimization (the classical method) returns one correct answer given your load inputs. Generative design (Autodesk's Fusion term) extends this with cloud computing to return multiple options optimized for different manufacturing methods. Both produce organic shapes because nature itself uses topology optimization — bones, coral, and tree branches all minimize material while maximizing structural performance under load.

In FRC we usually do a simpler version manually: pocketing — cutting recessed voids into structural plates to remove material in regions that don't carry significant load. Done thoughtfully, pocketing can reduce a plate's weight by 30–50% with minimal structural penalty. The community has published FeatureScripts for automated lightweighting — hexagonal or circular pocket fill at a specified infill fraction.

FRC weight budget

A robot that hits 120 lbs before sensors and bumpers is a problem. Pocketing your bellypan, drive rails, and intake plates — with either manual operations or a lightweighting FeatureScript — is one of the first optimization passes experienced teams make after the initial design is locked.

Orthographic Views

Orthographic projection is the method used to represent a 3D object on a flat 2D drawing. Unlike perspective drawing (where objects appear smaller the further away they are), orthographic projection uses parallel projection lines perpendicular to the drawing plane — so a 20mm edge measures exactly 20mm regardless of where it sits in space. Every engineering drawing uses orthographic projection because it preserves true dimensions and allows direct measurement from the drawing.

A single orthographic view is ambiguous — a circle in one view could be a sphere, a cylinder, a cone, or a hole. Multiple views, arranged according to a standard, eliminate the ambiguity by showing the same geometry from different directions simultaneously.

The glass box concept

The classic mental model: imagine placing your object inside a transparent glass box. Project the object's outline onto each face of the box by looking straight through that face. Then unfold the box into a flat arrangement. The resulting six flat projections — one per face — are the six standard orthographic views.

Object inside a glass box — looking straight through each face produces one orthographic view

The six standard views

Unfolding the glass box produces a cross-shaped arrangement. The front view is the anchor — the other five unfold around it. The front view is chosen to be the most descriptive view: the one that best shows the shape of the part.

In practice, most engineering drawings use three views: front, top, and right (or left). These three together are sufficient to unambiguously describe most mechanical parts. Complex parts — casting, forging, anything with features on multiple sides — may need more. Simple symmetric parts (a bolt, a shaft) may only need two.

Reading a three-view drawing — alignment rules

The power of the three-view layout comes from the precise spatial relationships between the views. When laid out in 3rd angle projection (ASME, US standard):

Front and top views share the same width. Any vertical line in the front view projects directly to the same X position in the top view — draw a vertical line through any feature in the front view and it intersects the same feature in the top view.

Front and right views share the same height. Any horizontal line in the front view projects directly to the same Y position in the right view.

Top and right views share the same depth. Depth in the top view (front-to-back) equals depth in the right view (left-to-right). This relationship is often shown in traditional drafting with a 45° miter line that maps depth from horizontal to vertical.

Interactive — Three-View Layout

The object is a stepped rectangular block — a 60×40×50 mm block with a 25×15 mm step cut from the upper-left front corner. Toggle the overlays to see how features map between views.

How features project across views

The fundamental skill of reading a multi-view drawing is tracing a feature through all three views simultaneously. Any edge or feature that exists in the object must appear in all views where it is visible — either as a solid line (visible) or a dashed line (hidden).

In the example above: the step cut is visible as the L-shape in the front view, appears as a vertical line (the step edge) in the top view, and becomes a horizontal dashed line in the right view — because from the right, the step is inside the block and blocked by the solid right face.

When you get lost, trace outlines. Start with the outermost boundary rectangle in each view — these must all be consistent. Then find each internal line and ask: what 3D feature does this represent? Find it in the other views.

Circular features project as ellipses at angles, lines in profile. A cylindrical hole appears as a circle only when you look directly along its axis. In the perpendicular views it appears as two parallel lines (visible or hidden). This is why every hole needs a centerline — it tells you the axis direction, and lets you find the corresponding circle in whichever view is parallel to that axis.

Onshape: automatic orthographic views

Onshape generates orthographic views automatically in the Drawing tab. Choose the primary view orientation, drag it onto the sheet, then right-click it to "Project Views" — top, right, left, and isometric views are generated instantly and stay linked to the Part Studio. Change the model and all drawing views update. You never draw views by hand; you review them for completeness, add dimensions, and annotate.

Minimum views needed

A good rule: use the minimum number of views that unambiguously describe the part. Most prismatic parts (things made of flat faces and holes) need exactly three. Turned parts (shafts, bolts, anything rotationally symmetric) usually only need one view plus a note — a circle and diameter symbol says "cylinder" more efficiently than two identical views. Very complex parts (engine blocks, castings with features on many faces) may need six views plus several section views.

Engineering Drawings & Standards

A engineering drawing (also called a technical drawing or print) is a formal 2D document that precisely communicates a 3D part's geometry, dimensions, tolerances, materials, and notes to a manufacturer. CAD software generates these from 3D models — you place views, add dimensions and annotations, and the drawing stays live-linked to the model.

Engineering drawings follow standardized formats. Two dominant standards govern technical drawings worldwide, and they differ in meaningful ways:

ASME Y14.5 (US / North America)

American Society of Mechanical Engineers. Used throughout North American manufacturing, aerospace, and defense. Uses Third Angle Projection by default. Inch and metric units both common. Symbol: circle with truncated cone, wide end toward viewer.

ISO 128 / ISO 2768 (International)

International Organization for Standardization. Used across Europe, Asia, and most of the rest of the world. Uses First Angle Projection by default. Metric units. Symbol: circle with cone, narrow end toward viewer.

FEA & CFD

Engineering analysis in CAD divides into two major simulation types that solve very different physical problems. Understanding which one you need — and what each can and can't tell you — is fundamental to using simulation tools responsibly.

FEA — Finite Element Analysis

What it solves: How solid structures deform, stress, and fail under mechanical loads.

  • Apply forces, pressures, fixed supports to a part
  • Mesh the geometry into small elements (tetrahedra, hexahedra)
  • Solver computes stress, strain, deformation at every element
  • Color maps show stress concentration — high-stress zones in red
  • Outputs: von Mises stress, displacement, safety factor
  • Use for: brackets, frames, shafts, welds, press fits

Available in: Fusion 360 (Simulation workspace), SOLIDWORKS Simulation, Ansys, SimScale (free browser-based), Onshape (limited, via SimScale integration)

CFD — Computational Fluid Dynamics

What it solves: How fluids (air, water, coolant) flow around or through geometry.

  • Define flow domain, inlet velocity, fluid properties
  • Solve Navier-Stokes equations across a fluid mesh
  • Outputs: velocity fields, pressure distribution, drag/lift forces
  • Use for: aerodynamic fairing design, cooling duct optimization, intake plenum flow
  • Much more computationally expensive than FEA
  • Results extremely sensitive to mesh quality and boundary conditions

Available in: Ansys Fluent, OpenFOAM (free, open-source), Fusion 360 (basic), SimScale (browser-based, free tier)

FEA and CFD use very different mesh types. FEA meshes the solid body itself. CFD meshes the fluid domain around the solid, with boundary layers of refined mesh near walls where velocity gradients are steep. Both require careful validation — simulation results are only as good as your boundary conditions, material properties, and mesh quality. "Garbage in, garbage out" applies especially to simulation.

FRC application

FEA is useful for validating critical custom brackets — intake pivots, drivetrain side plates, and arm joints that will see high repeated loading. Quick FEA in Fusion 360 or SimScale can catch under-designed parts before build season. CFD matters less in FRC since robots move slowly and aerodynamics are largely irrelevant — but teams doing shooter wheel or flywheel design sometimes use it to understand airflow through their mechanisms.

Creative & Sculpt Tools

Not everything in 3D is engineering CAD. A parallel world of tools handles organic shapes, animation, rendering, and digital art — useful for robot reveals, sponsor materials, and your broader design vocabulary.

Blender

Blender is a free, open-source 3D creation suite that has become genuinely professional-grade. It covers modeling, rigging, animation, rendering, compositing, sculpting, video editing, and 2D animation — all in one application. For FRC teams, it's particularly valuable for creating compelling robot reveal videos and sponsor-facing visualizations.

Modeling in Blender is polygon mesh-based, with three main modes: Object Mode (selecting and arranging objects), Edit Mode (manipulating geometry directly), and Sculpt Mode (clay-like organic sculpting). This is fundamentally different from NURBS-based CAD — there's no parametric history, no dimensional constraints, and no manufacturability guarantees. But for anything destined for a screen rather than a CNC machine, mesh modeling is often faster and more expressive.

Rendering engines: Blender ships with two: Cycles is a path-tracing renderer that produces photorealistic results by physically simulating light bouncing through a scene — excellent for still renders and animations but slower. EEVEE is a real-time renderer that trades some realism for speed, producing results in seconds rather than minutes. EEVEE is typically sufficient for robot reveal videos.

Geometry Nodes is Blender's visual programming system — you wire together algorithmic nodes to procedurally generate geometry from rules. While not the same as parametric CAD history, it brings a similar concept of rule-driven design to a mesh environment. It's become extremely powerful in recent versions for generating complex arrays, architectural details, and organic patterns.

Importing CAD geometry: Blender can import OBJ and FBX files directly. For STEP files (the native Onshape export), you need either a converting step through Fusion or a free add-on. Once imported, a robot assembly can be textured, lit, and animated for a reveal video. The typical FRC pipeline: Onshape → export individual parts as OBJ or STL → import into Blender → assign materials → animate → render with EEVEE → edit in the video editor or export to Premiere/DaVinci.

The FRC use cases: robot reveal videos, animated mechanism explainers for Chairman's/Impact Award presentations, photorealistic sponsor render deliverables, pit display visuals, and team branding renders. Teams that invest time in Blender for their media assets often stand out significantly in the pit and in award presentations.

Cloud vs. Local CAD

Where your CAD software lives has real practical consequences. Cloud-native tools like Onshape represent one philosophy; locally installed tools like SOLIDWORKS and Fusion represent another.

Cloud CAD (Onshape model)

  • Zero installation — any browser, any device
  • Always latest version, no update management
  • Real-time collaboration baked in from day one
  • Version control built into the platform
  • Files safe — no local disk failure risk
  • No internet = no work. Entire team blocked at once
  • Browser GPU rendering still stresses older machines
  • Files live on vendor servers — IP/privacy concerns
  • Service outages affect everyone simultaneously

Local CAD (Fusion/SOLIDWORKS)

  • Works completely offline — competition, plane, anywhere
  • Files on your machine — you own them completely
  • Performance scales with hardware investment
  • Can leverage a dedicated GPU directly
  • Sensitive IP stays on your local network
  • Installation and license management required
  • Updates can break workflows
  • Collaboration requires PDM systems or manual file sharing
  • Requires meaningful hardware — good GPU, RAM, fast SSD

The internet dependency of cloud CAD is more disruptive than it sounds. A dropped WiFi connection at a build meeting stops the whole team cold. A spotty hotel connection during competition week turns a design session into frustration. Local CAD has no such single point of failure.

The hardware argument cuts both ways. Yes, Onshape "runs on a Chromebook" — technically true. But a complex robot assembly in any browser-based CAD tool still taxes the GPU heavily via WebGL. A decent gaming laptop with a discrete GPU — even a few years old — will often outperform a current budget Chromebook for serious CAD work.

The hybrid reality

Fusion 360 sits in an interesting middle ground — a local install with cloud sync. You get offline reliability and full GPU access, plus cloud backup and basic sharing. Not as collaborative as Onshape, but more resilient to connectivity problems.

Hardware & Ergonomics

CAD is physically demanding in a way that isn't obvious at first. Hours of precise mouse and keyboard work accumulate into repetitive strain injuries, carpal tunnel syndrome, and chronic wrist and shoulder problems. The tools you use and how you set up your workspace matter more than most people realize — and the damage from poor ergonomics starts early.

A word on trackpads

Doing serious CAD on a trackpad is the single fastest route to hand fatigue and RSI. Trackpads require continuous muscle tension to maintain precision, and CAD demands precision continuously for hours. If you're doing more than casual work, get a real mouse. This is occupational health advice, not preference.

The SpaceMouse

The single most impactful hardware upgrade for CAD work is a SpaceMouse from 3Dconnexion. It's a 6-axis input device that sits to the left of your regular mouse — you navigate 3D space with your left hand (pan, zoom, rotate, and tilt simultaneously) while using your right hand for selections and tool operations. Once learned, it eliminates the constant mode-switching between navigation and modeling and dramatically reduces cumulative mouse travel over long sessions. Native driver support in Onshape, SOLIDWORKS, Fusion, CATIA, and most professional CAD tools. Entry-level: SpaceMouse Compact. With macro buttons: SpaceMouse Pro.

Mice

Premium pick

Logitech MX Master 3S / MX Master 4

The MX Master series has long been the recommendation for CAD and design work. Ergonomic sculpted grip, high-resolution sensor, Magspeed electromagnetic scroll wheel, and a horizontal scroll wheel useful in Onshape. Heavy relative to gaming mice, but the weight distribution aids precision during sustained CAD sessions. The 3S added near-silent clicks; the MX Master 4 continues the lineage.

Keyboards, Numpads & Macro Pads

CAD tools are heavily keyboard-driven. Most operations have shortcuts, and using them fluently is the difference between fast and slow designers. A few things worth knowing:

Ergonomics

Split / Ergonomic Keyboards

Split keyboards like the Keychron Q11, ZSA Moonlander, or Kinesis Advantage force your hands into a more natural shoulder-width position, reducing the ulnar deviation that causes wrist strain. If you spend hours daily at a keyboard, an ergonomic split layout is one of the best long-term injury-prevention investments available.

Productivity tip

Tenkeyless + Separate Numpad

A personal favorite setup: a compact tenkeyless keyboard (no integrated numpad) paired with a standalone numpad. The numpad can be placed to the right of your mouse when entering dimension values, then moved out of the way when you need desk space. This gives you the clean compact desktop of a TKL keyboard while retaining full numpad access without committing to a fixed layout. Works well with Keychron, Nuphy, and similar modular setups.

Macro pads

The Stream Deck's LCD buttons can be programmed with CAD-specific shortcuts: common views, feature toggles, FeatureScript operations, sketch mode switches. Used by a growing number of experienced CAD designers. The LCD labels update per-application so the same device works for CAD, video editing, and streaming. Small (6-key) to large (32-key) configurations available.

Numbers

Numpad Access for CAD

Whether integrated, standalone, or via a Stream Deck profile — numpad access matters in CAD for entering precise dimension values quickly. Standard views (Front=1, Top=7, Right=3 in many tools) are also numpad-mapped. If your keyboard lacks a numpad, a <15$ USB numpad is a worthwhile addition.

Prevent RSI & Carpal Tunnel

Take structured breaks. Every hour, 5 minutes away from the mouse and keyboard. Shake out your hands, stretch wrists and forearms. Small regular breaks prevent cumulative strain from reaching injury level. Relax your grip. White knuckles on a mouse = fast path to tendinitis. A relaxed whole-hand grip reduces cumulative strain dramatically. Adjust desk height. Elbows at ~90°, forearms parallel to the desk, wrists floating — not resting on a hard edge during active mouse movement.

Onshape & FRC

Onshape is our primary CAD tool — the default choice for this class and for many competitive FRC teams. It's fully browser-based, free for education, and has the deepest FRC community adoption of any platform. Here's how it's organized and why it differs from other tools.

Documents, Part Studios, Assemblies, and Drawings

Everything in Onshape lives in a Document. A Document is a container that holds multiple tabs — each tab can be a Part Studio, an Assembly, a Drawing, or a FeatureScript file. Unlike SOLIDWORKS (one-part-per-file) or Fusion (separate project files per design), an entire robot subsystem can live in one Document with many Part Studios and Assemblies all linked together.

Part Studios are where you design parts. A Part Studio is a parametric modeling environment with a feature tree on the left panel. What makes Onshape's Part Studios unusual compared to SOLIDWORKS is that multiple parts can coexist in a single Part Studio and reference each other's geometry directly. You can design an intake pivot bracket and its mating plate in the same Part Studio — the bracket sketch can be coincident with the plate's face, the hole patterns can reference each other's geometry, and the part dimensions are inherently coordinated. This is sometimes called in-context modeling, and it reduces the gap between designing parts in isolation and designing parts as a system.

In SOLIDWORKS, the equivalent workflow requires external references between separate files — powerful but more complex to manage. Onshape's Part Studio approach is more beginner-friendly and especially effective for FRC, where subsystem geometry is often tightly coupled.

Assemblies are where you bring parts together with Mates — Onshape's term for assembly constraints. Fasten two faces, make a shaft revolve inside a bearing, constrain a slider to a track. Assemblies automatically pull parts from Part Studios in the same Document, and can also import parts from other Documents.

Drawings in Onshape are live-linked to the models — change a dimension in a Part Studio and the associated drawing view updates automatically. You can add dimensions, tolerances, and GD&T annotations directly in the Drawing tab.

Onshape advantages

  • Zero install — browser runs on any device
  • Free for education with full features
  • Real-time multi-user collaboration — multiple people live in the same Part Studio
  • Built-in version control — branches, history, merge
  • FeatureScript — write custom features in code
  • MKCAD library — massive FRC COTS part library built in
  • Multi-part Part Studios — in-context design is natural
  • Zero version mismatch problems across team members

Onshape limitations

  • Requires internet — no offline fallback
  • No native CAM — need external tools for CNC toolpaths
  • No built-in renderer — export to Blender or KeyShot
  • Simulation capabilities are limited without third-party integration
  • Free plan makes documents publicly searchable
  • Performance on complex assemblies can lag in browser
  • Mobile experience is present but not ideal for serious work

Version control in Onshape

Every Document in Onshape has a built-in version history. You can create named Versions (snapshots) and Branches (parallel development lines). A team can have a stable "competition" branch and an experimental "intake-redesign" branch, work in parallel, and merge if the experimental design is approved — all without needing Git. This is one of Onshape's most underutilized features among FRC teams and one of its most powerful.

MKCAD — the FRC parts library

MKCAD (Make Kits CAD) is a community-maintained Onshape library of hundreds of FRC-relevant COTS components — REV motors, WCP gearboxes, TTB structural extrusions, Andymark wheels, sensors, pneumatic components, and more. Insert them directly into your assemblies from within Onshape without hunting for STEP files. Keeping up with MKCAD updates is worth doing at the start of each season as new COTS parts are added.

FeatureScript

FeatureScript is Onshape's built-in programming language for creating custom parametric features. Every built-in Onshape feature — Extrude, Fillet, Hole, Helix — is written in FeatureScript. The language is open: you can read the source code for all built-in features, write your own features from scratch, and share them with the community.

FeatureScript is a domain-specific language that looks and feels like a modern scripting language — similar to TypeScript or Swift in syntax. It runs inside Onshape's geometry kernel, which means it has direct access to geometric operations: boolean operations, sketch constraints, pattern generation, surface evaluation, and more.

// Simple FeatureScript example — a custom slot feature FeatureScript 2011; import(path : "onshape/std/geometry.fs", version : "2011.0"); annotation { "Feature Type Name" : "Slot" } export const slot = defineFeature(function(context is Context, id is Id, definition is map) { annotation { "Name" : "Width", "UIHint" : UIHint.REMEMBER_PREVIOUS_VALUE } definition.width is ValueWithUnits; annotation { "Name" : "Length", "UIHint" : UIHint.REMEMBER_PREVIOUS_VALUE } definition.length is ValueWithUnits; // Geometry construction goes here opExtrude(context, id + "extrude", { ... }); });

The FRC FeatureScript community

The FRC community has built an extensive library of FeatureScripts specifically for robot design. Community contributions include:

Lightweighting scripts — automatically fill a plate with pocketing patterns (hexagonal, circular, triangular) at a specified infill fraction. Dramatically reduces the time to pocket parts correctly.

Gusset generators — parametrically generate the standard FRC gusset patterns that fit REV and WCP structural tubing hole patterns. Change the tube length and the gussets update automatically.

Tube converter — convert a sketch path into a routed tube segment with endcap faces ready for assembly mates.

Swerve module templates — pre-built parametric assemblies for common swerve drive configurations.

These community scripts live in Onshape Documents that can be added to your Feature toolbar with one click. Julia Schatz’s FeatureScripts (part of the FRCDesign.org course setup) and many others are entire suites of FRC-specific FeatureScripts. Knowing these exist and how to install them is one of the first productivity steps for a new Onshape-FRC user.

Finding community FeatureScripts

Search Onshape's public documents for "FRC FeatureScript" or visit the Chief Delphi and FRC Discord CAD channels. The most-used scripts are well-documented. Most are installed by opening the Document, finding the Feature in the toolbar, and using "Add custom features to your toolbar."
