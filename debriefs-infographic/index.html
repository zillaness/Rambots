Sacramento Regional Report Mentor report

Written by a mentor for families, 2024-03-17. Not team input. Names shortened to first names.

The student newsletter is coming out soon, but I suspect they'll probably be sleeping, and recovering for the next few days. Many of them worked 16+ hour days at the competition. I'm sure the students will appreciate a little grace on their assignments. So for now I'm excited to share a summary of our time at the Sacramento regional from the perspective of a team mentor.

The 2024 FIRST Sacramento Regional proved to be a rollercoaster of emotions, challenges, and triumphs for our team, as we exceeded expectations and etched our names in the records. Rambots 2204 finished 26th in rankings and went into the playoffs as the 3rd backup team. To our delight and surprise we were asked to join 1678 Citrus Circuits' alliance, when one of their alliance members took irreparable damage. Citrus Circuits is a internationally top ranked team based in Davis, California,regularly placing highly at world championships and having won the whole championship in 2015, and . It was an emotional rollercoaster of an event, with students experiencing the full euphoric stress of a typical robotics competition. We made it all the way to the final match where we were eliminated. Still we bring home a trophy for being 2024 Sacramento Regional Finalists, our new highest placement in the history of our program.

In the finals after a brutal collision with an opposing robot sheared off the whole top half of our robot. Students from Rambots 2204 worked hand in hand with Citrus Circuits 1678 to get our back and working. Citrus Circuits provided expertise and spare parts to help our students triage and repair the massive damage. For their help, and for their faith in us selecting us to be their backup when another alliance member went down we gifted them some of our signature Ram Hats, this year with real floppy ear action.

[student] was voted as a Safety all star and also recognized for his gracious professionalism. The Safety All Star is given to a safety captain, that fellow safety captains from other schools nominate and vote on.

[student] was awarded an award for Gracious Professionalism for his enthusiastic portrayal of Rambo, greeting young children, making them feel safe and welcome.

We need help. East Bay regionals is coming soon, and we intend to lick our wounds, and implement fixes for the hard fought lessons we learned in Sacramento. Our electrical system experienced many faults, and we learned we need to improve it to withstand the impacts at the higher level of competition. Our drivers [student] and [student] performed admirably, but also need time to practice to compete with the new higher level of competition we are facing. The Rambots intend to win it all at East Bay regional and make it to the World Championships. We need time and help monitoring team meetings to fix our robot and hold practice sessions. If you like robots, and students striving for excellence in everything they do, please contact [mentor], or [mentor] to be a team mentor, experience not necessary.

Preliminary Report Mentor analysis

Google Doc, 2025-04-10. A mentor's read of the survey.

Starting with the Positives

  • The outlook is bright: almost everyone is VERY excited for next season (and a little anxious). They can't wait for preseason, and want the upcoming one to be more in-depth and hands-on. They want it to be stronger than ever.
  • Open House and Christmas Carnival both ranked extremely highly, showing that this team can be great at outreach; they just don't think of this as outreach yet.
  • Business has fairly good morale. They appreciate and admire both student leadership and mentors.
  • Programming has good morale as well. They feel like they learned a lot, appreciated [mentor], and while they didn't interact much with other mentors, they expressed positive sentiments overall.
  • Organization and cleanliness is improving. The cleanup checklist is considered very helpful overall, but we can't rest on our laurels, and this is an area we need to keep improving.

Areas to Improve

  • Standups, mentor updates, and season pacing were all ranked below average, all things associated with meeting planning and structure. There were also complaints about scheduling, likely due to last-minute meeting changes.
  • Documentation and judging were ranked below average as well, continuing to be an area we need to improve on.
  • Morale among the build team is notably lower than other subteams, with an average score of 2.375 and a mode of 3. They also rated student leadership and mentorship lower in both effectiveness and satisfaction. One particularly angry response stood out, blaming adults for taking credit while students do all the work. It's worth noting this came from a response that may have been written by multiple students together ([student] stated that he and [student] filled out the form togetehr in class), which could explain some contradictory answers. Regardless, this perception, that mentors don't contribute meaningfully, is the biggest threat to our next season. It shows a disconnect in how our contributions are understood or valued, and how we communicate the work we do behind the scenes.
  • There is also a common belief that the team gives up too easily, sometimes with a defeatist attitude. Several responses indicated that if we had just pushed a little harder for a little longer, we might have seen significantly better results.

Tensions and Team Dynamics

  • Build team members want more room to make mistakes and want mentors to hold off on intervening. There's tension around when intervention is appropriate: should mentors step in only if it's a costly mistake, whether in terms of time, money, or both? A dangerous one? There's even disagreement between mentors and students about what's considered unsafe. This creates a difficult dynamic where students want the freedom to fail but still want to be shielded from the consequences.
  • On the flip side, Business-side students appreciate mentor oversight and want closer monitoring to help prevent financial setbacks and mistakes.
  • This reinforces an idea we've long known: Preseason is the time to make mistakes. That's where we build trust, fail safely, and set expectations. Clarifying boundaries early might help ease mentor-student tension during competition season.

Ending on a Positive

  • We have passionate students, engaged mentors, and a growing understanding of where our pain points lie. With focused preseason planning, improved internal communication, and intentional mentoring, we're well positioned for a strong, confident return next season.

Socratic seminar Seminar summary

Google Doc "2026 Socratic Seminar Summary, Sacramento FRC Debrief" (2026-04-16). A written summary, not raw notes.

Don't overbuild last-minute; commit to decisions earlierFix team culture; reduce negative self-talkIncrease bandwidth; more prototyping + iterationScouting is strong; improve how it's usedAdaptability is good; but align with long-term goalsImprove pit structure; increase student involvementLearning must happen outside meetingsGoal: become a top-tier, self-driving team

1. Pre-Competition Prep (Week 0 → Sac)

  • The team overhauled too many systems too quickly (mechanical + software) in ~2–2.5 weeks.
  • This led to: lack of stability by competition; constant shifting design direction.
  • Key takeaway: more disciplined decision-making and sticking to a plan is needed; avoid "last-minute full rebuild" unless absolutely necessary.
  • Core idea: Depth over breadth, fewer, more refined changes.

2. Mindset & Team Culture

  • Mentors emphasized reducing negative self-talk ("we always mess up").
  • Important distinction: it's okay to fail; not okay to normalize failure as identity.
  • Strong belief: failure is part of learning, but reflection must lead to improvement.
  • TL;DR: Fail → learn → improve (not fail → joke → repeat)

3. Bandwidth & Resource Constraints

  • Major limitation compared to top teams (like Citrus): fewer people → fewer parallel prototypes.
  • Result: only 1 design path explored per subsystem instead of multiple.
  • Key insight: prototyping = learning what NOT to do quickly.
  • TL;DR: More people = more iteration → better final design

4. Scouting & Strategy

  • Scouting was a major success: high participation (lots of matches covered); useful qualitative + quantitative data.
  • Improvements: better filtering of what actually matters (salient data); use scouting more effectively for strategy decisions.
  • Added value: scouting also builds relationships + alliance opportunities.
  • TL;DR: Data is only valuable if you can interpret and use it

5. Competition Performance & Identity

  • Team ended up as a valuable support robot (defense/push role).
  • Mixed feelings: proud of adaptability and execution; but not aligned with original goal (scoring/shooting robot).
  • Key tension: playing a role vs. achieving intended robot capability.
  • TL;DR: Adapt to win, but don't lose sight of long-term goals

6. Pit & On-Site Execution

  • Pit space was crowded and underutilized; limited opportunities for newer students to participate.
  • Goal moving forward: better pit organization + rotation system; increase hands-on experience pipeline.
  • TL;DR: More structured access → more student growth

7. Student Engagement & Culture Wins

  • Big positive: high engagement, students actively contributing.
  • Also valuable: networking with other teams; social interactions at comp.
  • Reminder: "fun at comp" should be unique experiences, not passive phone time.
  • TL;DR: Engagement + community = long-term team strength

8. Learning & Skill Development

  • Mentors stressed: learning cannot happen only during build season.
  • Students must self-learn (YouTube, practice, etc.) and use offseason effectively.
  • Constraint: limited mentor bandwidth → students need initiative.
  • TL;DR: Offseason learning = in-season performance

9. Big Picture Takeaways

  • The team is improving and competitive, but still in a growth phase.
  • Next step evolution: from "Good enough to be picked" to alliance captain / top-tier team.
  • TL;DR: Shift from participant → contender → leader

2026 summer boot camp

Sam's instructor debrief of the May 25 to 29, 2026 fabrication boot camp: eight students, FRCdesign.org Stage 1A and 1B, a gusset project, lectures, and Jeopardy reviews. Student names are left out, as the debrief asks.

Verdict Instructor debrief

The week produced real hands-on results, and several of its best moments were unplanned. The schedule was wrong in one consistent direction: everything took longer than planned, and lectures were the first thing displaced.

Students worked through FRCdesign.org Stage 1A on Days 1 and 2, built the gusset project, learned the laser cutter and the Bambu A1 Mini, and sat through the electricity and fasteners lectures, with material selection added on Day 3. Most reached or passed Stage 1B by Thursday or Friday, two days later than planned. The engineering design cycle and punk rock engineering lectures were never delivered, the FRC intro slid from Day 1 to Day 5, and no swerve build appears anywhere in the recorded actuals.

Sam, Oct 2026: “summer meetings are good, bootcamp is good.”

Course facts

  • Monday May 25 to Friday May 29, 2026, with a Saturday continuation for one returning student
  • Eight students, from returning and advanced to first exposure to FRC and CAD
  • Spine: FRCdesign.org Stage 1A then Stage 1B, instructor lectures, one fabrication project, student-directed time
  • Tools: Onshape, laser cutter, Bambu A1 Mini; #10-32 fasteners, MaxTube, acrylic, wood, PLA

Plan versus actual

Day by day, as recorded in the debrief.

Day 1, Monday May 25

Planned: intro to CAD lecture, drive base game, Onshape setup and Stage 1A start, lunch, electricity lecture, two 15-minute stretch or eye breaks, inventory software demo, shop cleanup and inventory.

Actual:

  • 9:00 to 10:30: intro to CAD lecture (about 90 minutes)
  • 10:30 to 10:45: break
  • 10:45 to about 12:00: Drive Showdown game
  • About 12:00 to 1:30: Stage 1A, reaching Section 1 Exercise 5. No formal lunch; students ate and kept working.
  • About 1:30 to 3:10: electricity lecture
  • 3:10 to 3:40: unplanned break because students were burned out
  • 3:40 to 4:45 or 5:00: finished electricity lecture

Dropped: formal lunch, both stretch breaks, the inventory software demo, shop cleanup and inventory. Homework: finish Stage 1A, bring chargers and a mouse, consider headphones.

Day 2, Tuesday May 26

Electrical Jeopardy opener. Stage 1A Section 2 (plate workflow, gussets, superstructure gussets and plates, motor mounting). Digital fabrication lecture, laser cutter tutorial, Bambu A1 Mini tutorial. The gusset project ran the rest of the day; it was planned for half a day. Fasteners lecture with the Bolt Depot handout, drill/tap/clearance chart, and Starrett charts. Homework: Stage 1B (completion uncertain). The formal introduction of the new student planned for this morning did not happen.

The gusset project joined MaxTube sections at an acute angle using laser-cut wood and 3D-printed PLA plates with #10-32 fasteners. The handout left out the hole measurements so students had to derive them from the plus or minus half-pitch rule.

Day 3, Wednesday May 27

Fastener Jeopardy opener. Material selection lecture. Student project poll, then free project time. Some students took the 3D CAD challenge (Sam's college-taught material plus CSWA-style content), most worked on Stage 1B, and one student worked on a separate art project. 3D Printer Jeopardy and tool quizzes. The vinyl cutter demo was planned and did not happen.

Day 4, Thursday May 28

Most students were at or through Stage 1B. Some continued the CSWA-style challenge. A returning student explored tooltrace.ai and Tool Kaiser. The pinewood derby was introduced and not run.

Day 5, Friday May 29

Intro to FRC lecture (planned for Day 1). Claude lesson covering prompting practices, token efficiency, and building artifacts; each student made an artifact about something they learned that week. A returning student worked with mecanum wheels on a Vex bot. The class washed the 2013 robot, and the last 30 minutes were spent outside with ranger pushups and a conversation about FRC lifting day. This was most students' final day.

Saturday May 30

A returning student and Sam continued: more mecanum work, senior trophies, a 3D scanner trial.

What worked

  • Jeopardy openers (Electrical on Day 2, Fastener on Day 3, 3D Printer on Day 3). Late arrivals could join without disruption, they reviewed prior content, and they were easy to cut short.
  • Drive Showdown as a Day 1 activity.
  • The gusset project as a fabrication anchor, once the full day was given to it.
  • Student-directed time. A student finding tooltrace.ai, students choosing projects on Day 3, and mecanum work were among the most memorable parts of the week.
  • The CSWA-style 3D CAD challenge served as an advanced track on Days 3 and 4 without being planned as one.
  • Fasteners content tied to hands-on use, with the Bolt Depot handout and Starrett charts as physical references.
  • The Claude lesson and artifact building on the final day.
  • The ending. Washing the 2013 robot, going outside, and the pushups were unplanned and made Friday afternoon feel like a send-off.

What did not work

  • Lectures got displaced. The FRC intro moved to Day 5. The engineering design cycle and punk rock engineering lectures were dropped. The intro to drive bases and mechanisms was dropped and had already been noted as a weak lecture.
  • Day 1 pacing. No real lunch and no stretch breaks led to the 3:10 crash after nearly six hours of screen and lecture time with one 15-minute break. The electricity lecture ran about 3.5 hours including the forced break.
  • Stage 1B timing. Planned for Tuesday afternoon, actually 3 to 4 days of partial work.
  • Fabrication estimates. The gusset project took double its planned time, and Sam saw the same pattern in every hands-on activity.
  • New student introductions. The new student's formal introduction never happened.
  • Demos that never ran: vinyl cutter, and the cordless drill with wood screws (planned twice, skipped twice).
  • Logistics not reached: sourcing notebooks, the inventory software demo, shop cleanup and inventory.
  • Swerve capstone. The planning phase treated swerve, WPILib, and YAGSL programming as the capstone. The recorded actuals contain none of it.

Rules for the next iteration

These come from the Week 1 summary Sam commissioned on May 30. Week 2 is not scheduled, so none have been tested.

  1. Double fabrication estimates. Budget 3 to 4 days of partial work for Stage 1B and treat completing it as a stretch goal in a five-day course.
  2. Lock the core lectures into Day 1 morning. FRC intro, engineering design cycle, punk rock engineering. Planning them as flexible filler is how they ended up never happening.
  3. Introduce new students formally on Day 1, first thing. Skipping it had an outsized effect on a new student's sense of belonging.
  4. Protect lunch. A real 30-minute break away from screens. Add 5 minutes of physical activity after the morning opener and a 10-minute outdoor break after lunch, every day.
  5. Split or cut the electricity lecture. Either trim the content or spread it across Day 1 afternoon and Day 2 morning.
  6. Ready an advanced track before day one. Stage 1B done, then the 3D CAD challenge, then tooltrace.ai or free exploration, handed over without setup time.
  7. Schedule student-directed time explicitly. It produced the best moments when it happened by accident.
  8. End the last day with something physical and communal. It does not need to be educational.
  9. Keep the Jeopardy openers.
  10. Run the pinewood derby as a full-day event. (design, cut, weigh, finish, race), and only after track dimensions are confirmed.

Content to add: cordless drill and wood screws demo, vinyl cutter demo, a physical activity routine built into the daily template. Content to reconsider: the drive bases and mechanisms intro (improve or cut).

Decisions carried forward

  • Swerve stays core to the curriculum. Sam confirmed that swerve is required for competitive relevance, which overrode an early suggestion to defer it to a second year. Week 1 shows it did not get time, so the open question is where to fit it.
  • The 2013 robot retrofit is a separate long-term student project. Mixing it into a fixed one-week window adds fabrication and problem-solving scope the window cannot absorb.
  • Hole measurements stay off student handouts so students derive them from the half-pitch rule.
  • Sam's own wording is preserved when formatting his content for sharing.
