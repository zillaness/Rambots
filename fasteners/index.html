WHY THREADS MATTER — HISTORY

The Most Important Invention Nobody Talks About

Before standardized threads, every bolt was a custom part. If a nut came from one workshop, it probably wouldn't fit a bolt from another. Machines broke, and parts couldn't be replaced. Manufacturing was slow, expensive, and deeply unreliable.

This wasn't just inconvenient — it was the fundamental bottleneck preventing large-scale industry from existing at all.

Joseph Whitworth — 1841

In 1841, British engineer Joseph Whitworth surveyed the screws in use across British workshops and found nearly every one was different. He proposed a single standard: a 55° thread form with defined pitches for each diameter. Parliament adopted it. For the first time, a nut made in Birmingham would fit a bolt made in Manchester.

"The standard of measurement which has been neglected is that of perfect accuracy of workmanship." — Joseph Whitworth, 1840

This was the British Standard Whitworth (BSW) — the world's first national fastener standard. It still exists today (you'll see it on old British motorcycles and equipment).

William Sellers — 1864 (The American Answer)

American engineer William Sellers proposed a simpler system for the US: a 60° thread angle (easier to manufacture than Whitworth's 55°), with flat crests and roots (easier to measure). The Franklin Institute adopted it in 1864 as the US Standard Thread — the ancestor of today's Unified National (UN) thread system used in all modern SAE fasteners.

How Threads Enabled the Industrial Revolution

The impact of standardized fasteners cascades through all of industrial history. Here's the chain:

Water Wheels → Belt Drives → Lathes

Early factories used water wheels. Power was transmitted by long leather belts (called "buffalo belts" — often made from buffalo hide) running through the ceiling of a factory, with pulleys dropping power to individual machines. The lathe — a machine that spins material and cuts it — became the critical tool for making precision cylindrical parts.

Lathes → Precision → Repeatability

A lathe could cut precise threads. A machine with a precise leadscrew could cut threads on another leadscrew. Precision bootstrapped itself — each precise machine could make more precise machines. This is called "scraping and lapping" up a standard — you can create a more accurate reference than any of your tools, using only those tools, through a process of comparison and correction.

Precision → Standardized Parts → Steam Engine

James Watt's steam engine was only possible because of precise cylinder boring (done by John Wilkinson's boring machine). That boring machine used precision leadscrew technology. The pistons, valves, and fittings required standardized fasteners. Without threads and precision machining, the steam engine was physically impossible to build reliably.

The Big Picture

Standardized threads → interchangeable parts → factory assembly lines → mass production → the modern world. Every FRC robot you build is downstream of this chain. The reason you can order an M5×0.8 bolt from any supplier and know it will fit any M5 nut is because of Whitworth in 1841 and the standardization movement he started.

Modern Standards Today's systems: ISO Metric (global standard, 60°), Unified National — UNC/UNF (USA/Canada/UK, 60°), and legacy systems like Whitworth (55°), ACME, and pipe threads. FIRST Robotics primarily uses metric and Unified National fasteners.

THREAD FORMS

The "thread form" is the cross-sectional profile shape of the thread. Different forms exist for different purposes — power transmission, sealing, strength, or ease of manufacture.

Unified National (UN) / ISO Metric — 60°

The most common thread form in the world. 60° included angle, with flat (or slightly rounded) crests and roots. UN is the SAE standard (UNC coarse, UNF fine). ISO Metric uses the identical 60° geometry but specifies pitch in mm. These two forms are geometrically the same shape — just different sizes and pitching systems.

★ What 99% of your FRC bolts use

Whitworth (BSW / BSF) — 55°

55° included angle, with rounded crests and roots. The original Whitworth standard. Slightly stronger root due to rounding. You'll encounter this on vintage British equipment, some motorcycles, and old tools. BSW = British Standard Whitworth (coarse). BSF = British Standard Fine. Not interchangeable with UN or ISO threads even if they appear close in size.

ACME — 29°

29° included angle, wide flat top and bottom. Designed for power transmission — lead screws, vises, jacks, anything converting rotation to linear motion. The wide, flat profile handles high axial loads better than 60° threads. Easier to engage under load. You'll see ACME on drill press quills, milling machine leadscrews, and large clamps.

Trapezoidal (ISO TR) — 30°

The metric equivalent of ACME. 30° included angle. Used in machine tool leadscrews and linear actuators in metric countries. Same concept as ACME — optimized for power transmission, not clamping.

Buttress — 45°/0°

Asymmetric thread: one face is 45°, the other is nearly vertical (7° or 0°). Designed to handle very high loads in one direction only. The steep face carries the load; the shallow face is just for retraction. Used in gun breeches, hydraulic cylinders, and quick-release mechanisms where force is always in one direction.

Square Thread — 0°

Perfectly flat, square thread faces — no angle at all. The most efficient thread for power transmission (no radial force component), but very difficult and expensive to cut. Cannot be tightened with a standard die easily. Used in high-precision linear stages and old machine tools. Rarely encountered in modern practice.

NPT — National Pipe Taper — 60°

Tapered thread — the diameter increases along the thread, creating a wedge seal when tightened. Designed to seal, not clamp. The interference of the taper creates a pressure-tight joint. Requires Teflon tape (PTFE tape) or pipe dope to fully seal. 1° 47' taper per side. Used in all pneumatic plumbing, hydraulics, and gas lines. Common on FRC pneumatic systems.

Not interchangeable with structural threads

UNR / UNJ — Rounded Roots

Variants of the UN standard with rounded roots instead of flat. This reduces stress concentration at the thread root, improving fatigue life significantly. UNJ is the aerospace standard (MIL-spec). You likely won't buy these at a hardware store — they're for high-cycle, safety-critical applications. Good to know they exist when reading aerospace specs.

For FRC: Only UN/ISO and NPT Matter Day-to-Day UN/ISO for all structural fasteners. NPT for pneumatic plumbing. ACME if you're building a lead-screw linear actuator. Everything else is educational context.

THREAD ANATOMY & TERMS

Thread cross-section — 60° UN/ISO form
TermDefinition & Why It Matters
Major Diameter The largest diameter — measured across the crests of an external thread (bolt OD). This is the "nominal" size. A ¼" bolt has a major diameter of ≈0.250". An M8 bolt has a major diameter of 8.0mm.
Minor Diameter The smallest diameter — measured at the roots of an external thread. Also called root diameter or core diameter. This is the cross-section that carries tensile load — the weakest point. This is approximately the tap drill size.
Pitch Diameter The imaginary cylinder where thread thickness = gap between threads. This is the "functional" diameter used for tolerance and fit calculations. Thread wires measure this. Critical for manufacturing — tolerance is specified here.
Pitch (metric) Distance in millimeters between adjacent thread crests. M8×1.25 has a 1.25mm pitch. Smaller pitch = finer thread.
TPI (imperial) Threads Per Inch — the reciprocal of pitch. ¼-20 has 20 threads per inch (pitch = 1/20" = 0.050"). Higher TPI = finer thread.
Lead Linear advance per full revolution. For single-start threads: lead = pitch. For multi-start threads: lead = pitch × number of starts. Multi-start threads advance faster per turn (used in pens, bottle caps, quick-release).
Flank Angle Half the included angle of the thread cross-section. 30° for UN/ISO (60° included). 27.5° for Whitworth. The angle determines how much radial force is created when axial load is applied.
Crest The peak (top) of the thread. Often slightly truncated — exact form matters for tolerance class.
Root The valley (bottom) of the thread. Where fatigue cracks initiate under cyclic loading. Rounded roots (UNR, UNJ) significantly improve fatigue life. This is why you don't want threads in the shear plane of a joint.
Hand Direction of advancement. RH (right-hand) = clockwise to tighten = standard. LH (left-hand) = counterclockwise to tighten. LH used where rotation would loosen a RH thread (left pedal on bicycle, some turnbuckles, propellers).
Tolerance Class How tight or loose the fit is. 2A/2B = standard fit (bolt/nut). 3A/3B = precision/close fit. 1A/1B = loose fit. For FRC, you never need to specify this — standard hardware is 2A/2B.
Coarse vs Fine Coarse (UNC/metric coarse): Fewer threads per inch, stronger and faster to assemble, more tolerant of damage. Default for most applications. Fine (UNF/metric fine): More threads, better vibration resistance, more torque for same wrench effort, easier to strip. Use fine where adjustment precision matters or in thin-walled sections.

GAUGE NUMBERING #0 – #14

Why Numbers Instead of Fractions?

For screws smaller than ¼", imperial sizing uses a gauge number system inherited from wire gauge standards. The formula is simple:

Diameter (inches) = 0.060 + (N × 0.013) Where N = the gauge number #0 → 0.060 + (0×0.013) = 0.060" #1 → 0.060 + (1×0.013) = 0.073" #2 → 0.060 + (2×0.013) = 0.086" ← smallest common machine screw #4 → 0.060 + (4×0.013) = 0.112" #6 → 0.060 + (6×0.013) = 0.138" #8 → 0.060 + (8×0.013) = 0.164" #10 → 0.060 + (10×0.013) = 0.190" ← most common in FRC #12 → 0.060 + (12×0.013) = 0.216" Then jumps to ¼" (0.250") for fractional sizes

Once you memorize this formula, you can work out any gauge size in your head. The key ones for FRC are #10 (0.190") and #6 (0.138").

Measuring an Unknown Thread — Field Methods

Thread Pitch Gauge

Fan of blades, each with a thread profile. Drop into thread until one sits flush. Reads in TPI or mm pitch. Get a combo set. Fast and accurate for field identification.

Calipers (Quick ID)

Measure major diameter → look up in table. Count threads in 1" (or over 10mm for metric). Not high precision but identifies the fastener for replacement.

Known Nut Test

Try threading a nut you know. If it runs free and smooth: match. If it binds immediately: wrong size. If it starts then seizes: STOP — cross-threading (especially Metric vs SAE confusion).

Metric vs SAE Confusion

#10-32 (0.190") and M5×0.8 (5mm=0.197") are very close. They will appear to engage and then seize. Never force. Use a pitch gauge on anything that "almost fits."

SCREW vs BOLT vs STUD

The Technical Definitions

TermProper Definition
Bolt A fastener inserted through clearance holes in all mating parts and secured with a nut. The bolt itself does not thread into any material — only the nut does. The joint is clamped between head and nut.
Screw A fastener that threads into a hole in one of the mating parts (tapped hole, wood, plastic, etc.). The screw itself creates or engages threads in the receiving material. May or may not have a nut.
Stud Threaded on both ends (or all over), no head. One end threads permanently into a part; a nut goes on the other. Allows removing and replacing a component without unscrewing a bolt from a deep hole. Common in engines, suspension, and anywhere bolt-head clearance is unavailable.
Set Screw / Grub Screw A screw with no head — threaded its entire length. Sits fully recessed when installed. Used to clamp one part against another (hub on shaft). Typically hex socket drive. Grub screw = British term for the same thing.
Machine Screw A fully-machined, precision-threaded fastener intended for use with a tapped hole or a nut. Uniform thread from head to (near) tip. The workhorse of mechanical assembly.
Everyday Reality In a robotics shop, people call everything a "bolt" or "screw" interchangeably. What matters practically: Does it need a nut? (bolt behavior) or does it thread into the part? (screw behavior). The terminology matters when reading documentation, ordering hardware, or taking a machining class.

Names That Break the Rules

Many common fastener names are technically wrong but universally used. You'll need to know both the name and the reality:

NameWhat It Really Is
Lag Bolt Actually a screw — it threads into wood, never uses a nut. Called a bolt because it has a hex head driven with a wrench like a bolt. Technically: Lag Screw.
Carriage Bolt A true bolt (goes through, uses a nut), but has an unusual head — round top with a square neck that bites into wood to prevent spinning. Cannot be driven from the head side.
Eye Bolt Can be either — some thread into material (screw behavior), some use a nut. Context determines.
J-Bolt / U-Bolt Curved bolts that embed in concrete or wrap around pipe. True bolts (use nuts).
Hex Bolt vs Cap Screw A hex bolt typically has a washer face and is used with a nut. A cap screw has tighter tolerances and is typically threaded into a tapped hole. Often used interchangeably in practice.
Machine Screw vs Bolt Machine screws are technically screws (thread into tapped holes), but they're constantly used with nuts (bolt behavior). ASME draws the line at ¼" — above ¼" diameter with a hex head = bolt; below = screw.
The Practical FRC Rule Use the term your mentor, supplier, or documentation uses. Understand the underlying behavior (needs nut vs threads into part) and you'll always order the right hardware regardless of what it's called.

CUTTING THREADS

Threads can be cut three ways: single-point on a lathe, thread milling on a CNC, or with tap and die. Each has tradeoffs in speed, precision, flexibility, and equipment required.

Single-Point Threading (Lathe)

The most fundamental method — one tool with a ground tip cuts the thread profile in multiple passes. Slow, requires skill, but can produce any thread form, any size, any pitch, including left-hand, multi-start, and custom forms.

Setup

  • Use a 60° fishtail (center) gauge to align the threading tool perpendicular to the workpiece axis
  • Set the compound rest to 29.5° (for 60° threads) — you'll feed with the compound, not the cross-slide. This cuts on one face at a time, reducing chatter and giving better finish
  • Set the quick-change gearbox for the desired TPI (or configure the change gears on older lathes)
  • Turn the OD to the major diameter first

Procedure

  • Take a scratch pass — very light, count thread crests to verify TPI
  • Each pass: feed in with compound (not crossslide), engage half-nut at same mark on thread dial
  • Thread dial tells you when to re-engage for even-numbered TPI (any line), odd TPI (same line only), fractional TPI (must not disengage)
  • Take ~0.005" per pass near finish, lighter as you approach final depth
  • Check fit with a nut or thread ring gauge
  • Final passes: spring cuts (same depth, just removes spring and deflection)
Threading Depth Formula For 60° UN threads: thread depth ≈ 0.6495 ÷ TPI. For M threads: depth ≈ 0.6495 × pitch (mm). This is the theoretical full depth — in practice stop about 0.010"–0.015" short and fit-check.

Thread Milling (CNC)

A rotating thread-mill cutter traces a helical path around the bore or OD. The spindle rotates the cutter; simultaneously the X/Y axes trace a circle and the Z axis advances one pitch per revolution.

Types of Thread Mills

  • Single-form thread mill: One thread profile. Helical interpolation produces any length thread. Most flexible — one cutter makes many sizes.
  • Full-form thread mill: Full-length of threads, one pass per thread zone. Faster but less flexible.
  • Combined drill/thread mill: Drills and threads in one tool. Fast for production.

Advantages over Tapping

  • No risk of tap breakage in hard materials or blind holes
  • Can produce left-hand threads with the same cutter
  • Can produce partial threads at entry/exit
  • Better chip evacuation (no chip packing in blind holes)
  • One cutter = many thread sizes

Rigid Tapping (CNC)

The spindle and Z-axis are synchronized — as the spindle turns, Z advances at exactly 1/TPI (or pitch mm) per revolution. Much faster than thread milling, equivalent quality to hand-tapping. Modern CNC machines do this automatically. The tap must be rigid — no floating chuck.

FRC Relevance If your team has a CNC mill or router, rigid tapping is the fastest way to put many threaded holes in aluminum plate. Thread milling is useful for large holes (riv-nut size and above) where taps are expensive.

Tap and Die (Hand Threading)

The most common method in any shop — including your FRC pit. Simple, cheap, effective.

Tap = cuts internal threads (in a hole). Die = cuts external threads (on a rod or shaft).

TAPPING GUIDE & DRILL CHARTS

The Process — Step by Step

  1. Select the tap drill size — use a Starrett chart, Machinery's Handbook, or the formula below. Drill too small = tap breaks. Too large = weak thread engagement.
  2. Drill the hole — use a sharp bit at the correct speed. Dull bits wander and make oversized holes. Use cutting fluid on aluminum and steel.
  3. Deburr — chamfer the top of the hole slightly with a larger drill bit or countersink. This starts the tap square and gives threads a clean entry.
  4. For blind holes — drill deeper than needed (roughly 3× the tap pitch extra) to give the tap room to clear chips before bottoming out.
  5. Apply cutting fluid — Tap Magic, WD-40 (aluminum), or Kool Mist work well. Always use something — dry tapping in steel will break your tap.
  6. Start the tap square — sight from two perpendicular directions. A tap started at an angle makes a crooked hole and will break.
  7. Two forward, half back — rotate 360–720° forward, then 180° back to break the chip. Repeat. In aluminum you can go further; in steel, be more conservative.
  8. Feel for resistance — increasing resistance = chip buildup. Back off, clear chips, add fluid, continue. Never force a tap. They are made of hardened HSS or carbide and are extremely brittle.
  9. For blind holes — switch from plug tap to bottoming tap to thread the last few threads near the bottom.
  10. Clear chips and inspect — flush with compressed air, check depth with a known bolt.

The Drill Press Fixture Trick

Starting a tap perfectly perpendicular is the hardest part of hand tapping. A drill press can be your fixture even if you don't run the motor.

Setup
  1. Drill your tap hole in the drill press as normal — this ensures it's perpendicular to the work surface.
  2. Without moving the workpiece, swap the drill bit for a center punch (or a dead center, or any blunt point) in the chuck. Remove the drill bit carefully and do not move the workpiece.
  3. Lower the quill until the center punch just contacts the top of the tap (seated in the tap wrench, placed in the hole).
  4. The quill spring provides downward pressure; the column keeps everything aligned 90° to the table.
  5. Turn the tap wrench by hand while the quill maintains pressure and alignment.
  6. Once the tap is 2–3 threads deep and self-guiding, you can remove from the drill press and finish by hand.
Alternative Methods (1) Commercial tap guides — a machined bushing that holds the tap square. (2) Drill press with belt removed — use the quill for pressure and alignment, rotate the chuck by hand. (3) Tap extension / T-handle with a built-in guide bushing.

Reference Resources

Starrett Tap Drill Chart

L.S. Starrett Company publishes free drill and tap charts covering every common thread size, recommended drill size, and clearance drill size. Keep one printed in your pit. Available at starrett.com and from any tool supplier. Also posted in most machine shops.

Machinery's Handbook

The machinist's bible. Has complete thread data, tolerance tables, material properties, torque specs, and virtually everything else. 30th edition is current. Heavy, expensive, and worth owning. Your school machine shop almost certainly has a copy.

Thread % Engagement Tradeoff

75% thread engagement is the standard target — it captures 95%+ of maximum thread strength. 100% engagement is harder to tap and increases tap breakage risk by ~3×, while adding only 5% strength. In aluminum and 3D prints especially, aim for 75% — and use helicoils or heatserts for better pullout strength in soft materials.

Broken Tap Recovery Broken taps are hard. Options: (1) EDM (spark erosion) removes it — requires a machine shop. (2) Tap extractors — work sometimes in large taps. (3) Drill it out with a carbide bit oversize and use a helicoil insert. (4) Move the hole. Prevention: never force, always use cutting fluid, back off regularly.

3D Print Hole Sizing — The ±½ Pitch Rule

3D printers consistently produce holes smaller than designed due to material shrinkage and bead geometry. A rule that works well for metric threads:

Clearance/thru hole (bolt passes through freely): Design diameter = nominal bolt diameter + (0.5 × pitch) Example: M5×0.8 → design hole at 5 + 0.4 = 5.4mm (prints to approximately 5.0–5.1mm = nice sliding fit) Interference fit / light press fit (no threads): Design diameter = nominal bolt diameter − (0.5 × pitch) Example: M5×0.8 → design hole at 5 − 0.4 = 4.6mm (prints undersized, bolt self-taps or press-fits) For tapping (drill-press-tap after printing): Use the standard tap drill diameter from Section 8. Then actually tap it — PLA threads are weak, use heatserts instead. Note: Always print a test coupon first — your specific printer/material/settings will shift these numbers.
Best Practice for 3D Print Threads Always use heatserts (heat-set brass inserts) for any structural screw in a 3D print. Self-tapped plastic threads strip in one match under load. Heatserts convert the joint to metal-on-metal and can be reused many times.

HEAD STYLES — WHEN TO USE EACH

The head style determines how the fastener is driven, how much bearing area it has, whether it sits flush, and what clearance it needs. Choosing the right head style is as important as choosing the right grade.

Socket Head Cap Screw (SHCS)

Cylindrical head with a hex (Allen) socket. The FRC default. High torque capacity, no cam-out, compact head diameter. Typically Grade 12.9 / Grade 8 strength. Head is taller than button head but has more drive engagement. Use wherever high torque and precise fit matter.

★ Use this by default for structure

Button Head Cap Screw (BHCS)

Low-profile dome-shaped head, hex socket drive. Wider bearing surface than SHCS for its diameter, but less torque capacity — the shallow socket can strip if overtightened. Use where head height clearance is limited, on panels and covers, or where a cleaner look is wanted. Don't use where high torque is required.

Flat / Countersunk Head (FHCS)

Conical underhead surface that seats in a countersunk hole — sits flush with or below the surface. 82° angle (US standard) — match your countersink cutter. Use on panels where flush surface is required, on guides and rails where a protruding head would catch, and on any sliding interface. Requires a countersink operation in the material.

Pan Head

Slightly rounded top with a flat bearing surface underneath — the "standard" machine screw head profile. Wider bearing area than SHCS for same diameter. Less torque than socket head. Common drive: Phillips, slotted, or Torx. Used in general electronics, enclosures, and any non-structural application where appearance and wide bearing matter.

Truss Head

Extra-wide, low-profile head — even wider than pan. Distributes clamping load over a very large area. Useful for thin, soft, or fragile materials (sheet plastic, thin aluminum, gaskets) where a standard head would punch through or pull through. Also looks clean on exposed panels.

Round Head

Classic domed head — the old-style machine screw shape before pan head became standard. Less common now but still found on older equipment and some specialty hardware. Similar bearing area to pan head.

Hex Head Bolt (HHB)

Six-sided external hex head — driven by wrench. Standard structural bolt. Available in Grade 2/5/8. Large bearing area. Easy to torque accurately with a socket or wrench. The head sticks up significantly — may need clearance. This is the bolt you see in bridge and building construction. Use in FRC for structural connections where you have wrench access.

Hex Washer Head

Hex head with an integrated washer flange below the hex. Larger bearing area, self-contained (no separate washer needed). Very common in sheet metal screws and self-drilling screws. Good for thin sheet metal where a regular hex head might pull through.

Hex Flange Head (Flange Bolt)

A standard hex bolt head with a wide circular flange below it. The flange acts as a built-in washer — spreads load, sits flat, and prevents loosening better than a plain hex head. Common in automotive (cam cover bolts, intake bolts). Serrated flange versions also grip and resist rotation.

Carriage Bolt Head

Smooth rounded dome on top, small square neck below. The square neck bites into wood (or aluminum plate) when the nut is tightened, preventing rotation — meaning you can tighten the nut without needing to hold the bolt head. No drive feature on the head. Cannot be driven or removed from the head side without destroying it. Used for wood-to-metal connections, handrails, and anywhere a smooth, anti-tamper head is wanted.

Oval / Raised Countersunk Head

Like a flat head but with a slightly domed top — sits flush at the base but has a decorative dome above. Used for aesthetics on covers, handles, and trim pieces where a fully flush surface isn't required but a flat head would look too industrial.

Fillister Head

Deep cylindrical head with a flat top and narrow OD — fits into recesses and counterbores. Less common, but used when you need a deep drive recess and a small head OD. Often used in electrical equipment.

FRC Head Style Decision Tree Structural bolt (tapped or nut) → SHCS. Need flush with surface → FHCS + countersink. Space-constrained head height → BHCS. Wrench tightening preferred → Hex Head Bolt. Wood connection → Carriage Bolt or Lag Screw. Electronics/panel → Pan Head or SHCS.

DRIVE TYPES

Hex Socket / Allen (Internal Hex)

Six-sided recess driven by hex keys or hex bit drivers. Best torque transfer, no cam-out, excellent engagement. Available in SAE (1/16" to ¾") and metric (1.5mm to 17mm+). The standard for SHCS, button head, and set screws. Get a full set of both SAE and metric hex keys — you'll use them constantly.

★ FRC primary driver

External Hex (Hex Head)

Standard bolt head with 6 flat sides driven by open-end, box, or socket wrench. Most structural hex bolts. Size = AF (across flats). ¼-20 bolt typically has 7/16" AF head. ⅜-16 has 9/16" AF. Metric M8 has 13mm AF, M10 has 17mm AF. High torque possible with a breaker bar or torque wrench.

★ FRC structural hardware

Torx / Star (T-Drive)

Six-lobe star pattern. Higher torque than Phillips with zero cam-out. Used in automotive, deck screws, structural wood screws, and electronics. T10, T15, T20, T25, T27, T30, T40 are common. T25 covers most structural wood screws (GRK, Spax). Increasingly common in FRC for sheet metal screws.

Excellent — use for sheet metal and wood

External Torx (E-Torx)

Torx star shape on the outside of the fastener. Used where an internal recess isn't possible. E6, E8, E10, E12, E14, E16, E18, E20 are common sizes. Found in automotive suspension, some specialty bolts, and aircraft hardware. Requires E-Torx sockets (not interchangeable with standard Torx).

Phillips (#0–#4)

Cross-shaped drive intentionally designed to cam out at a set torque — originally to prevent overtightening on assembly lines. This is a feature that became a bug in repair contexts. #2 is the most common. Very low torque before cam-out, damages quickly. Avoid for structural robotics fasteners. Use only for electronics panels and other low-torque applications.

Designed to strip — avoid structural use

Slotted (Flat)

Single slot. Oldest drive type. Terrible torque, easy to slip. Still found on terminal screws, electrical boxes, and legacy equipment. Almost never a good choice for new construction. A flat-head screwdriver in a slotted screw under high torque will slip and damage surrounding material.

Avoid for anything new

Robertson (Square Drive)

Square recess — common in Canadian woodworking and some sheet metal screws. No cam-out, better than Phillips. R0 (orange), R1 (yellow), R2 (green), R3 (black). If you see a square hole in a screw head, this is it. Also found on some deck screws sold at US retailers.

12-Point / Double-Hex

Two overlapping hex patterns = 12 contact points. Only 30° of clearance needed to engage (vs 60° for standard hex). Used in high-torque aerospace and automotive fasteners where wrench swing is limited. Requires special sockets — standard 6-point sockets will work but may not fully engage.

Combination Heads

Some screws have two drive types: Phillips + slot, or Phillips + square. Useful when you're not sure which driver someone will have. Common in electrical panels, HVAC equipment, and budget hardware. Not a deliberate engineering choice — more of a manufacturing hedge.

WASHERS — Types & Why

Washers are often skipped or treated as optional. They're not. Each type solves a specific problem.

Flat Washer (Plain Washer)

Why it exists: (1) Distributes bolt head load over a larger area — prevents the bolt head from pulling through or crushing soft material. (2) Provides a smooth, hardened bearing surface for the nut to torque against — important because rough surfaces waste torque as friction. (3) Bridges oversized clearance holes. (4) Protects surface finish from wrench marks. Always use one under the nut and one under the bolt head on structural joints.

Sizes: Inner diameter (ID) ≈ bolt diameter + small clearance. Outer diameter (OD) determines bearing area.

★ Use on almost every structural bolt

Fender Washer

A flat washer with an extra-large OD relative to the ID. Used when the material is very thin, soft, or has an oversized hole — the large OD prevents pullthrough. Named for automotive fender mounting. Very useful for mounting components to thin sheet metal, polycarbonate panels, or anywhere you need maximum bearing area.

Split Lock Washer

A ring cut and offset — looks like a spring coil cut open. Intended to bite into both the bolt head and the surface to resist rotation. In reality, these are largely ineffective — research (and NASA studies) shows they flatten immediately under proper torque and add little retention. A nylock nut or Loctite is vastly more effective. Still useful as a spacer or to provide a small spring load in delicate electronics. Don't rely on these for vibration resistance.

Less effective than widely believed

Nord-Lock Washer

Two-piece washer system: two washers with radial teeth on the outside and cammed wedge surfaces on the inside. When the bolt tries to rotate loose, the wedge action actually increases clamping force. One of the most effective anti-loosening systems available, short of safety wire. Used in mining, rail, wind turbines, and critical machinery. More expensive than standard washers but worth it for critical FRC joints. The pair must remain together — they're useless separated.

Excellent vibration resistance

Belleville Washer (Disc Spring)

A conical washer that acts as a spring under compression. Maintains clamping load even when the joint relaxes slightly (thermal cycling, creep, etc.). Used in high-temperature applications, joints that cycle thermally, and precision assemblies where constant preload matters. Can be stacked to increase or vary spring rate. Used in aerospace, automotive valvetrain, and precision instruments.

Wave Washer

Corrugated in a wave pattern around the circumference. Acts as a spring — provides a specific spring force when compressed. Used to maintain bearing preload, take up axial play in shafts, and provide light clamping. Not for high-load structural joints — the wave collapses at high torque.

Serrated / Toothed Lock Washer

Internal or external teeth that bite into both surfaces. Better than split lock washers but still not as good as nylock or Loctite. Internal teeth (bite only the bolt head) are less damaging to the surface. External teeth are more aggressive. Used in electronics where conductivity through the joint is important (the teeth cut through anodizing or paint for ground connections).

Finishing Washer (Countersink Washer)

A small countersink washer used with flat-head (countersunk) screws in wood or thin materials. The washer's conical recess receives the screw head, distributing load and creating a clean, finished look. Common in furniture and cabinetry. Adds a professional look to exposed countersunk fasteners.

Reducing/Shoulder Washer

Has a cylindrical shoulder (boss) that fits into the hole, providing exact centering of the bolt. Used in precision assemblies and insulating bushings (the shoulder is often plastic, electrically isolating the bolt from the sheet). Important in electronics where bolt-to-chassis contact would cause a short circuit.

Washer Material Note Standard washers are low-carbon steel. Hardened washers (Grade 8 equivalent) should be used with Grade 8 bolts — a soft washer will crush and lose preload. Stainless washers for stainless bolts. Nylon or plastic washers for electrical isolation. Never mix metals that will galvanically corrode (aluminum washer on steel bolt in a salt environment = bad).

NUTS, INSERTS & CAPTIVES

Hex Nut

Standard nut. Six-sided. Match grade to your bolt — a Grade 2 nut on a Grade 8 bolt fails at the nut first. Grade 5 nuts = 3 lines on face. Grade 8 = 6 lines.

Nylock Nut (Nylon Insert Lock)

Nylon ring deforms around threads, creating friction. Effective vibration resistance. Replace after every removal — nylon is permanently deformed and loses retention. Cannot be used above ~250°F (nylon melts). Single use recommended; 3–5 uses before replacement in non-critical applications. ★ FRC default

All-Metal Lock Nut (Top Lock)

Metal-only lock nut — top is mechanically distorted to grip the bolt. Works at high temperatures (no nylon). Used in automotive exhaust, turbo systems, anywhere >250°F. Can be reused more times than nylock but still has finite life.

Flange Nut / Serrated Flange

Built-in washer flange. Serrated version bites into surface and resists rotation. Self-contained — no separate washer needed. Very useful for sheet metal. ★ Good FRC choice for sheet metal

Jam Nut

Thin nut tightened against a standard nut to lock both in place. Also used to lock a threaded rod end at a specific position (turnbuckle, rod end bearing, threaded adjuster). Requires two wrenches to install properly.

Coupling Nut

Long hex nut for joining two threaded rods end-to-end. Used for adjustable-length struts, extending all-thread, and antenna mounts. Available in Grade 2 and 5.

Castle Nut + Cotter Pin

Slotted top accepts a cotter pin through a drilled hole in the bolt — mechanical positive lock. Cannot back out without removing the pin. Used in safety-critical applications: steering, suspension, brake caliper pins. More effort to install but the gold standard for critical rotating joints.

T-Nut (Wood Type)

Pronged nut driven into wood from one face — prongs bite in when bolt is tightened from the other side. Used in workbench fixture plates, climbing holds, and jig tables. Allows a bolt to be threaded in from the outside with no access to the nut side.

T-Slot Nut (Extrusion Type)

Slides into the T-slot of aluminum extrusion (80/20, Misumi, Bosch Rexnord). Drop in from the end or roll into the slot. Rotate 90° to lock. Common in FRC for adjustable mounting on aluminum extrusion frames. Use a low-profile version for narrow slots.

Riv-Nut (Rivet Nut / Nutsert)

Blind threaded insert — installed with a rivet-nut tool (mandrel pulls, body collapses, locks to backside). Creates a permanent threaded hole in sheet metal, hollow tube, or thin plate with one-sided access only. Available in ¼-20, #10-32, M5, M6 etc. Requires correct hole diameter. ★ Critical FRC tool

Helicoil (Thread Insert)

Coiled wire insert in a specially-tapped hole. Expands when bolt engages. Stronger than the parent material (stainless coil in aluminum or plastic). Repairs stripped threads and strengthens tapped holes in soft materials. Requires a special oversize tap + installer tool. Repair tool for stripped holes

Heatsert (Heat-Set Insert)

Brass knurled insert melted into a 3D-printed part with a soldering iron. Provides metal-on-metal threads in plastic. Essential for any structural screw in FRC 3D prints. Iron temp: ~180–200°C (PLA), ~220–240°C (PETG/ABS). Push slowly and straight — don't wiggle. ★ Required for 3D print structure

Weld Nut / Clinch (PEM) Nut

Permanently attached by welding (weld nut) or press-staking (clinch/PEM). Very clean installation. PEM nuts require a press or arbor press — the knurled shank is pressed into a precisely sized hole and displaces material. Common in production sheet metal assemblies, electronics enclosures, and robot frames where riv-nuts aren't preferred.

Captive / Cage Nut

Nut captured in a stamped spring-steel cage that snaps into a square hole — common in rack-mount server equipment and some fabricated frames. Installs with a finger snap; bolt from the other side. Allows nuts to be installed in spaces you can't reach with a wrench later.

TORQUE, PRELOAD & BOLT STRETCHING

The Physics of Bolt Preload

A bolt doesn't hold things together by magic — it holds them together by stretching. When you torque a bolt, you're converting the rotation of your wrench into tensile load (stretch) in the bolt shank. The bolt acts like a very stiff spring, and the spring force is what clamps the joint.

The Torque → Preload Chain
  1. You apply torque T to the bolt head with a wrench.
  2. ~90% of that torque overcomes friction (under the head and in the threads). Only ~10% actually goes to stretching the bolt.
  3. The bolt stretches elastically (like a spring) — this is preload or clamp force.
  4. The preload compresses the joint. The joint is now in compression; the bolt is in tension.
  5. External loads must first overcome the preload before they can separate the joint. Properly preloaded joints almost never fail — bolts that are finger-tight fail constantly.
Torque-Preload Relationship: T = K × d × F T = applied torque (lb·in or N·m) K = nut factor / friction coefficient K ≈ 0.20 for dry steel-on-steel K ≈ 0.15 for lubricated (oil or anti-seize) K ≈ 0.12 for Teflon / molybdenum grease d = bolt nominal diameter (in or m) F = clamp force / preload (lb or N) Target preload ≈ 75% of Proof Load (Proof Load ≈ 85% of Yield Strength)
Friction Dominates Because K (friction) is ~0.20, a 30% change in lubrication condition causes a 30% change in actual clamp force for the same torque. This is why you should always lubricate or always run dry — don't mix. Anti-seize or oil dramatically reduces friction and required torque.

Bolt Stretching — The Engineering Details

Bolts are designed to operate in their elastic region — stretch under load, return to original length when unloaded. This is the fundamental mechanism that makes them work.

Elastic Range = Safe Operating Zone
  • Under proof load: Bolt behaves perfectly elastically. Torque it, it stretches. Remove torque, it returns. Can reuse many times.
  • At yield strength: Permanent deformation begins. The bolt is now shorter at rest than it was — it has relaxed. Clamp force is permanently reduced.
  • Torque-to-yield (TTY) bolts — intentionally tightened slightly past yield. One-time use. Very consistent clamp force. Used in automotive head bolts, main bearing caps. MUST be replaced if removed.
  • Fatigue: Even below yield, cyclic loading eventually causes crack initiation at thread roots. This is why aerospace bolts have a finite life in service.

Field Torque Checks — When No Wrench Is Available

You won't always have a torque wrench at competition. Some practical techniques:

Calibrated Feel
  • Snug: The bolt compresses the joint — no movement, but no significant torque beyond that. Starting point.
  • Firm: ~50% of spec torque. Takes conscious effort. Joint is definitively clamped.
  • Tight: ~75–80% of spec. Takes real effort. Recommended minimum for non-critical FRC fasteners.
  • Full torque: At spec. Requires a torque wrench to know for certain. Develop a feel by practicing with a wrench — tighten by feel, then check with the wrench.
  • Paint stripe method: Apply stripe at correct torque. Inspect stripe at competition — moved stripe = loose bolt. This is faster than re-torquing everything between matches.

PREVENTING LOOSENING & REUSE LIMITS

Loctite Threadlocker

Purple (222) — Low Strength

For screws #4 and smaller where blue would make removal very difficult. Small electronics, sensor screws, adjustment screws. Removable with hand tools.

Blue (243) — Medium Strength ★

FRC default. Removable with hand tools. Resists vibration well. Sets in 10 min, full cure 24 hr. One drop on threads — it wicks by capillary action. Works on clean, oil-free metal. ★ Use on all structural fasteners

Red (271) — High Strength

Permanent. Requires heat (250°F+, heat gun) to remove. Use only for permanently locked studs, press-fit inserts, things you truly never want to disassemble. FRC use: set screws on critical hubs, press-fit motor pinions.

Heat required to remove

Green (290) — Wicking Grade

Very low viscosity — wicks into assembled joints by capillary action. Apply after assembly when you can't disassemble to add blue. Medium strength. Good for field repairs where disassembly isn't possible.

Loctite Doesn't Replace Torque Always torque to spec first. Loctite is the backup, not the primary retention method. Also: doesn't cure on stainless without primer. Doesn't work on plastics (can craze/crack). Doesn't work on oily or dirty threads.

Mechanical Methods

Nylock Nut

Nylon insert grips threads. Effective. Finite life. ★ FRC standard

Nord-Lock Washer

Wedge action — loosening increases clamping force. Best mechanical retention. More expensive.

Safety Wire

Wire through drilled bolt heads — any rotation tightens the wire. Aerospace gold standard. Requires pre-drilled bolts and safety wire pliers. Wire routing: loosening direction tightens the wire.

Paint / Torque Stripe

Line across bolt head and surface after final torque. Visual indicator of any movement. Fast to inspect between matches. ★ Do this at build season end

Split Lock Washer

Common but largely ineffective — flattens before providing meaningful resistance. Better than nothing, not better than nylock. NASA and military studies confirm limited effectiveness.

Castle Nut + Cotter Pin

Positive mechanical lock. Cannot back off without removing the pin. Safety-critical pivots and wheel bearings.

Every fastener has a finite life. Knowing when to replace is as important as proper installation.

RIVETS

Blind (Pop) Rivets

Two-part: body (rivet) + mandrel (pulling pin). Installed with a rivet gun from one side only. The mandrel is pulled, expanding the rivet body against the far surface, then the mandrel snaps. Name comes from the early squeezing sound ("pop").

Rivet Hole Prep Correct hole size is critical. Too small: rivet can't seat. Too large: rivet doesn't grip. Use a drill bit 0.003–0.005" over the rivet diameter. Deburr both sides. Clamp parts tightly together before riveting — the rivet doesn't pull parts together like a bolt; it just locks them in position.

Solid Rivets

A solid metal cylinder inserted through aligned holes. A bucking bar supports the tail while the opposite end is hammered or squeezed to form a shop head. Both-sided access required. The gold standard for aircraft and heavy structural work.

Hot rivets = heated before installation (traditional steel construction — the rivet contracts as it cools, creating enormous clamping force). Modern aircraft use cold aluminum solid rivets.

FIELD CHECKS & PIT PROTOCOL

Between-Match Inspection System — 2 Minutes

Step 1 — Visual Stripe Scan (30 sec) Scan all paint stripes on critical fasteners. Broken or offset stripe = that bolt has moved. Mark any suspect bolt for immediate attention. One person can scan the entire robot this way while the other is connecting the battery.
Step 2 — Touch-Torque Check (60 sec) Designated person goes through P1 and P2 fasteners with a hex key or wrench. Snug any that move even slightly. If a nylock spins on with no resistance — the nylock is dead, replace it. Carry a bag of spare nylocks in your pit kit at all times.
Step 3 — Visual Damage Check (30 sec) Any cracked structure? Bent members? Missing fasteners? Report to mechanical lead immediately. A missing bolt usually means it's somewhere inside your robot — find it before it shorts electronics.

WOOD FASTENERS

Wood Screws — Design & Selection

Wood screws differ from machine screws in four key ways: (1) tapered/pointed tip for self-starting, (2) coarser pitch for wood fiber engagement, (3) partial threading (see below), and (4) variable root diameter (threads thicken from tip to shank — creates a wedge action for pullout resistance).

Why Partial Threading?

The Pull-Down Effect The smooth shank passes freely through the top board — no threads engage it. The threads only bite in the bottom board (the "receiver"). As you drive the screw, the threads advance faster than the shank, pulling the top board tight to the bottom. If the screw were fully threaded, the top board would lift. This is identical to a clamp. Always choose screw length so the smooth shank spans the top board, with all threads in the receiver piece.

Nails in Robotics — The Glue-Up Trick

Brad Nail as Clamp Substitute

When building field elements, game tables, or wooden practice field components:

  1. Apply wood glue to joint surfaces
  2. Align parts precisely
  3. Shoot 2–3 brad nails (18 gauge finish nailer) to register the parts and hold position
  4. Wipe squeeze-out immediately
  5. Let cure — the glue provides all structural strength
  6. The nails are just temporary clamps (that you leave in)

This technique allows fast assembly without needing a dozen clamps. Wood glue in a good joint is stronger than the wood itself.

FASTENERS IN CAD

Onshape

Hole Feature

The Hole feature generates counterbored, countersunk, clearance, and tap holes automatically from a standard size selection. Always use this rather than manually creating hole geometry — it's faster and produces correct tolerances. The feature also annotates the drawing automatically.

Fastener Library (FeatureScript)

Onshape has a built-in standard part library and many community FeatureScripts that insert parametric fastener models. The Fastened Mate in assemblies automatically mates a bolt to a hole. Use the McMaster-Carr integration to drag real part numbers directly into your assembly — the geometry is exact and the part number is captured for ordering.

Thread Representation

In CAD, threads are almost never modeled as actual helical geometry — it's too slow and adds no manufacturing value. Instead: model the minor diameter cylinder (what the hole looks like), add a cosmetic thread annotation. For 3D printing: model actual geometry if you're printing self-tapping threads.

Fusion 360

Thread Feature

Fusion's Thread feature adds cosmetic or physical (modeled) threads to any cylindrical face. For 3D printing, use physical threads. For CNC and manufacturing documentation, use cosmetic threads — they generate proper thread callouts in drawings.

McMaster-Carr Integration

Direct McMaster-Carr import — search by part number or description, drag the 3D model into your assembly. The model includes exact geometry and the part number saves to your BOM automatically. Same workflow works in Onshape. Get in the habit of using real hardware in assemblies — interference checks and clearance verification depend on it.

Design for Manufacturability

Always check: Can I get a wrench in there? Is there enough clearance for the bolt head? Can the nut be installed? Does the counterbore depth clear the bolt head? Model the actual tool paths mentally — a perfectly designed joint on screen is useless if you can't tighten it in the real world.

General CAD Best Practices

Model Clearance Holes Correctly

Always model your clearance holes at the correct clearance diameter (not the bolt diameter). A ¼-20 bolt goes in a 0.266" hole — not a 0.250" hole. This matters for DXF export to a waterjet or laser, and for interference checking. Tap holes should be at minor diameter (the unthreaded hole diameter).

Counterbore and Countersink

Model these features explicitly. In drawings they generate proper GD&T annotations. In assemblies they confirm the bolt head clears adjacent geometry. The counterbore depth should be the head height + 0.010–0.020" clearance. The countersink angle must match the screw (82° US, 90° DIN — verify before modeling).

Fastener Checklist Before Sending to Build
  • Every hole has a correct type and size
  • Every bolt head has wrench clearance
  • Every nut has installation access
  • Partially threaded bolts have correct grip length
  • Grade and material specified for critical fasteners
  • Loctite or nylock noted on vibration-critical joints
  • All fasteners are real McMaster/supplier part numbers
