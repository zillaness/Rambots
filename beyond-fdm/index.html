How SLA / Resin Printing Works

SLA (Stereolithography Apparatus) was the first commercial 3D printing technology, patented by Chuck Hull in 1984 and commercialized by 3D Systems. All resin printing variants share a core principle: UV light cures liquid photopolymer resin layer by layer. The differences are in how that UV light is delivered.

Why Orientation Matters So Much in Resin
In FDM, orientation primarily affects strength. In SLA/MSLA, orientation affects: support quantity (more supports = more surface marks), suction cup forces (flat faces peeling off the FEP can delaminate prints), print time, and layer line visibility. Most parts print best at 30–45° angles to the build plate — this distributes suction force across multiple layers and minimizes support contact on visible surfaces.

Two-Photon Polymerization (2PP / Nanoprinting)

The highest-resolution additive manufacturing process available. An ultrafast pulsed laser (femtosecond pulses) cures photopolymer resin via two-photon absorption — a nonlinear quantum process that only occurs at the precise laser focal point, where photon density is high enough for two photons to be absorbed simultaneously. Because the curing reaction requires simultaneous absorption of two photons rather than one, it only occurs at the very center of the focused beam — far below the diffraction limit that constrains conventional SLA.

Technical Specifications
Resolution: Features in the hundreds of nanometers — sub-micron routinely
Build volume: Typically millimeter-scale (a few cubic mm)
Speed: Very slow — scanning at nanometer steps
Cost: Research-grade ($100K–$500K+ systems)
Machines: Nanoscribe (Photonic GT², Quantum X), UpNano, Multiphoton Optics
Applications
• Micro-optics: lenses, gratings, waveguides for integrated photonics
• MEMS and microfluidic devices
• Biomedical: vascularized tissue scaffolds with sub-100 µm channels
• Metamaterials with engineered optical/acoustic properties
• Drug delivery nanocarriers
• Photonic crystal structures

Computed Axial Lithography (CAL) — Volumetric Printing

Developed by Hayden Taylor et al. at UC Berkeley in 2019, CAL turns conventional layer-by-layer thinking completely on its head. Instead of building one cross-section at a time, a rotating vial of photosensitive resin is illuminated from multiple angles by a continuously updated projected image sequence — similar to a CT scan running in reverse. Each 2D projection contributes a dose of light; where the accumulated dose from all angles exceeds a curing threshold, the resin solidifies. The entire 3D object emerges from the vat simultaneously, not sequentially.

No Layers — True Volumetric
Because the entire object cures simultaneously rather than sequentially, there are genuinely no layer lines, no staircase artifacts, and no anisotropy. Parts are fully isotropic. Print times can be under 60 seconds for small objects. The Berkeley team famously printed a small replica of Rodin's The Thinker in roughly 30 seconds.
No Supports Needed
The surrounding uncured resin provides support — there's no peeling force, no build plate adhesion issue, no layer-by-layer stress. Any geometry printable within the vial geometry is achievable without supports.
Print Around Pre-Existing Objects
CAL's most unique capability: the resin vial can contain a pre-existing object (a metal rod, an electronic component, a biological sample, a fiber optic strand) and the printed structure forms around it, embedding it within. This enables optical connections printed around fiber ends, electronics encapsulated without potting compound, or hybrid bio-synthetic constructs.
Commercial Status
Technology transfer has produced early commercial systems through Readily3D (Switzerland). Current build volume: ~cm³. Primarily research and specialized manufacturing. Expected to expand as photopolymer formulation and projection hardware matures.

SLA Resins — Complete Guide

Resin selection is the most nuanced aspect of SLA/MSLA printing. Unlike FDM where "material" is a well-understood polymer, photopolymer resins are complex chemical formulations — photoinitiators, monomers, oligomers, and additives — and performance varies enormously between brands and grades.

Standard / General Purpose Resins

Standard / Regular Resin
The default. Relatively brittle but inexpensive. Good detail. Suitable for display models, miniatures, prototypes where toughness isn't required. Wide color range. Most printer profiles are tuned for standard resin.
Tensile: ~60–80 MPa · Elongation: <5% · Brittle
ABS-Like / Tough Resin
Formulated for reduced brittleness — better impact resistance and some flexibility compared to standard. Popular for functional prototypes, snap fits, hinges. Formlabs Tough 2000 and Tough 1500 are benchmark products. Accuracy may be slightly reduced due to rubber-toughening agents.
Tensile: ~50–70 MPa · Elongation: 10–20% · Impact resistant
Flexible / Elastic Resin
Rubber-like photopolymers. Shore 50–80A typical. Used for gaskets, grip surfaces, wearable prototypes, stop/bumper applications. Tends to have limited tear resistance — thin sections can tear on support removal. Formlabs Flexible 80A and Elastic 50A are reference products.
Shore 50A–80A · Elongation: 50–160% · Tear resistant
Rigid / Stiff Resin
Maximum stiffness photopolymers, often glass-fiber or silica-filled to increase modulus. Formlabs Rigid 4000 and 10K are benchmarks. 10K refers to 10,000 MPa flexural modulus (similar to glass-filled thermoplastics). Used for jigs, fixtures, housings requiring dimensional stability.
Flexural modulus: 4,000–10,000 MPa · Very low elongation

Engineering Resins

High-Temperature Resin
Formulated for heat deflection temperature (HDT) up to 200°C+. Requires full post-cure to achieve maximum thermal properties. Formlabs High Temp resin HDT: 289°C at 0.45 MPa load. Used for: mold tooling for low-run injection, testing in heated environments, under-hood automotive checks.
HDT: 100–289°C · Brittle · Full post-cure required
Durable Resin
Polyethylene-like properties — waxy feel, extreme impact resistance, high elongation. Designed for assemblies with sliding, press-fit, or snap-fit features. Formlabs Durable: 48 MPa tensile but very high elongation (55%). The closest resin to PE/PP functional behavior. Difficult to post-finish due to waxy surface.
Tensile: ~48 MPa · Elongation: 55% · PE-like feel
Castable / Burn-out Resin
Formulated for lost-wax/lost-plastic investment casting. Burns out cleanly in a burnout oven (~1,000°C) without ash residue that would contaminate the casting. Critical for jewelry and dental crowns. Formlabs Castable Wax, Asiga CastPure. Post-curing schedule critical for clean burnout. High detail — captures gemstone settings at micron level.
Near-zero ash residue · Fine detail · Used with investment plaster
Dental Resins
FDA-cleared / CE-marked formulations for dental applications. Categories: model resin (arch models, study models), surgical guide resin (implant placement), crown & bridge, denture base, temporary crown. Each is a separate regulatory product — not interchangeable. Formlabs is the dominant dental resin brand; Asiga and NextDent also significant.
Biocompatible · Regulatory cleared · Requires validated workflow
Biocompatible (Medical) Resin
ISO 10993 or USP Class VI tested for skin/mucous membrane contact. Not the same as dental resin — covers surgical templates, hearing aid shells, custom patient-contact devices. Short-term contact only for most grades. Formlabs Surgical Guide resin and BioMed series are category leaders.
ISO 10993 · USP Class VI · Requires post-cure protocol
Ceramic-Filled / Silica Resin
Resin loaded with silica or ceramic particles. When the green part is sintered (fired at 1,000°C+), the photopolymer burns away leaving a dense ceramic object. Formlabs Ceramic, Lithoz CerAM, ProtoFab. Applications: high-temperature components, chemical reaction vessels, custom ceramic parts. Fragile as green parts — handle carefully.
Post-sintering: true ceramic · 15–20% shrinkage · Brittle green state
Transparent / Clear Resin
Formulated for optical clarity after post-cure and polishing. Fresh off the printer: frosted. After wet sanding through 2000 grit + polish: near-optical clarity. Formlabs Clear resin enables transparent enclosures and optical components. Water-clear finish requires extensive surface finishing — sanding from 400 through 2000 grit + automotive plastic polish.
Post-polish clarity · Brittle · Yellows over time with UV exposure
Composites (Carbon-Fiber Filled)
Photopolymer resins loaded with chopped carbon fiber or graphite for increased stiffness and ESD (electrostatic dissipation) properties. Formlabs ESD (static-dissipative) resin for electronics manufacturing jigs. Higher stiffness; surface is matte black with distinctive fiber texture. Not the same structural advantage as Markforged continuous fiber.
ESD: 10⁷–10⁹ Ω surface resistivity · Stiff · Antistatic

Consumer vs Professional Resin Ecosystem

Open / Third-Party Resins

Most MSLA printers (Elegoo, Anycubic, Phrozen, Creality) use an open resin system — any 405nm photopolymer resin works if you dial in the exposure settings. The community has published thousands of exposure profiles for popular resins in slicer software (Chitubox, Lychee). Pricing: $20–60/liter for consumer resins; $80–200/liter for engineering grades.

Brands: Elegoo (ABS-Like, Water-Washable), Siraya Tech (engineering grades — Tenacious, Build, Sculpt), Monocure 3D, 3D Resin Solutions, Phrozen, Anycubic Tough / Standard.

Closed / Validated Systems (Formlabs)

Formlabs uses RFID-chipped cartridges and prints only with validated Formlabs resins on Form series printers. Each resin has a validated print profile maintained in PreForm slicer. This closed approach ensures repeatability, batch consistency, and regulatory compliance (dental/medical applications require validated workflows). Cost: $150–$300/liter. The trade-off is higher cost for manufacturer-guaranteed results.

Third-party "open mode" modifications for Formlabs printers exist in the community but void warranty and regulatory compliance.

Wax Jetting & Castable Processes — Solidscape and Jewelry/Dental

Solidscape (Material Jetting Wax) — Jewelry Since 1994
Solidscape is a single-purpose, jewelry- and dental-optimized material jetting system — two inkjet heads, one for build wax and one for a soluble support wax. The support dissolves in a warm bath in minutes, leaving a clean wax pattern.

Key specs: 5,000 DPI XY resolution · 25 µm layer thickness · ±0.001 inch accuracy · Near-zero thermal expansion in investment plaster (critical for preserving fine prong and filigree geometry during casting)

The wax formulation is specifically engineered for precious metal investment casting — gold and platinum, where even tiny geometric distortions during burnout ruin a fine piece. Trusted by jewelry trade for production casting. More expensive than SLA/DLP castable resin workflows but delivers the most consistent burnout of any printed pattern.
Castable Resin (SLA / DLP / MSLA)
Wax-loaded photopolymers (typically 20–40% wax filler by weight) designed for near-zero ash content and clean burnout in investment casting. Formlabs Castable Wax Resin, Phrozen Castable W40, Liqcreate Wax Castable, and others run on general-purpose printers at a fraction of Solidscape's machine cost.

Burnout protocol matters: Each resin-investment plaster pair requires validation. Different resins have different burnout temperature ramps and kiln hold times. An incorrect ramp can crack the investment or leave carbon residue that ruins the cast.

Applications: Fine jewelry (filigree, wire work, thin prongs), dental copings, crowns, RPD frameworks, full-contour patterns for press-ceramic — both industries are well-served by modern castable resins.

Dental note: Direct-print zirconia is increasingly replacing cast metal frameworks for full-arch prostheses, but cast metal RPDs and copings remain very much in production — keeping castable printing highly relevant in dental labs.

Low Force Stereolithography (LFS) — The Technology Differentiator

Traditional SLA/MSLA peels the cured layer from the FEP/PDMS release film after each exposure. This creates significant peel force that can tear thin features or cause layer delamination. Formlabs' patented LFS (Form 3/4 series) uses:

  • Flexible tank floor: The bottom of the resin tank flexes away from the cured layer — mechanically releasing the part rather than pulling it. Dramatically reduces peel force (by ~300× compared to Form 2 rigid tank).
  • Light processing unit (LPU): Moves back and forth under the tank exposing each layer — the light source moves rather than the galvanometer mirrors rotating. More uniform illumination.
  • Result: More reliable prints of thin features, flexible parts, and large flat cross-sections. Less stress on supports = fewer support marks on delicate surfaces.

PreForm — The Slicer

Formlabs' PreForm is free and remarkably polished. Key features:

  • One-click auto-orient: Algorithm analyzes part geometry and selects optimal orientation for minimum supports on visible surfaces + maximum build success probability
  • Auto-support generation: Density, touchpoint size, and internal supports all configurable
  • Material-specific profiles: Print parameters locked to validated material settings — removes human error
  • Remote printing: Send jobs directly to Form printers over network; monitor via Dashboard
  • Fleet management: Enterprise features for managing multiple printers

Resin Post-Processing

The Mandatory Wash + Cure Workflow

Unlike FDM where prints come off the bed ready to use (usually), all SLA/MSLA prints require post-processing before they can be handled safely or used functionally. Skipping this workflow leaves uncured resin on and potentially in the part — a health hazard and a print quality failure.

→

→
Remove supports
→

→

Washing Solvents

IPA (Isopropyl Alcohol) — 90%+
The standard. Dissolves uncured photopolymer effectively. Use 99% IPA — lower concentrations (70% "rubbing alcohol") contain water that can cause resin to cloud and leave residue. Flammable — no open flames nearby. IPA degrades as it absorbs resin — monitor color (turns yellow/amber when exhausted). Dispose as chemical waste; don't pour down drain.
TPM (Tripropylene Glycol Monomethyl Ether)
Non-flammable IPA alternative. Formlabs recommends for their Form Wash system. Lower VOC emission. Works well for most standard resins; slightly less effective on some engineering resins. Can be purchased in bulk. Longer lifespan than IPA before exhaustion. Safer to handle in lab environments.

Post-Curing — Why and How

Fresh-washed prints are only partially cured — the photopolymer conversion is incomplete. Full post-curing is critical for achieving the mechanical properties listed on the resin datasheet.

What Changes With Post-Cure
Uncured/undercured: soft, tacky, lower mechanical strength, potential skin sensitizer residue.
Fully post-cured: hardened to datasheet spec, stable, safe to handle, maximum mechanical properties.

Heat + UV > UV alone: Temperature during post-cure increases polymer chain mobility, allowing higher conversion rates. Formlabs Form Cure heats to 60–80°C during UV exposure — particularly important for High Temp resin (289°C HDT only achieved with specific heat+UV protocol). For general resins, a nail UV lamp works; for engineering resins, a heated station is preferred.

Support Removal

  • Before full cure: Supports easier to remove when resin is still slightly flexible — do it after wash, before final cure if possible.
  • After cure: Supports become brittle and snap off more cleanly but may leave more noticeable nubs.
  • Tools: Flush cutters (flush cutters, not wire cutters), dental picks, hobby knife. Work under magnification for fine features.
  • Support marks: Small nubs where supports contacted the surface. Sand with progressive grits (400 → 800 → 2000) for cosmetic surfaces.
  • Minimize by design: Orient to place supports on non-visible faces. Adjust touchpoint size (smaller = less mark, more failure risk). Internal hollow features often need drainage holes to release trapped uncured resin during wash.

Surface Finishing for Resin

SLA/MSLA prints have visible layer lines (step artifacts) on curved surfaces. Finishing options:

  • Wet sanding: 400 → 800 → 1000 → 2000 grit. Wet sanding prevents dust — important for resin (see safety section). Creates smooth surface.
  • Spray filler primer: Grey automotive primer fills small steps, creates paintable surface.
  • XTC-3D / UV resin brush-on: Thin layer of resin brushed on surface and cured — fills layer lines, creates glossy finish.
  • Transparent resin polishing: Wet sand through 2000 grit → automotive plastic polish → polishing compound → optically clear finish.
  • SLA layer lines vs FDM: SLA layer lines are much finer (0.025–0.1 mm vs. 0.1–0.3 mm FDM) — on curved vertical surfaces they appear as very fine rings, typically requiring 400-grit sanding to remove.

Resin Safety — Critical Information

Uncured Resin is a Sensitizer and Irritant
Liquid photopolymer resin (before and during printing) contains photoinitiators, monomers, and acrylates that are skin sensitizers. With repeated exposure, some people develop a severe allergic response that worsens with each contact — eventually becoming unable to work with resin at all. This sensitization is permanent and cumulative. Sensitization most commonly occurs through skin contact, not inhalation.

This is NOT the same as a one-time irritation — sensitization means your immune system permanently recognizes the compound as a threat.

Protection Hierarchy

🧤 Nitrile Gloves — MANDATORY
Wear nitrile gloves for every contact with liquid resin, freshly printed parts, IPA wash solution (contains dissolved resin), and the resin tank. Nitrile resists acrylate permeation; latex does not. Double-glove for extended work. Change gloves when torn or after contact with concentrated resin. Never touch your face with resin-contaminated gloves.
🥽 Eye Protection
Safety glasses or goggles during resin handling, support removal, and part finishing. Splash risk from resin removal, pouring, or IPA wash. Resin in eyes requires immediate medical attention.
💨 Ventilation
Resin printing emits VOCs and acrylate fumes — most noticeable when the enclosure is opened. Print in ventilated space (fume hood, window, or at minimum a room with air exchange). Activated carbon filtration recommended. Enclosed printers contain most fumes during printing; the main exposure is during part removal, pouring, and washing.
🏥 Fully Cured Parts Are Safe
Once properly washed and UV-cured, the resin is chemically stable and no longer a sensitizer. Fully cured resin objects are safe to handle without gloves. The hazard is in the liquid and partially cured states only. Children and pets should not contact liquid resin or unwashed parts.

Disposal

  • Liquid resin: Never pour down the drain or into trash. Cure it first — cure liquid resin in the sun or with a UV lamp until solid, then dispose as solid plastic waste (check local regulations).
  • IPA wash solution: Let IPA evaporate in sun (outdoors) — cures the dissolved resin, leaving solid residue. Or filter resin out and cure the resin solids. Exhausted IPA should not go down the drain.
  • Resin-soaked gloves and paper towels: Cure before disposal — spread flat in the sun or use a UV lamp. Uncured resin in landfill is a hazardous materials concern.
  • Empty resin bottles: Many areas accept cured-out bottles as plastics recycling.

Safe Working Practices Summary

  • Always glove up before opening the printer, touching the FEP, or handling parts fresh off the plate
  • Keep a UV flashlight at your workstation — use it to quickly cure any resin spills before cleaning
  • Water-washable resins are NOT safe to dump in water — they're just easier to wash off skin. Still sensitizers. Still require proper disposal.
  • Wear closed shoes — dropped resin bottle or IPA will soak through socks
  • If you develop itching, redness, or rash from resin exposure, stop and consult a doctor. The reaction typically worsens with re-exposure.

SLS Packing Density — The Economics
Because no supports are needed and powder surrounds parts, the build volume can be packed in 3D — parts stacked on top of and around each other. This "nesting" dramatically improves economics at service bureaus — the cost per part drops significantly when a full build is packed tightly vs. a single part in a large machine. This is why SLS is often more economical than it appears at first glance for production runs of medium-complexity parts.

The default SLS material. Good mechanical properties, low moisture absorption (better than PA6), slightly waxy surface finish. Formlabs Nylon 12 Powder, EOS PA2200. Derived from castor oil — more sustainable than PA12 (petroleum-based). Better impact resistance and elongation than PA12. Slightly lower stiffness. More expensive. Tensile: 50 MPa · Elongation: 30%+ · Biobased

Flexible powder bed fusion — like printing flexible parts without supports. Produces truly isotropic flexible parts with consistent Shore hardness throughout. Formlabs TPU 90A powder. Post-Processing SLS Parts

Depowdering
Breaking parts out of the powder cake and removing unfused powder. Automated depowdering stations (Formlabs Fuse Sift) use vibration and vacuum. Manual: brushes, compressed air. Internal channels need drainage holes for complete powder removal. Remaining powder can jam moving parts.
Bead Blasting
Media blasting with glass beads or plastic media removes surface powder residue and creates a more uniform surface finish. Standard step for professional SLS output. Smooths the naturally grainy/powdery SLS surface to a matte, consistent texture. Formlabs Fuse Blast automates this.
Dyeing
SLS PA12/PA11 parts can be dyed in near-boiling fabric dye baths — the porous surface absorbs color deeply and evenly. Common colors: black (most popular), navy, red. Black is especially popular as it eliminates the grey/beige raw SLS color and gives professional appearance. Requires sealing coat for color fastness.
Vapor Polishing / Tumbling
Chemical vapor polishing (solvent vapors) or mass finishing in tumbling media can reduce surface roughness further. Industrial options for production volumes. Smoothed SLS approaches injection-molded appearance. Some service bureaus (Shapeways, Xometry) offer these as finishing options.

Critical CFR Limitation — Anisotropy
Continuous fiber reinforcement is inherently anisotropic. Parts are exceptionally strong along the fiber paths and still relatively weak perpendicular to them (between layers, across fibers). Designing for CFR requires understanding load paths — the slicer's fiber routing must align with the primary stress directions, not just fill arbitrarily. Poor fiber routing can produce a part that's weaker in critical directions than a well-designed standard FDM part at the same weight.

Metal 3D Printing

Metal additive manufacturing produces fully dense metal parts with mechanical properties approaching or matching wrought/forged metal. Multiple process categories exist, each with different trade-offs in material options, resolution, cost, and required post-processing.

Process Categories

LPBF — Laser Powder Bed Fusion
Also called DMLS (Direct Metal Laser Sintering) or SLM (Selective Laser Melting) depending on vendor
A high-power laser (200–1000W) melts metal powder in an inert atmosphere (argon/nitrogen). Layer by layer, parts are built up from the powder bed. Support structures are required (metal, machined off post-build). The most common metal AM process. Achieves near-full density (99.5%+) and excellent mechanical properties.

Key vendors: EOS (M series), Trumpf (TruPrint), 3D Systems (DMP), GE Additive (Arcam/Concept Laser), SLM Solutions

EBM — Electron Beam Melting
Arcam (GE Additive) · Vacuum environment
Uses an electron beam instead of a laser to melt metal powder. Operates in high vacuum rather than inert gas. Pre-heats the entire powder bed to 700–1000°C — this dramatically reduces thermal stress and warping. No supports required for titanium builds (pre-heated powder acts as support). Slower than laser LPBF for most materials but preferred for titanium alloys in aerospace/medical. Parts exit at elevated temp and must slow-cool in the machine.

Post-Processing Metal AM Parts

Support Removal (LPBF)
Metal supports are sintered metal — machined off with EDM (electrical discharge machining), CNC milling, or manual grinding. Design to minimize supports: orient to build on supported faces, design chamfers instead of overhangs, use self-supporting angles (<45° from build plate).
HIP — Hot Isostatic Pressing
Parts placed in a high-pressure (100–200 MPa) inert gas environment at elevated temperature (1000°C+). Collapses internal voids and pores, increasing density to near 100%. Dramatically improves fatigue life. Standard for aerospace and medical metal AM parts. Adds cost ($200–2,000/batch) but essential for critical applications.
Stress Relief + Heat Treatment
LPBF creates significant residual stresses from rapid thermal cycling. Stress relief annealing (post-build, still on build plate) prevents distortion during support removal. Solution + aging for precipitation-hardening alloys achieves final mechanical properties.
Machining to Tolerance
Metal AM surfaces have Ra 6–25 µm as-built. Critical dimensions (bearing bores, sealing faces, thread starts) must be post-machined to tolerance. Design with machining allowance (0.5–1.0 mm extra) on critical surfaces. Most metal AM parts are "near-net-shape" — require finish machining for functional use.

WAAM — Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing

A welding arc melts metal wire feedstock to deposit material — essentially robotic MIG/TIG/plasma welding under CNC control. Deposition rates are many times higher than any powder-bed process (kilograms per hour), and equipment cost is far lower than LPBF or EBM. Enables printing of very large metal structures measured in meters rather than centimeters.

  • Scale: Up to several meters; the only practical AM method for large aerospace and marine structural components
  • Deposition rate: 1–10 kg/hr — orders of magnitude faster than powder-bed processes (~0.1–0.5 kg/hr)
  • Cost: Dramatically lower equipment cost than LPBF. A WAAM cell with a robotic welder and CNC positioner costs $50K–$500K vs. $500K–$3M for comparable output in LPBF
  • Materials: Steel, titanium, aluminum, Inconel, bronze — any weldable alloy
  • Surface finish: Rough as-deposited (Ra ~100–300 µm) — requires CNC machining to final dimension
  • Key players: Lincoln Electric, Cranfield University (UK), TWI, Gefertec, MELD Manufacturing

Applications

  • Aerospace titanium frames and brackets — Airbus has WAAM structural components in production
  • Propeller hubs and offshore hardware — where large-scale metal forms are needed
  • Large aluminum tooling structures — replacing multi-piece fabricated assemblies
  • Near-net-shape preforms for subsequent forging — reduces buy-to-fly ratio
  • Repair and cladding of worn large components

Variants

  • MIG-WAAM: Most common; high deposition rate; slightly lower quality
  • TIG-WAAM: Better control; lower deposition; better for aluminum
  • Plasma-WAAM: Very high deposition; coarser microstructure
  • Cold Metal Transfer (CMT): Low heat input; best microstructure control

The largest-scale metal AM process available. Sciaky's EBAM systems use an electron beam in a high-vacuum chamber to melt wire feedstock, building titanium and specialty-alloy parts up to 19 feet (5.8 meters) long.   • Scale: Up to 5.8 m × 1.2 m × 1.2 m build envelope
  • Deposition rate: Up to 9 kg/hr for titanium
  • Materials: Ti-6Al-4V, Inconel, tantalum, niobium, refractory metals
  • Primary use: Aerospace bulk structures — wing spars, fuselage frames, landing gear components — where large titanium forgings are the alternative
  • Economics: Replaces expensive, long-lead titanium forgings. A 20 kg EBAM titanium part can replace a 200 kg billet forging with 90% material waste

Ultrasonic Additive Manufacturing (UAM) — Fabrisonic

UAM combines ultrasonic metal welding with CNC milling in a unique hybrid process. Metal foil layers are ultrasonically welded together under pressure using a rotating sonotrode — the ultrasonic vibration scrubs oxide layers and creates solid-state atomic bonds without melting the metal. Between weld layers, a CNC mill cuts precise features. The key result: fully dense, high-strength metal parts built at near room temperature.

Why Low Temperature Changes Everything
Because UAM never melts metal (it's a solid-state process), it uniquely enables:

Embedded electronics: Sensors, fiber optic cables, thermoelectric elements, heating circuits, and shape-memory alloy actuators can be placed between foil layers and welded over — the low process temperature doesn't damage electronics. This creates "smart structures" with sensors embedded in the metal itself.

Dissimilar metal bonding: Aluminum-titanium, copper-steel, and other combinations that are metallurgically incompatible in fusion welding can be ultrasonically bonded.

Material: Primarily aluminum alloys (3003, 6061), but also copper, stainless steel, titanium · Use cases: aerospace smart structures, heat exchangers with embedded flow channels, RF shielding with embedded antennas

PolyJet & Multi-Material Jetting

PolyJet (Stratasys) and Connex (predecessor) jet liquid photopolymer droplets from inkjet-style print heads, then cure each layer with UV. The key advantage: multiple materials can be jetted simultaneously, enabling functionally graded parts with varying Shore hardness, color, and material properties in a single print.

Ultra-High Resolution
PolyJet achieves ±0.02–0.05 mm accuracy and layer heights of 16–30 µm — finer than SLA. The finest surface finish available in additive manufacturing for general-purpose use. Details invisible to FDM or SLS are printable. Used for: watch components, hearing aid shells, injection mold patterns, micro-fluidic devices.
Multi-Material in One Build
Multiple print heads jet different materials simultaneously. Shore 85A rigid + Shore 40A flexible in the same part — gradients possible. True overmolded assemblies printed as one piece. Stratasys J55, J826, J850 support up to 7 materials simultaneously including colored photopolymers and elastomers.
Full-Color Parts
Stratasys J826 / J850 Plus print full CMYK color photopolymers — 500,000+ achievable colors. Texture maps from 3D CAD software can be applied to printed surfaces. Output looks like painted/molded parts. Used for: product visualization, anatomical models for surgical planning, design review prototypes.
Support Material
PolyJet uses a wax-like support material that melts or dissolves in water/sodium hydroxide solution (WaterJet or dishwasher method). Enables complex internal geometries and overhangs without visible support marks — the cleanest support removal of any FDM-adjacent process.

PolyJet Limitations

  • Cost: Machine cost $30K–$300K; material costs high ($200–500/kg)
  • Mechanical properties limited: Photopolymers don't match injection-molded thermoplastics in fatigue, UV stability, or long-term durability
  • UV aging: Standard PolyJet materials yellow and embrittle with UV exposure — not for outdoor or high-UV applications
  • High-temp parts: Limited to ~60–80°C maximum service temp for most resins
  • Best use: High-fidelity visual and tactile prototypes, medical models, custom consumer products for photography/presentation

NanoParticle Jetting (NPJ) — XJet

XJet's NanoParticle Jetting process jets metal or ceramic nanoparticles suspended in a wax support matrix, simultaneously depositing both build material and soluble wax support. After printing, the wax support dissolves, and the green part is sintered in a furnace to produce a dense metal or ceramic component — without any powder bed, laser, or loose powder handling.

Key Properties
• Very fine feature resolution — comparable to PolyJet for polymers
• Wax support dissolves cleanly — no manual support removal for complex internal geometry
• Materials: Zirconia (dental, industrial ceramics), alumina, stainless steel (in development)
• Surface finish: Very smooth for a sintered ceramic — rivals traditionally processed ceramics
• Cost: Industrial ($200K–$500K range)
Primary Application: Zirconia Dental
Zirconia (ZrO₂) is the material of choice for modern dental crowns and bridges — strong, biocompatible, tooth-colored. Design for Additive Manufacturing (DfAM)

Traditional design-for-manufacturing (DFM) minimizes complexity to reduce machining time. DfAM flips this — additive manufacturing rewards complexity. Complex geometry that would be impossible to machine is free in 3D printing. Understanding these principles enables step-change design improvements.

Topology Optimization

A computational method that removes material from areas of low stress while preserving structural paths. Input: load cases, boundary conditions, volume fraction target. Output: organic, bone-like structure that uses minimum material for required stiffness.

  • Tools: Altair OptiStruct, ANSYS Topology Optimization, Fusion 360 Generative Design, nTopology
  • Result: Parts 30–70% lighter than conventional designs at equivalent stiffness
  • Best processes: Metal LPBF (most complex geometries printable); SLS/MJF (no supports); SLA (for polymer topology-optimized parts)
  • Post-processing note: Topology-optimized parts have complex internal features — ensure all trapped powder/support can be removed

Lattice Structures

Replacing solid infill with engineered lattice geometry — not just a print setting but a designed structural element. Benefits:

  • Weight reduction: 40–80% lighter than solid with tunable stiffness
  • Energy absorption: lattice structures crush predictably — crash structures, protective padding
  • Thermal: high surface area lattices for heat exchangers, conformal cooling channels in tooling
  • Gradient lattice: density varies spatially — denser near stress concentrations, sparse in low-stress regions
  • Adidas Futurecraft 4D (Carbon CLIP), Nike Flyprint (HP MJF TPU) — both use lattice midsoles designed for specific athletic performance

Consolidation of Assemblies

Multiple parts assembled together can often be consolidated into a single printed part. Examples:

  • Hydraulic manifold: traditionally 10 blocks + machined channels + seals → 1 printed manifold with internal channels, no seals, no leakage risk
  • Heat exchanger: traditional welded construction → single printed part with complex internal flow channels impossible to machine
  • Robotic joint: 5-piece assembly → 1 printed part with living hinges, snap features, and embedded bearing races
  • Design rule: If an assembly has features that exist only to enable manufacturing (fasteners, alignment pins, seams), consider whether those can be eliminated by redesigning as a single AM part

Conformal Cooling Channels

In injection mold tooling printed via LPBF metal, cooling channels can follow the mold cavity surface exactly — impossible with drilled straight channels. Conformal cooling reduces cycle times 20–60% and improves part quality by eliminating hot spots. This application alone has justified metal AM investment for many injection molding companies.

Internal Geometry Freedom

AM enables features physically impossible with subtractive manufacturing:

  • Internal lattices in sealed parts
  • Impossible internal channels (S-curves, spirals, branches)
  • Captured hardware (nuts, bearings printed around, not inserted)
  • Living hinges and snap fits in rigid materials
  • Gradient materials (PolyJet): transition from rigid to flexible within one part
DfAM First Principles
1) Complexity is free — add features that improve function without cost penalty
2) Minimize supports — orient and design to eliminate overhangs and trapped powder zones
3) Hollow when appropriate — thin shells with internal ribs often stronger per unit weight than solid
4) Consolidate assemblies — every joint is a failure point; eliminate joints
5) Design for the process — each AM process has unique design rules; learn them for your chosen process
6) Post-processing is part of the design — account for support removal, HIP, machining allowance from the start

Lattice Structures & Why AM Makes Them Possible

Lattice structures are engineered, repeating geometric networks that fill volume with a predictable, tunable architecture — not random foam or solid material, but a precise mathematical arrangement of struts, surfaces, or cells. They are one of the most powerful tools in additive manufacturing design, enabling properties that solid material simply cannot achieve: controlled anisotropy, gradient stiffness, designed crush behavior, and extreme surface area within a bounded volume.

Why Lattice Structures Are Nearly Impossible Without Additive Manufacturing
Before AM, producing internal lattice geometry required extraordinary effort and expense — and still couldn't approach the complexity achievable with a 3D printer:

Investment casting with sacrificial cores: Ceramic cores define internal voids. For simple passages — yes. For a periodic 3D lattice with hundreds of interconnected nodes — the cores become impossible to remove after casting, and too fragile to survive the pour. Limited to very simple internal geometry.

Wire EDM + stacking: CNC cutting thin lattice layers then bonding them — 50–200 layer operations for a simple lattice block. Each layer must be cut, inspected, aligned, and diffusion bonded. Cost: $10,000–$100,000 per part. Geometry: strictly 2D-extruded, no true 3D node connectivity.

Textile/weaving methods: Woven metal mesh, braided fiber preforms — produce simple repeating 2D+layer lattices. Limited to specific geometries, can't produce arbitrary node topology, and are fundamentally 2D sheets stacked rather than true 3D printed lattices.

Foaming: Closed-cell aluminum foam (Alporas), open-cell ceramic foam — random stochastic structure, no control over cell geometry, size, or orientation. Can't produce gradient density or designed anisotropy.

With AM (LPBF, SLS, SLA): Any mathematically definable lattice at any scale, density, orientation, and gradient — printed directly in one operation with near-full-density struts and no assembly. The complexity is free.

Lattice Taxonomy — Three Families

Strut-Based (Beam) Lattices
The most intuitive type: a network of beams (struts) connected at nodes, like a 3D truss. Each unit cell is defined by strut positions and connectivity. Familiar geometries:

BCC (Body-Centered Cubic): Struts from corners to center — good shear resistance
FCC (Face-Centered Cubic): Struts across faces — higher stiffness
Octet Truss: Face + edge + vertex struts — very high stiffness-to-weight (Maxwell stable)
Kelvin cell: Space-filling truncated octahedra — acoustics and thermal

Properties are highly direction-dependent (anisotropic) based on strut orientation. Fail by strut buckling at high compression.
TPMS — Triply Periodic Minimal Surfaces
Mathematically defined surfaces with zero mean curvature that tile all of 3D space with no self-intersection. The minimal curvature distributes stress extremely evenly — no stress concentrations at nodes. Key types:

Gyroid: S(x,y,z) = sin(x)cos(y) + sin(y)cos(z) + sin(z)cos(x) = 0 (the FDM infill you already know)
Schwartz P (Primitive): cos(x) + cos(y) + cos(z) = 0 — cube-face-centered; high stiffness
Schwartz D (Diamond): sin(x)sin(y)sin(z) + sin(x)cos(y)cos(z) + ... = 0 — high surface area; excellent for bone scaffolds
Neovius: Ultra high surface area variant — thermal and filtration

TPMS are near-isotropic — same properties in all directions. No stress concentration nodes — smoother load paths than strut lattices. Dominant in medical implant and heat exchanger applications.
Stochastic / Voronoi Lattices
Random or semi-random cell geometry — like printed foam. Voronoi cell walls, randomized strut lengths, or simulated natural structures (trabecular bone, wood grain).

Properties: Isotropic (random direction = no preferred axis), visually organic, can mimic natural structures for biomimetic applications.

Advantage: No periodic stress concentrations from repeating geometry — periodic lattices can develop resonance failure modes at their natural frequency; stochastic lattices avoid this.

Generate with: Voronoi algorithms, Meshmixer (Make Pattern), nTopology, or procedural tools in Blender/Grasshopper. Typically used for medical bone scaffolds and artistic designs.

The Math Behind TPMS — Why Zero Mean Curvature Matters

A surface has zero mean curvature when the principal curvatures κ₁ and κ₂ at every point satisfy κ₁ + κ₂ = 0. This means for every concave direction there's an equal convex direction — the surface is "as flat as possible" in an average sense. For engineering structures this has profound consequences:

  • Even stress distribution: No stress concentration at corners or nodes — load travels smoothly along the curved surface. TPMS structures can outperform strut lattices in fatigue by 2–5× at equivalent density.
  • Both sides are continuous: A TPMS divides space into two interlocking continuous channels — one is solid, one is void. In heat exchangers, two different fluids can flow through the two channels with maximum contact area and no mixing. In bone scaffolds, one channel conducts bone tissue and the other conducts vasculature.
  • Closed-cell vs. open-cell: TPMS can be printed "sheet" style (walls with thickness = solid shell) or "skeleton" style (volumetric fill) depending on the application — the same surface geometry serves both.

Medical Implants — Osseointegration and Stress Shielding

The bone ingrowth application deserves special attention. When a solid titanium implant is placed in bone, its stiffness (~110 GPa) is far higher than cortical bone (~15–25 GPa). The implant "shields" the surrounding bone from load — bone responds by resorbing (the Wolff's Law principle: bone remodels to match mechanical load). This leads to long-term implant loosening as surrounding bone thins.

A lattice Ti implant at 60–70% porosity has an effective stiffness of ~5–20 GPa — much closer to bone. Additionally:

  • Pore sizes of 300–900 µm allow bone cells (osteoblasts) to grow into the structure — the implant and bone mechanically interlock at the microscale
  • The Schwartz D TPMS is particularly favored: its continuous channels allow simultaneous bone ingrowth and vascular supply (both channels are independently continuous)
  • Surface roughness of as-built LPBF (Ra ~15–25 µm) promotes cell adhesion better than polished surfaces — as-built is often preferred over smoothed for the biological interface
  • EBM (electron beam melting) is preferred over LPBF for CoCr orthopedic implants — the pre-heated powder bed reduces residual stress in the thin struts of the lattice

Gradient Lattices — Variable Density

One of AM's most powerful capabilities: lattice density, cell size, and geometry can vary continuously through a part. This enables:

  • Density gradient: Denser lattice (smaller cells, thicker struts) near high-stress zones; sparser lattice in low-stress regions. Reduces weight without compromising structural integrity. Requires FEA to guide the gradient.
  • Stiffness gradient: Progressively softer from structural core to surface — used in joint implants to avoid a sharp stiffness transition at the bone interface
  • Functional gradient: Rigid base + compliant lattice top surface (running shoe midsole). The transition is smooth — no discrete interface that could delaminate
  • Thermal gradient: Finer lattice near heat source (higher surface area for heat transfer), coarser away from it — used in conformal cooling and heat sink design

Practical Manufacturing Considerations

Powder/Resin Removal from Lattice Interiors

Internal lattice structures trap powder (SLS/LPBF) or uncured resin (SLA). Design rules:

  • LPBF metal: Every enclosed cell needs a path to the outside for powder removal. Design drainage holes (≥2–3 mm) at dependent low points. Orientation matters — build the lattice so powder falls out in the gravity direction. Some complex lattices require EDM or drill-through to create escape paths after build.
  • SLS polymer: PA12 powder is easier to remove than metal. Compressed air and vibration can clear most lattice cells. Minimum interconnect diameter: 1.5 mm for reliable depowdering.
  • SLA/MSLA: Uncured resin trapped in closed cells will never fully cure and will degrade over time. Always design drainage holes or orient so resin drains during printing. Wash solvents (IPA) must reach all internal surfaces.

Fatigue in Metal Lattice Struts

Thin LPBF struts have lower fatigue life than bulk material due to surface roughness and residual stress. HIP (hot isostatic pressing) is critical for any cyclically loaded metal lattice — it closes pores in thin struts and relieves residual stress, improving fatigue life 3–10×. Surface finishing is impractical for internal lattices — HIP is essentially the only post-process available.

The Summary Case for Lattices in AM
Lattice structures enable a fundamentally different class of engineering design — material that is tuned, not just shaped. Where traditional manufacturing gives you a choice of materials and then shapes them, AM with lattice structures lets you design the effective material properties themselves: stiffness, density, thermal conductivity, crush behavior, and acoustic response can all be engineered spatially within a single printed part. No joining, no assembly, no compromise from manufacturability constraints. This is the deepest expression of what additive manufacturing uniquely enables.

One of the most powerful intersections of additive manufacturing and traditional manufacturing: using 3D printed tooling as injection mold inserts to produce real thermoplastic parts — weeks faster and orders of magnitude cheaper than traditional machined steel tooling. This "bridge tooling" approach lets engineers test actual injection-molded material properties, gate locations, draft angles, and part geometry before committing $15,000–$80,000 to a production steel tool.

A production injection mold in P20 steel takes 6–14 weeks and costs $10,000–$80,000+ depending on complexity. Design errors discovered after steel tooling is cut require expensive modifications (welding, re-machining) or scrapping the tool entirely.

A 3D printed mold can be produced in 1–3 days for $50–500, allowing real injection-molded part samples in actual production material before steel commitment. Design iterations cost $50 each instead of $5,000 each.

Design Rules for 3D Printed Molds

Draft Angles — More Generous Than Steel

Steel molds often use 0.5–1° draft. Printed molds have more friction and slightly rougher surfaces even after polishing. Use 3–5° draft minimum on all walls. Aggressive draft reduces ejection force and extends mold life dramatically. If your part design allows, design for 5° and the printed mold will last far longer.

Wall Thickness in the Mold

Printed mold bodies need adequate wall thickness to withstand injection pressure (typically 5,000–20,000 PSI). Minimum:

  • Resin mold walls: 8–12 mm minimum around cavity
  • Thin sections over 15mm tall: add external ribs or metal backing frame
  • Corners: generous radii — sharp corners are stress concentration points that crack under injection pressure

Gate Location and Size

Place gates at thick sections to fill thin sections, minimizing injection pressure. Tab gates or edge gates work well in printed molds. Avoid direct pin gates on thin-walled parts — the local stress concentration cracks resin molds. Gate diameter: 0.8–1.5 mm for most parts. Larger gate = lower injection pressure = longer mold life.

Ejection Strategy

Standard steel molds use ejector pins actuated by the press. For prototype printed molds, options include:

  • Manual ejection: Open mold, pry part out with small tool. Only works for simple geometry with generous draft.
  • Printed ejector pin holes: Print through-holes at 1–3 mm diameter; push pins through manually. Requires more draft and careful placement.
  • Spring-loaded pins: Machine ejector pins into the printed mold — works if the mold is thick enough to accommodate pin channels.
  • Air ejection: Compressed air through a drilled channel — surprisingly effective for simple parts.

Venting

Trapped air creates burn marks, short shots, and void defects. In steel molds, fine vents are machined into the parting line (0.02–0.05 mm deep). In printed molds, the slightly porous or textured surface often provides natural venting — but add 0.05 mm parting line vents on the far end from the gate as insurance.

Surface Orientation

Orient cavity surfaces parallel to the build platform (horizontal) for the smoothest surface finish — vertical surfaces have visible layer lines. Best practice: print the entire mold insert at an angle that puts the part cavity faces as close to horizontal as possible.

Mold Release — Critical for Longevity

Without mold release, even polypropylene will bond to or abrade the resin mold surface. Mold release extends shot count dramatically:

  • Ease Release 200 (SmoothOn): Silicone-based aerosol. Standard choice for resin and FDM molds. Apply thin coat, allow to dry 60 seconds, shoot.
  • Mold Wax (Johnson Paste Wax): Traditional approach. Apply and buff. Creates a sacrificial layer. Re-apply every 3–5 shots.
  • PTFE spray: Good for polypropylene and polyethylene — these materials naturally want to stick to everything. PTFE spray creates near-zero friction surface.
  • Frequency: Apply before every shot for first 10 shots; every 3–5 shots thereafter as the mold surface "breaks in".

Reducing Injection Pressure for Printed Molds
Standard injection molding runs at 10,000–30,000 PSI. Resin molds crack at these pressures. For bridge tooling, lower injection pressure and temperature to the minimum that fills the part:

• Use very slow injection speed (reduces pressure spike at fill)
• Reduce hold pressure to minimum needed
• Eliminate pack pressure if possible (accept slightly undersized parts for prototype purposes)
• Shorter cooling time is OK — the mold won't have thermal channels anyway

Bridge Tooling Economics — When It Makes Sense
Use 3D printed tooling when:
• Part design is not yet finalized — expect geometry changes
• Need 10–500 parts in actual production material for testing, sales samples, or regulatory submission
• Part complexity is low-to-medium (simple cores, no complex side actions)
• Timeline is critical — 3 days vs 8 weeks

Transition to steel when:
• Design is frozen after prototype validation
• Volume exceeds 1,000 parts (steel economics improve rapidly above this)
• Material requires temperatures/pressures beyond resin capability (PC, PPS, glass-filled nylons)
• Surface finish requirements exceed what sanded resin can achieve (textured/grained surfaces)

Consider metal AM tooling when:
• Complex cooling or undercut geometry needed
• Volume is medium (10,000–100,000 shots) and conformal cooling ROI is justified
• Repair of existing tools economically preferable to new conventional tooling

Vacuum Forming & Thermoforming — 3D Printed Tooling

3D printed parts make excellent low-cost tooling for sheet plastic forming. Where a machined aluminum vacuum forming tool costs $500–$5,000 and takes days to produce, a printed buck can be ready in hours for under $50. This makes 3D printing a natural fit for short-run thermoformed packaging, custom enclosures, product development samples, and hobbyist sheet-forming projects.

Thermoforming vs. Vacuum Forming — The Distinction
Vacuum forming: Sheet plastic is clamped over a buck (positive mold). Heat softens the sheet, then vacuum pulls it tightly against the buck surface. One good surface — the inside (facing the buck). The outside retains the plastic sheet's original finish.

Drape forming: Simplified vacuum forming without a machine — heat the sheet with a heat gun until soft and pliable, then manually drape/press it over the buck. Lower fidelity but accessible with no specialized equipment.

Compression thermoforming: Heat sheet plastic until soft, then press between a positive AND negative mold — both surfaces are formed. Both faces of the sheet take on the mold geometry. Requires matching positive/negative pair.

3D Prints as Vacuum Forming Bucks

FDM Bucks — The Porosity Advantage

FDM prints have a significant hidden advantage as vacuum forming bucks: they are not airtight. The micro-gaps between layer lines allow air to pass through the print naturally. In conventional vacuum forming, you drill a pattern of 1–2 mm vacuum holes across the buck to allow air evacuation. With FDM prints, this step is usually unnecessary — the print itself acts as a distributed vacuum distributor.

Exceptions — When FDM Bucks DO Need Holes
FDM prints that have been sanded smooth and coated with primer/epoxy may become airtight enough to need vacuum holes. Add 1–2 mm holes at low points and deep pockets where air can trap. Similarly, if you're using a heavy coating (XTC-3D, automotive primer) specifically for surface quality, test with a short vacuum cycle first.

Resin (SLA/MSLA) Bucks

Cured resin prints are genuinely watertight and airtight. Resin bucks always need vacuum holes — without them, the vacuum can't pull the sheet down. Drill 1.0–1.5 mm holes with a pin vise at the deepest recesses and any enclosed pockets before vacuum forming. Resin bucks offer much better surface quality than FDM — the formed sheet takes a cleaner impression.

Drape Forming with a Heat Gun

The simplest thermoforming technique — no vacuum machine required:

  1. Print or prepare your buck (positive form). ABS/ASA/PC for most sheet materials.
  2. Cut sheet plastic slightly larger than needed. Clamp edges if possible to prevent warping during heating.
  3. Heat the sheet plastic evenly with a heat gun, moving continuously to avoid hot spots. The plastic becomes glossy and sags when ready — typically 10–30 seconds per zone at 6–8 cm distance.
  4. Quickly drape the softened sheet over the buck. Press down with a padded block or gloved hand. The sheet cools quickly — you have 5–15 seconds to work.
  5. Hold in place until cooled (30–60 seconds). Trim the excess.

Good Applications

  • Simple curved covers and enclosures
  • Custom-fit organizer trays
  • Shaped guards and deflectors
  • Rapid packaging prototypes
  • Kydex knife sheaths over a printed form
  • Holsters (Kydex over a printed gun form)

Limitations vs. Vacuum Forming

  • Detail fidelity is lower — no vacuum pulling sheet into fine features
  • Uneven heating creates wrinkles and thin spots
  • Deep draws are difficult — sheet stretches unevenly
  • Fine textures on the buck don't transfer as cleanly
  • Best for: gentle curves, simple shapes, Kydex-style materials that retain form well

Compression Thermoforming — Positive + Negative Printed Pair

Print both the positive (male) form and the matching negative (female) cavity. Heat the sheet plastic until pliable, then clamp the two halves together with the sheet between them. Both surfaces of the formed part take the mold geometry — this is the key advantage over single-buck vacuum forming where only one side is controlled.

  • Design the gap: The positive must be smaller than the negative by the sheet plastic thickness + 0.2–0.5 mm clearance. For 1.5 mm PETG sheet: negative cavity = positive + 1.5 mm + 0.3 mm clearance on all faces.
  • Material: Both halves need to be heat-resistant. PC or High-Temp Resin recommended. ABS or ASA is marginal for PETG sheet.
  • Press method: A simple bench vise, F-clamps, or a drill press (unspinning, just for clamping force) works for small parts. Large parts need a frame press.
  • Release: Apply mold release (PTFE spray or Ease Release 200) to both mold surfaces before forming — heated sheet plastic bonds aggressively to printed surfaces without release agent.
  • Best for: Parts where both faces need defined geometry — seating surfaces, panels, complex contoured parts where vacuum forming would leave the back face undefined.

Printing Your Own Forming Sheet Stock

An often-overlooked technique: instead of buying sheet plastic, print thin sheets or mesh patterns as the forming material itself.

Printed Thin Sheet
Print a flat, solid 0.5–2 mm thick sheet of PETG, TPU, or PC at 100% infill. This becomes your forming stock. Useful for: custom material with embedded patterns or textures, creating sheets of engineering materials not available in sheet form, small quantities where buying minimum-order sheet material is wasteful. Print slowly at 100% infill, 3–5 solid top/bottom layers for consistency.
Printed Mesh / Lattice Sheet
Print an open mesh or lattice pattern flat, then thermoform the mesh over a buck. The formed mesh retains the mesh geometry conforming to the 3D shape of the buck. Applications: custom shaped mesh guards, anatomically contoured orthotics, aesthetic grille structures, patterned enclosure panels. The mesh pattern is integrated into the material — not a surface texture but the structure itself.
Positive + Negative with Printed Stock
Combine all techniques: print a patterned sheet (mesh, texture, lattice) as the forming stock, then compression-form it between a printed positive and negative pair. The result: a custom 3D-shaped part with designed internal structure, formed precisely to geometry, entirely from printed materials. No commercial sheet stock required at any step.
Layer Lines in Formed Parts — Feature or Bug
When vacuum forming or drape forming over an FDM buck, the layer lines on the buck surface will transfer to the inside surface of the formed part. This can be:

A bug: For cosmetic parts where a smooth inside surface matters — sand and prime the FDM buck before forming. 400-grit sanding + filler primer eliminates most layer line telegraphing.

A feature: The ridge pattern from layer lines creates a subtle texture on the formed part that can improve grip or aesthetics. Coarser layer heights (0.3–0.4 mm) create more pronounced texture; finer layers (0.1 mm) are barely visible. Resin bucks produce near-mirror-smooth formed surfaces without any finishing.

Safety — Resins, Powders & Metal

Every additive manufacturing process category has distinct hazards. Many are invisible or unfamiliar to people coming from a desktop FDM background. Understanding these hazards before you encounter them is critical — some can cause permanent health damage or catastrophic equipment failure.

Photopolymer Resin — Sensitization Hazard

Uncured Resin is a Permanent Sensitizer — Not Just an Irritant
Liquid photopolymer resin contains acrylate monomers and photoinitiators that are skin and respiratory sensitizers. A sensitizer is fundamentally different from an irritant: repeated exposure trains your immune system to treat the compound as a threat. Once sensitized, even tiny exposures trigger increasingly severe allergic responses — and this sensitization is permanent and irreversible.

People who work with resin without proper protection and become sensitized may find themselves permanently unable to work with any photopolymer resin — a career-ending outcome for dental technicians, product designers, and lab technicians who rely on the technology. Sensitization most commonly occurs through skin contact, not just inhalation.
🧤 Nitrile Gloves — Non-Negotiable
Wear nitrile gloves for every resin contact — opening the printer, touching FEP, handling fresh prints, working with IPA wash (which contains dissolved resin). Nitrile resists acrylate permeation; latex does not. Double-glove for extended or heavy contact. Never touch your face with resin-contaminated gloves. Change when torn.
💨 Ventilation During Printing & Handling
Resin printing emits VOCs and acrylate vapors — most concentrated when opening an enclosed printer. Print in a ventilated space. Activated carbon filtration is more effective than HEPA alone (HEPA catches particles; carbon catches VOCs). The main exposure window is during part removal, resin pouring, and wash operations — not passively sitting next to the printer.
🥽 Eye Protection
Safety glasses minimum during resin handling. Splash risk from resin removal (suction cup forces), pouring, and IPA wash agitation. UV glasses protect from curing UV light if your cure station is not fully enclosed. Resin in eyes requires immediate and prolonged water flushing and medical attention.
✅ Cured Parts are Safe
Properly washed and UV-post-cured resin is chemically stable and safe to handle barehanded. The hazard is exclusively in the liquid and partially cured states. Sanding cured resin generates dust — wear a dust mask. Water-washable resins are NOT safe to drain — they're easier to clean off skin but are still sensitizers requiring identical PPE and disposal protocols.

Resin Disposal Protocol

  • Liquid resin: Never pour down drains or into trash as liquid. Cure it — spread thin layers on cardboard in sunlight or under UV lamp until fully solid, then dispose as solid plastic waste per local regulations.
  • IPA wash solution: Cure the dissolved resin first. Expose IPA wash to sunlight in an open container (outdoors) until resin solids sink and cure. Filter out solids (dispose as solid waste). Evaporated IPA is not a drain disposal — many municipalities class it as hazardous liquid waste.
  • Gloves, wipes, paper towels: Cure any resin contamination on these before disposal — spread flat in sun or UV lamp. Uncured resin in landfill is an environmental hazard.
  • Emergency skin contact: Wipe off with dry cloth first (water spreads acrylates), then wash thoroughly with soap and water. Do not just rinse with water alone. Consult a physician if rash develops.

SLS / MJF Polymer Powder — Inhalation & Explosion Hazard

🔥 Polymer Powder is Combustible Dust — Do NOT Use a Standard Shop Vacuum
Fine nylon (PA12/PA11) powder used in SLS and MJF is classified as a combustible dust per NFPA 654 (Standard for the Prevention of Fire and Dust Explosions). When fine polymer dust is dispersed in air at or above its minimum explosive concentration (MEC), a single spark — including the static discharge from a standard household or shop vacuum motor — can trigger a dust explosion.

Nylon PA12 dust explosion parameters:
• Minimum Explosive Concentration (MEC): ~30–60 g/m³
• Minimum Ignition Energy: ~10–100 mJ (easily achieved by static discharge)
• Kst (dust explosion index): ~150–200 bar·m/s — "moderate" severity, but that's still a structural explosion

What to use instead of a shop vac:
• Anti-static, explosion-rated vacuum (e.g., Ruwac, Nilfisk ATEX, Kercher industrial HEPA explosion-proof)
• Manufacturer-supplied depowdering stations: Formlabs Fuse Sift, HP Build Manager — designed with anti-static and sealed systems
• Ground yourself and all equipment with ESD wrist straps and grounding cables before handling loose powder
• Never use a standard vacuum (not even HEPA) for SLS powder — the motor brushes create sparks; the filter is not designed for combustible dust

Inhalation Hazard

Nylon powder particles in SLS/MJF are typically 40–100 µm in diameter — but broken particles and processing can create fines well below 10 µm (respirable fraction). Fine nylon dust is an inhalable and potentially respirable nuisance dust. Chronic exposure without respiratory protection may cause:

  • Respiratory irritation
  • Potential sensitization (polymer dust, similar to occupational asthma)
  • Long-term lung effects with repeated unprotected exposure

Required PPE for powder handling:

  • FFP2 / N95 respirator minimum during depowdering, sieving, or any open powder handling
  • P3/N100 respirator for extended work or fine-sieving operations
  • Nitrile gloves (powder skin contact — check SDS for specific grade)
  • Safety glasses (powder splash)

Safe Powder Handling Practices

  • Use a dedicated depowdering station (enclosed, with filtered exhaust) — not an open workbench
  • Ground all equipment — bonding and grounding cables on the machine, depowdering station, and containers
  • Anti-static tools only — ESD-safe brushes, scoops, containers
  • Store powder in original sealed containers — closed lid prevents moisture absorption and accidental spill
  • No open flames or ignition sources in the powder handling area
  • Keep powder quantities small in the open work area — transfer from bulk storage as needed rather than leaving large open containers
  • Dispose of exhausted/contaminated powder per manufacturer SDS guidance — many municipalities classify polymer powder waste specifically
  • Formlabs Fuse Sift: Specifically engineered with anti-static systems, sealed powder transfer, HEPA filtration, and grounding — the correct tool for Fuse 1 depowdering
HP Fusing Agent & MJF Specific
HP MJF parts come out of the build with residual fusing agent (a dark thermal absorber) and detailing agent coating the powder and part surfaces. These agents contain proprietary solvents and surfactants — wear gloves and eye protection during depowdering. The dried agent on parts is generally low hazard once fully cured in the build, but fresh build powder with wet agents warrants the same combustible dust + skin contact precautions as SLS PA12 powder.

Metal AM Powder — Reactive, Pyrophoric, and Toxic

Metal Powder is Categorically More Hazardous Than Polymer Powder
Metal powders for LPBF (titanium, aluminum, stainless steel, inconel, cobalt-chrome) present hazards that polymer powders do not:

Pyrophoric metals (Ti, Al): Fine titanium and aluminum powder can spontaneously ignite in air at sufficient concentration. Titanium fires burn at ~1,670°C and cannot be extinguished with water (water produces hydrogen gas — accelerates the fire). Dry sand or Class D fire extinguisher only.

Inert atmosphere requirement: LPBF machines operate under argon or nitrogen atmosphere specifically because metal powder + oxygen + heat = fire/explosion risk. This is not a quality measure — it is a safety measure.

Toxic metals: Cobalt-chrome (CoCrMo) powder is classified as a probable carcinogen (IARC Group 2A). Nickel-containing alloys (Inconel, stainless steel) release nickel compounds — occupational carcinogens. All metal powders require P3/N100 respirator and full face protection during open handling.
Titanium Powder (Ti6Al4V)
Pyrophoric — can spontaneously ignite as fine powder. Storage in inert atmosphere (argon) containers. No water extinguishment — Class D extinguisher or dry sand only. Inhalation of Ti dust is a respiratory hazard. Biologically compatible once implanted (this is why it's used for implants) — but the powder form is entirely different from bulk solid metal.
Aluminum Alloy Powder (AlSi10Mg)
Extremely pyrophoric. Aluminum powder + air + ignition source = thermite-class fire intensity. Class D extinguisher only. LPBF aluminum builds require the most rigorous inert atmosphere control. Machine maintenance and powder handling done with nitrogen purging of all equipment. One of the highest-risk metal AM powders.
Cobalt-Chrome (CoCrMo)
IARC Group 2A carcinogen (cobalt compounds). P3/N100 respirator mandatory — FFP2/N95 is NOT adequate for CoCr powder. Full-face visor. Gloves + arm protection. Used in dental and medical AM — dental technicians handling CoCr powder have historically shown elevated occupational cobalt exposure. Requires dedicated sealed handling systems in dental labs.
Stainless Steel / Inconel (Ni-alloys)
Nickel and nickel compounds are IARC Group 1 carcinogens (Group 1 = confirmed human carcinogen). Fine nickel-containing powder in Inconel 625/718 and 316L stainless steel warrants P3 respirator and HEPA-filtered powder containment. Less acutely dangerous than Ti/Al pyrophoric risk but significant long-term occupational exposure concern.

MJF Post-Processing — Key Differences from SLS

MJF shares the powder-bed cooling and depowdering workflow with SLS, but with important differences driven by the fusing agent chemistry:

  • Grey/black coloration from fusing agent: MJF PA12 parts emerge uniformly dark grey/black from the HP fusing agent thermal absorber. Unlike SLS natural grey, MJF parts look definitively "industrial" — this is why dyeing to black is very common to create a uniform, professional surface rather than the blotchy agent-patterned appearance.
  • Bead blasting is essential: The detailing agent residue creates a "crusty" surface that bead blasting effectively removes. Skip blasting and the surface feels gritty and looks inconsistent.
  • Cooling time is equally critical: Same slow-cooling requirement as SLS. HP's Build Manager software predicts cooling time based on build density.
  • Powder refresh rate: MJF typically requires ~20–50% fresh powder per build (vs. 30–50% for SLS). Recycled powder without adequate refresh can cause part property degradation and surface pitting — MFI (Melt Flow Index) degrades with each thermal cycle.
  • Color printing (Jet Fusion 300/500 series): Color agent residue requires careful washing and handling. The colored surfaces are slightly more delicate than standard PA12 during blasting — reduce bead blast pressure for color parts.

Sintering Shrinkage — Design Strategy

The ~15–20% linear shrinkage during sintering must be pre-compensated in the design:

  • Scale the part 1.18–1.22× in all dimensions (machine-specific; run calibration coupons for your furnace/powder batch)
  • Shrinkage is generally isotropic after calibration, but slightly higher in Z than XY — calibrate per axis
  • Flat parts warp during sintering: The bottom surface drags on the setter during shrinkage. Use ceramic setter plates with a release powder (alumina), design parts with a lip to keep them off the setter face, or design for expected warp and machine flat afterwards
  • Thin walls (<2 mm): Can sag or warp during sintering. Support with ceramic setter media on both sides for critical flat sections
  • Hole spacing: Holes can close during sintering if too small — design holes 20% larger than final target, accounting for shrinkage. Holes tend to close rather than shrink proportionally

PolyJet — Removing Waxy Support Material

WaterJet Support Removal
High-pressure water jet blasts away the waxy SUP706/SUP707 support material. Very clean result — no surface marks. Best for exterior surfaces and complex geometry. Stratasys WaterJet cleaning station is purpose-built. Can be done with a pressure washer at lower pressure.
NaOH Solution Soak
Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution at 2–3% concentration dissolves PolyJet support material chemically. Used for internal channels and geometries a water jet can't reach. 30–60 min soak at 50–70°C. Safety: NaOH is strongly alkaline — gloves, goggles, and ventilation essential. Rinse thoroughly.
Surface Finish Note
PolyJet parts come off the machine with an inherently smooth finish (Ra ~1–3 µm) — the material jets as droplets onto the previous layer, creating a near-continuous surface. Typically requires only light hand sanding (600–1000 grit) for cosmetic work. The glossy photopolymer surface is UV-sensitive — use UV-blocking clear coat for anything exposed to light long-term.
