02

Why frameworks work

A framework is a repeatable structure for thinking through one type of problem. It never supplies the answer. It decides which questions get asked, in what order, and who gets to speak. That sounds small, but most bad team decisions come from the same few failures, and each framework is a fix for one of them.

It moves thinking out of heads and onto the page
Working memory holds roughly four items at once. A decision with five options and five criteria is 25 judgments, which nobody can hold mentally. A matrix or a chart holds them so the group can look at the same thing.
It separates generating from judging
When ideas are criticized as they appear, people stop offering them. Brainstorming tools forbid judgment until a set time; decision tools come after.
It gives quiet people an equal voice
Silent writing, dot voting, and multiple scorers capture everyone's view before the loudest voice anchors the group.
It makes reasoning visible and challengeable
"I think the roller is better" cannot be argued with productively. "I scored reliability 2 because the belt slipped in testing" can.
It creates a record
A saved worksheet explains to next year's team, or to judges, why a choice was made. That is engineering-notebook evidence.
It stops the same problem from returning
Root-cause tools push past "replace the part" to the process that let it fail, so fixes last.

What frameworks cannot do

A framework cannot fix bad information. A Decision Matrix built on guessed scores produces a confident-looking guess. It cannot replace testing: an FMEA predicts failures but does not prove the robot works. And it cannot make a group agree on values: if half the team values speed and half values reliability, the matrix will show the split clearly, which is useful, but the team still has to decide.

Process theater
The most common misuse is filling out a framework after the decision is already made, to justify it. Students notice. If the outcome is decided, say so and skip the tool.
03

Diverge, then converge

Most problem solving alternates between two modes. Diverging means generating as many options, questions, or causes as possible without judging them. Converging means narrowing those down to a choice. The two modes need opposite behavior: diverging needs quantity and suspended judgment; converging needs criteria and critical thinking. Meetings fail when a group tries to do both at once, because the first idea gets criticized and nobody offers a second.

Diverge, then converge

The design-thinking "double diamond" applies this twice: first to find the right problem, then to find the right solution. Many robotics teams skip the first diamond and jump straight to solutions, then build an excellent answer to the wrong question.

04

Reasoning tools vs preference tools

Every decision tool sits somewhere on a spectrum from recording reasoning to recording preference. Reasoning tools write down why: which criteria, how much each mattered, how each option scored. Preference tools record only what people chose. Both are legitimate, but they answer different questions.

Use reasoning tools when

  • The choice costs money or time: a mechanism, a drivetrain, a large purchase.
  • Someone will need to explain it later: judges, sponsors, next year's team.
  • The team disagrees: visible scores show exactly where people differ.
  • Safety is involved: never decide safety by preference.

Use preference tools when

  • The choice is taste: a robot name, a shirt, a theme, a lunch spot.
  • There is no right answer: any option is acceptable.
  • Speed matters more than precision: narrowing 30 ideas to 5.
  • Engagement is the goal: getting the whole team involved and having fun.
Mismatch is the failure
Picking a drivetrain by bracket feels democratic but leaves no record of why, so nobody can learn from it. Picking a shirt by weighted matrix is overkill and drains the fun. Match the tool to the stakes.

06

Frameworks inside the planning meeting

The team's planning meetings follow a sequence: decide whether it should be a meeting at all, list the agenda, order it, expand items into an outline, make decisions and action items, then assign owners and due dates. The loop ends by feeding the next meeting. Each step has a tool for the moment the group gets stuck. You will not use all seven in one meeting; use the one that matches where the group is stuck.

The planning meeting sequence and its tools

Online planning meetings

Planning meetings are usually calls with a shared screen. Two habits make toolkit worksheets work well remotely. The facilitator types; everyone else talks. One person sharing the screen and filling the worksheet keeps the group looking at one thing. Silent steps happen in chat. For dot voting or brainwriting, have everyone post in the call chat at the same time, then the facilitator transfers the results.

Who has the final say
Early in the season, mentors make the final call and model the process. As the team moves toward competition season, final say shifts to the captain and leads. The frameworks stay the same; only the decider changes.

08

Facilitation fundamentals

The facilitator's job is to run the process, not win the argument. A good facilitator protects time, protects quiet voices, and makes sure the session ends with decisions and owners. These eight habits cover most of it.

One tool per problem
Two frameworks in one meeting usually means neither gets finished. Save the second for next time.
Silent first, then talk
Have people write or vote alone before discussion. It prevents the first speaker from anchoring everyone.
Load the example
For a new tool, show the robotics example for two minutes, then press Start blank.
Timebox everything
Several tools have built-in timers. When time runs out, decide with what you have.
Write down the why
For engineering choices, record reasons, not just results. Use reasoning tools.
End with owners
The last five minutes are for names and dates. No owner means no change.
Stay neutral
If you have a strong opinion on the content, hand facilitation to someone else for that item.
Save the file
One file per worksheet, version number in the name, so it reopens next meeting.

09

Running a session, start to finish

Every framework session has the same three phases. Most of the value is decided before and after the meeting, not during it.

Before (5 to 10 minutes)

  1. Pick one tool using Section 05 or the finder.
  2. Write the question in the worksheet title so everyone sees the same problem.
  3. Prefill what you can: options, criteria, or roster. It saves meeting time.
  4. Decide the decider: mentor, captain, or group vote. Say it at the start.
  5. Set the timebox and share it.

During

  1. Name the mode: generating or deciding.
  2. Silent round first where the tool allows.
  3. Fill the worksheet live on a shared screen.
  4. Watch the clock and call the next step on time.

After (5 minutes)

  1. Read back the decision and the reason in one sentence.
  2. Assign owners and dates for every action.
  3. Save the file and post it where the team keeps meeting notes.
  4. Export a PNG or PDF if it goes into the engineering notebook or an award submission.
  5. Put a revisit date on the calendar for living documents like risk maps and OKRs.

Decide and prioritize

These tools turn a disagreement into a visible structure. The Decision Matrix and Pugh Matrix are for choices where the reasons can be written down: specs, cost, weight, build time. Eisenhower, MoSCoW, RICE, Kano, and Impact / Effort sort lists so the team works on the right thing first. Use these when the team keeps arguing in circles. Writing the criteria down usually ends the argument faster than the scores do.

11.1

Decision Matrix

20-40 min · 2-8 · Partly documented

Rank several options against weighted criteria, then see the tradeoffs on a heat map.

What it is

You score every option on every criterion and weight the criteria by how much they matter. The math ranks the options. The map shows tradeoffs that a single score hides, with a third criterion as bar height.

When to use it

Choosing between three or more real options: intake designs, drivetrains, fundraisers, travel plans.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. List the options as rows and the criteria as columns.
  2. Give each criterion a weight. A weight of 3 counts three times as much as a weight of 1.
  3. Score every cell. Tick "lower is better" for cost, weight, or build time, and the toolkit flips it so higher always means better.
  4. Read the ranking, then use the map: pick two criteria for the floor and a third for bar height.
  5. For a group decision, turn on Multiple scorers. Each person scores alone, then look at the SPLIT cells together.
In depth
  1. Write the decision as one question with a clear scope, such as "Which intake design do we build for Week 1?" A vague question produces criteria that do not compare the options fairly.
  2. List the options as columns, keeping them to roughly 3 to 6. More than that and people stop scoring carefully; cut obvious losers first.
  3. List the criteria as rows, and make each one something you can judge for every option (cycle time, cost, build hours, reliability). Remove any criterion that is a hard requirement and treat it as a pass/fail filter before scoring, because a must-have should not be traded off against nice-to-haves.
  4. Assign a weight to each criterion before anyone scores options, for example on a 1 to 5 scale or as percentages that sum to 100. Setting weights first stops people from tuning weights to make their favorite win.
  5. Score each option on each criterion using a defined scale (for example 1 = poor, 3 = acceptable, 5 = excellent), and write a one-line reason for each score. The reasons are what make the matrix reviewable later.
  6. Multiply each score by its weight and add the column. The total gives a ranking, not a verdict.
  7. Run a sensitivity check: change the top two weights by a point and see whether the winner changes. If a small change flips the result, the options are effectively tied and you need more data or a prototype test.
  8. Record the final choice, the matrix, and any reasons you overrode the math. This gives the team a record to revisit after the event.

History

Who created it

No single inventor. Benjamin Franklin described an early version in a 1772 letter to Joseph Priestley (his "prudential algebra" of weighing pros against cons), and the weighted scoring grid grew out of 20th-century operations research and multi-criteria decision analysis. Engineering versions, such as Stuart Pugh's 1981 concept selection method, made it a standard design tool.

What it was created for

Making a choice between several options when many factors matter at once and no single factor decides it. The point was to replace gut feeling with an explicit, written trade-off that others can check.

What it is used for

Choosing vendors, products, designs, software tools, job offers, and project proposals. Teams use it to make a group decision traceable so people can see why an option won.

Industries Engineering designProcurement and purchasingProduct managementConsultingGovernment contractingHealthcare administration

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Score blind when you can: have each person fill in scores separately, then compare. This cuts down on people anchoring to the loudest voice.
  • Keep criteria independent. If "weight" and "center of gravity" measure the same thing, you are double-counting it.
  • If the top two totals are within about 10 percent, treat it as a tie and decide with a test or a mentor review instead of the spreadsheet.
  • For a quick group version, give each person a fixed number of dots to place across criteria to set the weights.
Watch out

Scores feel precise but are opinions. If the winner surprises everyone, check the weights before trusting the math.

Rambots example (made up)

Before kickoff week ends, the drive subteam compares three drivetrains: an off-the-shelf swerve, a 6-wheel tank, and a mecanum base. They weight maneuverability 5, reliability 5, cost 3, and build time 4, then score each option 1 to 5. Swerve totals 67, tank 63, and mecanum 51, and when they drop reliability to 4 the gap grows to 5 points. The lead is small and swerve scored below tank on reliability, so they order one swerve module early and run it for a week before committing.

11.2

Pugh Matrix

15-30 min · 2-6 · Well documented

Compare design concepts against a baseline with +, 0, and −.

What it is

Pick one concept as the baseline. Mark every other concept better (+), the same (0), or worse (−) on each criterion. It is faster than numeric scoring and works with sketches.

When to use it

Early mechanism design, when you have concept sketches but no numbers yet.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Put criteria in rows and concepts in columns. The first column is the baseline.
  2. Click each cell to cycle 0, +, −.
  3. Read the weighted net score. Look at the winner's minuses and ask if another concept fixes them.
  4. Combine the best ideas and run it again with the winner as the new baseline.
In depth
  1. Sketch each concept at the same level of detail. If one idea is a full CAD model and another is a napkin drawing, the comparison is unfair.
  2. Pick a datum (reference) concept, usually the current design, a known good design, or the one the team leans toward. Every other concept is judged only relative to this one, which is easier and more honest than scoring in absolute terms.
  3. List the criteria as rows. These come from the requirements: game tasks, weight, cost, manufacturability, serviceability.
  4. For each concept and criterion, mark + (better than datum), - (worse), or S (same). Argue about the marks out loud, because the discussion is where the team learns what each concept really does.
  5. Count the pluses, minuses, and sames for each concept. Do not just subtract them; look at which minuses matter and whether they can be fixed.
  6. Attack the weak spots: for strong concepts, ask how to remove their minuses, often by borrowing a feature from another concept. Pugh called this controlled convergence, alternating between generating and cutting ideas.
  7. Pick the strongest concept, or a new hybrid, as the new datum and run the matrix again. Repeat until one concept clearly wins across rounds.

History

Who created it

Created by Stuart Pugh, a British engineering design professor, who presented it in a 1981 paper titled "Concept Selection: A Method that Works." He expanded it in his 1991 book Total Design while he was at the University of Strathclyde, and it was used on General Motors' Saturn project.

What it was created for

Narrowing a set of early product concepts in engineering design without pretending to have precise numbers. Pugh wanted teams to compare each idea against one reference design and combine the best features of several ideas.

What it is used for

Early-stage concept selection in product and mechanical design, and in Six Sigma and Design for Six Sigma projects. It is also used to compare process or software design alternatives.

Industries Mechanical engineeringAutomotiveAerospaceMedical devicesSix Sigma and quality improvementConsumer products

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Change the datum and rerun. If a concept only wins against one particular datum, it is not clearly better.
  • A weighted Pugh matrix (multiply +1/-1 by criterion weights) is common, but keep the unweighted version too so a single heavy weight does not hide a lot of minuses.
  • Run it with a mixed group (design, build, drive, programming). Different people spot different minuses.
  • Many minuses on one row across every concept usually means the requirement is unclear or too hard, not that all the ideas are bad.
Watch out

The result depends on the baseline. Re-run it with the winner as the new baseline before committing.

Rambots example (made up)

For a game piece intake, the team uses last season's over-the-bumper roller intake as the datum and compares an under-the-bumper intake, a claw, and a wide funnel intake. The funnel scores + on pickup speed and alignment tolerance but - on frame perimeter and weight, while the under-bumper design scores + on protection. The team combines the funnel's wide mouth with the under-bumper mounting, makes that hybrid the new datum, and reruns the matrix the next meeting.

11.3

Eisenhower Matrix

10-15 min · 1-4 · Partly documented

Sort tasks by urgency and importance.

What it is

Each task gets an urgency score and an importance score. The four quadrants tell you what to do first, what to schedule, what to hand off, and what to drop.

When to use it

Build season to-do lists, or when a lead has more on their plate than time.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. List the tasks.
  2. Score urgency (how soon it must happen) and importance (how much it matters to the goal).
  3. Do the top-right first. Put top-left on the calendar. Hand off bottom-right. Drop bottom-left.
In depth
  1. Dump every task onto a list first, without sorting. Sorting while listing makes people skip tasks they do not want to face.
  2. Define "urgent" with a time rule before sorting, for example "needed within 72 hours or something breaks." Without a rule, everything feels urgent.
  3. Define "important" by your goals, for example "directly affects robot performance at the next event, safety, or a grant deadline." This forces the team to say what actually matters.
  4. Place each task in one quadrant: urgent and important (do now), important not urgent (schedule), urgent not important (delegate), neither (drop or defer). Put it where it belongs today, not where you wish it were.
  5. Give each Quadrant 2 task (important, not urgent) a calendar slot and an owner. This quadrant is the whole point; it is where planning, training, and maintenance live, and it gets crowded out if it is not scheduled.
  6. For Quadrant 3 tasks, name who will take each one and by when. Delegating without a name is the same as forgetting.
  7. Review the grid weekly and look at where last week's urgent work came from. If many Quadrant 1 items were once Quadrant 2 items that slipped, the fix is better scheduling, not working harder.

History

Who created it

Named after Dwight D. Eisenhower, who in a 1954 speech to the World Council of Churches assembly in Evanston, Illinois quoted an unnamed former college president: "I have two kinds of problems, the urgent and the important." Eisenhower did not draw the matrix. The four-quadrant grid was popularized by Stephen Covey in The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People (1989), where it is called the Time Management Matrix.

What it was created for

The original remark was about how a leader's attention gets pulled toward pressing problems that are not the most important ones. Covey turned that idea into a grid for personal time management.

What it is used for

Sorting a to-do list into do now, schedule, delegate, and drop. People use it for weekly planning, triaging email, and helping leads hand off work.

Industries Business managementProject managementEducationHealthcareSoftware developmentPersonal productivity coaching

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Cap Quadrant 1 at a small number (three to five items). If it is bigger, some items are not really both urgent and important.
  • Use it as a team tool: have each subteam lead sort their own list, then compare. Disagreements show where priorities are unclear.
  • Check Quadrant 4 for tasks that someone cares about. Dropping something silently can upset a sponsor or teammate, so tell people when you cut it.
  • Track how much time goes to Quadrant 2 each week. If it trends toward zero, the team is running on fires.
Watch out

Teams rate everything urgent and important. Force at least a third of the list out of the top-right corner.

Three days before a regional, the build lead sorts the list: a cracked elevator gusset and the pit safety glasses go in Quadrant 1, a spare-parts kit and the driver practice schedule go in Quadrant 2 with Thursday slots, reprinting the sponsor banner goes to Quadrant 3 and is handed to the outreach lead, and redesigning the team logo goes to Quadrant 4 until after the season.

11.4

Risk Matrix

20-30 min · 3-8 · Partly documented

Rate risks by likelihood and impact, then plan a response for the worst ones.

What it is

Each risk gets a likelihood and an impact score. Their product is the risk score. The heat map makes the dangerous corner obvious.

When to use it

Before a competition, a trip, a big purchase, or a risky build step.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Brainstorm what could go wrong.
  2. Score likelihood and impact.
  3. Plan a response and owner for every risk in the hot corner.
  4. Revisit before the event: did any scores change?
In depth
  1. Brainstorm risks as specific events with a cause and effect, such as "chain jumps off elevator sprocket during a match, elevator drops." Vague risks like "robot breaks" cannot be rated or fixed.
  2. Define the likelihood scale in words and numbers, for example 1 = less than once a season, 3 = once per event, 5 = most matches. Written definitions keep different people from rating the same risk very differently.
  3. Define the impact scale the same way, for example 1 = cosmetic, 3 = lose one match, 5 = injury or out of the event. Put safety at the top of the impact scale so it cannot be outranked by performance concerns.
  4. Rate each risk on both scales and place it in the grid. Discuss the ratings as a group, since the conversation exposes risks only one person knew about.
  5. Color the grid by zone (for example green, yellow, red) and agree in advance what each zone requires: red needs a fix before the next event, yellow needs a mitigation plan, green is monitored.
  6. For each red and yellow risk, write a mitigation, an owner, and a date. A matrix without owners just documents what will go wrong.
  7. Re-rate after mitigations and after each event. Risks move as the robot changes, and new ones show up in match footage and pit notes.

History

Who created it

No single inventor; it developed gradually in safety engineering and business planning. The US Department of Defense took steps toward it in the late 1970s and formalized a 5x4 hazard risk matrix in MIL-STD-882B (System Safety Program Requirements) in 1984. Risk analyst Tony Cox's 2008 paper "What's Wrong with Risk Matrices?" is the best-known critique of its limits.

What it was created for

Ranking safety hazards in military systems so that engineers and managers could decide which hazards needed design changes and who had authority to accept the remaining risk.

What it is used for

Rating risks by how likely they are and how bad they would be, so a team can focus prevention on the worst ones. It appears in project plans, safety reviews, and enterprise risk registers.

Industries Defense and aerospaceConstructionOil and gasHealthcareProject managementEvent planning

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Watch for high-impact, low-likelihood risks landing in a "medium" cell next to frequent nuisances. Cox showed matrices can rank these poorly, so flag any severity-5 risk for review regardless of its cell.
  • Keep the grid small (3x3 to 5x5). More cells give a false sense of precision.
  • Split "impact" into separate columns (safety, competition, cost) when risks are very different in kind, and rate the worst one.
  • Add a "detectability" note for risks you would not notice until too late; this borrows from FMEA.
Watch out

Rare but severe risks (battery fire) look small in the score. Any impact of 5 deserves a plan even if it is unlikely.

Rambots example (made up)

At the post-Week 1 debrief, the team rates "swerve module encoder cable unplugs" as likelihood 4 and impact 4 because it cost two matches, putting it in the red zone. The electrical lead adds strain relief and a pre-match connector check to the pit checklist by Tuesday, and the team re-rates it to likelihood 2 after the next event.

Stakeholder Map

20-30 min · 2-5 · Partly documented

Plan how to work with everyone who affects or cares about the team.

What it is

Score each stakeholder on interest (how much they care) and power (how much they can affect you). The quadrant tells you how closely to work with them.

When to use it

Sponsor outreach, grant planning, school approvals, and big public events.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. List people and groups who affect the team or care about it.
  2. Score interest and power.
  3. Write what each one needs from you and when you will next contact them.
  4. Manage the top-right closely; keep the top-left satisfied; keep the bottom-right informed.
In depth
  1. List every person or group affected by or able to affect the project, including ones who seem minor. People left off the list are the ones who surprise you later.
  2. Rate each stakeholder's power: their ability to approve, block, fund, or change the work. Be concrete about the source of power (budget, facility access, signatures).
  3. Rate each stakeholder's interest: how much they care about the outcome and how closely they follow it. Interest can be positive or negative.
  4. Place each one in the 2x2 grid. High power and high interest: manage closely. High power, low interest: keep satisfied. Low power, high interest: keep informed. Low both: monitor.
  5. For each quadrant, set a communication plan with channel and frequency, such as monthly email updates for sponsors or weekly check-ins with the school's facilities contact. The grid is only useful once it drives actual communication.
  6. Mark each stakeholder's current position (supporter, neutral, opponent) next to their dot. A high-power opponent needs a different approach from a high-power supporter.
  7. Revisit the map each season or when something changes, like a new administrator. Stakeholders move between quadrants, and a keep-satisfied person can become a key player overnight.

History

Who created it

The power/interest grid is credited to Aubrey Mendelow, whose stakeholder paper appeared in the proceedings of the Second International Conference on Information Systems (often cited as 1991, though that conference was held in 1981). It builds on the broader stakeholder theory associated with R. Edward Freeman's 1984 book Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach.

What it was created for

Helping organizations scan their environment and decide which outside groups could affect their strategy, and how much attention each group needed.

What it is used for

Planning who to inform, consult, or closely involve on a project or change. It is standard in project management and communications planning.

Industries Project managementPublic policy and governmentNonprofitsCorporate strategyConstruction and infrastructureHealthcare

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Watch for low-power, high-interest groups that can gain power, such as parents who can go to the principal or the school board.
  • Name individuals, not departments. "The school" is not a stakeholder you can email; the assistant principal who signs field trip forms is.
  • Add an arrow to show where you want a stakeholder to move (for example, from low interest to high interest for a potential sponsor).
  • Keep the map private if it includes candid notes on people. Share the communication plan instead.
Watch out

People move between quadrants. Revisit the map each season, especially for sponsors.

Rambots example (made up)

Planning an off-season build of a new shop space, the team lead puts the school principal and facilities manager in manage closely, the main corporate sponsor in keep satisfied, parents and alumni in keep informed, and neighboring classrooms in monitor. That leads to a biweekly meeting with facilities, a one-page monthly update to the sponsor, and a parent newsletter section.

11.6

MoSCoW

15-20 min · 3-8 · Well documented

Scope a project into Must, Should, Could, and Won't.

What it is

Sort every feature or task into four buckets so the team agrees on what has to ship and what gets cut when time runs out.

When to use it

Setting robot scope at kickoff, or planning an event with a fixed budget.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write the project and its deadline.
  2. Put each feature in one bucket. One per line.
  3. Keep Must small: if everything is a Must, nothing is.
  4. Writing the Won't list is the point: it stops scope creep.
In depth
  1. Fix the deadline and the scope boundary first, for example "what ships in the robot at our first regional." MoSCoW only works when the time box is fixed and scope flexes.
  2. List every requested feature or task. Include ideas that seem unlikely, since writing them down is how you later mark them Won't.
  3. Mark the Must haves: items without which the result is unusable, unsafe, illegal, or pointless. Test each with the question "if this is missing, do we still compete?" If the answer is yes, it is not a Must.
  4. Mark the Should haves: important but with a workaround. Then mark the Could haves: nice extras that go only if time allows.
  5. Mark the Won't haves (this time): items explicitly out of scope for this deadline. Writing them down stops them from creeping back in and tells people their idea was heard.
  6. Check the balance. A common DSDM guideline is to keep Musts to about 60 percent of the effort, so there is a buffer of Shoulds and Coulds to drop when things go wrong.
  7. Revisit priorities at each checkpoint. When a Must slips, cut Coulds and then Shoulds to protect it, rather than extending the deadline.

History

Who created it

Created by Dai Clegg at Oracle in 1994 for use in rapid application development (RAD) software projects. It was later built into the Dynamic Systems Development Method (DSDM), which spread it widely.

What it was created for

Fixed-deadline software projects where not every requirement could be delivered on time. Teams needed a shared language with clients about what would be cut first if time ran short.

What it is used for

Prioritizing features, requirements, and deliverables for a release, sprint, or event. It is common in agile software teams and in any project with a hard deadline.

Industries Software developmentProduct managementBusiness analysisEvent planningMarketing campaignsIT project management

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • The lowercase letters are only there to make the word readable; do not let anyone argue that "o" means something.
  • Agree on who has final say over category disputes before the meeting, so the session does not stall.
  • Pair MoSCoW with effort estimates. A Should that takes two hours may be worth doing before a Could that takes two weeks, and vice versa.
  • Watch for everything becoming a Must. If more than about two-thirds of items are Musts, the deadline is probably unrealistic.
Watch out

If Must is more than about a third of the list, it stops meaning anything.

Rambots example (made up)

Two weeks before the first event, programming lists autonomous features. Must: drive out of the starting zone and score one preloaded piece. Should: a two-piece auto. Could: vision-aligned three-piece auto. Won't (this event): a path that picks up from the center line. When the shooter gets rebuilt and costs four days, the team drops the Could and keeps the two-piece auto.

11.7

Impact / Effort Matrix

10-15 min · Any · Thin record

Sort a pile of ideas into quick wins, big projects, fill-ins, and money pits.

What it is

Each idea gets an impact score and an effort score. High impact with low effort is a quick win. High effort with low impact is a money pit: drop it.

When to use it

After a brainstorm, when you have more ideas than time.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. List the ideas.
  2. Score impact (how much it helps the goal) and effort (time, money, people).
  3. Do quick wins now, plan the big projects, fit fill-ins into spare time, and drop the money pits.
In depth
  1. List every candidate task or idea on its own sticky note or spreadsheet row. Keep items roughly the same size (break 'build the robot' into real tasks) so they can be compared fairly.
  2. Agree on what 'impact' means for this decision before scoring, for example match points gained, sponsor dollars, or students trained. Without a shared definition, people score by gut and argue past each other.
  3. Define 'effort' in the unit the team actually runs short of: student-hours, mentor time, machine time, or money. Effort should reflect your real bottleneck, not just how hard something sounds.
  4. Score each item on both axes (1 to 5 works well), silently first, then discuss the big disagreements. Disagreements often reveal hidden information, like a mentor knowing a part is backordered.
  5. Place items on the grid: high impact and low effort are Quick Wins, high and high are Major Projects, low and low are Fill-ins, and low impact with high effort are Thankless Tasks. The grid shows the trade-offs to the whole group at once.
  6. Do Quick Wins now, schedule and staff Major Projects on purpose, save Fill-ins for spare time, and drop or redesign Thankless Tasks. The decision step is the point; the chart alone changes nothing.
  7. Re-plot when new information arrives, such as a scouting report, a failed test, or a grant decision. First effort estimates are often wrong, and items move.

History

Who created it

There is no single inventor. The impact versus effort 2x2 grew out of Lean and Six Sigma improvement work, where teams needed a fast way to sort large numbers of improvement ideas, and it resembles older 2x2 prioritization grids such as the Eisenhower urgent/important matrix. 'Action Priority Matrix' is one of several names for the same grid.

What it was created for

Improvement teams that generated more ideas than they could act on needed to pick the ones that delivered the most value for the least work.

What it is used for

Teams use it to sort backlogs, brainstorm results, and to-do lists into Quick Wins, Major Projects, Fill-ins, and Thankless Tasks so limited time goes to the right work.

Industries Lean manufacturingSix Sigma and quality improvementProduct managementSoftware developmentNonprofitsMarketing

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • If almost everything lands in Quick Wins, effort is being underestimated. Ask 'Who exactly will do this, and when?' for each one.
  • Add a third dimension with dot size or color, such as risk or deadline, when two axes are not enough.
  • With a new group, place items relative to each other ('Is this harder than that?') instead of giving absolute scores.
Watch out

Effort is usually underestimated. Ask the person who will do the work.

Rambots example (made up)

After a week 0 scrimmage, the team lists 12 fixes. Adding a beam-break sensor to the intake (2 hours, stops double-feed jams) is a Quick Win; redesigning the elevator to reach a higher scoring level (about 40 student-hours plus a new gearbox) is a Major Project scheduled for the gap before the second regional; repainting the bumper numbers is a Fill-in; and rewriting all the drive code in a new framework mid-season is a Thankless Task that gets dropped.

Force Field Analysis

20-30 min · 3-8 · Well documented

Weigh the forces pushing for a change against those pushing back.

What it is

Kurt Lewin's tool. List what pushes for a change and what resists it, and score each force. Then plan how to strengthen the drivers or, often easier, weaken the biggest restrainer.

When to use it

Proposing a big change: a new drivetrain, a new meeting format, a new tool the team must adopt.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write the change.
  2. List driving and restraining forces and score their strength.
  3. Plan around the strongest restrainer.
In depth
  1. Write the change you want as a specific goal in the middle of the page, such as 'Every subteam logs its work in the task tracker.' A vague goal produces vague forces.
  2. List the driving forces on one side: the reasons, people, and pressures pushing toward the change. Include what actually motivates people, not just arguments you wish were persuasive.
  3. List the restraining forces on the other side: habits, costs, fears, missing skills, and competing priorities. Ask the people affected, because they know the restraints better than the planners do.
  4. Score each force, for example from 1 to 5, and draw arrows sized by strength. Seeing the balance explains why the current situation is stuck.
  5. Focus first on weakening restraining forces rather than adding more pressure. Pushing harder often increases resistance, while removing a blocker lets change happen with less conflict.
  6. Choose 2 or 3 specific actions, each tied to a named force and an owner. This turns the diagram into a plan.
  7. After the change, look for forces that could pull things back, and add supports such as a checklist, a role, or a routine to hold the new state in place.

History

Who created it

Social psychologist Kurt Lewin developed force field analysis in the 1940s as part of his field theory of behavior. His 1943 paper 'Defining the Field at a Given Time' and the collection Field Theory in Social Science (1951) are the usual references.

What it was created for

Lewin wanted to explain why group habits and social situations stay stable, and how to change them by shifting the balance between forces pushing for change and forces holding it back.

What it is used for

Leaders use it to plan a change by listing the forces that drive it and the forces that resist it, then deciding which to strengthen and which to weaken.

Industries Change managementOrganizational developmentHealthcareEducationPublic policyEngineering management

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Separate forces you can control from forces you cannot, and spend your energy on the ones you can move.
  • Run it with the people who resist the change in the room. Their restraining forces are often legitimate design input, not just complaints.
  • Revisit the diagram halfway through the change. Forces shift as people see early results.
Watch out

Strength scores are guesses. The conversation about the strongest restrainer is the valuable part.

Rambots example (made up)

The team wants every robot part modeled in CAD before it is cut. Driving forces: fewer remakes (9 plates were recut last season), sponsors asking for CAD screenshots, and mentor support. Restraining forces: only 2 students know Onshape well, shop laptops are slow, and builders feel CAD slows them down. Instead of issuing a rule, the leads run three Saturday CAD workshops and pair each builder with a CAD student, which removes the biggest restraint.

11.9

Weighted Pros and Cons

10-15 min · 1-6 · Well documented

A T-chart where each pro and con gets a weight.

What it is

A simple pros and cons list, except each item is scored from 1 to 5 for how much it matters. It is lighter than a Decision Matrix and works for yes-or-no choices.

When to use it

A single yes-or-no decision: join a second event, buy a tool, change a meeting time.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write the decision as a yes-or-no question.
  2. List pros and cons and weight each one.
  3. Read the totals, but trust the discussion more than the math.
In depth
  1. Divide a page into two columns labeled Pro and Con for one specific option. Franklin's point was to see all the reasons at once instead of whichever one is in your head right now.
  2. Add reasons over several days, as Franklin suggested (three or four days), or at least across two sessions. New reasons appear as you live with the question.
  3. Give each reason a weight, for example 1 to 5. Not every reason counts equally, and the weights are what make this better than counting items.
  4. Cross out a pro and a con of equal weight, or one pro against two cons that add up to the same weight, as Franklin described. This simplifies the list until the balance is clear.
  5. Look at what is left and see which side is heavier. If the balance is close, the decision probably matters less than it feels.
  6. Before deciding, check the weights with someone who disagrees. Weights are opinions, and a second view catches ones that are inflated.
  7. Write down the final decision and the top reasons. This makes it easier to revisit later and explain to others.

History

Who created it

Benjamin Franklin described a weighted pros and cons method in a letter to the chemist Joseph Priestley dated 19 September 1772, calling it 'moral or prudential algebra.' People certainly listed pros and cons before Franklin, but his letter is the best-known early written description of weighing and cancelling them.

What it was created for

Priestley had asked Franklin for advice on whether to take a job as librarian and companion to Lord Shelburne. Franklin did not tell him what to do; he gave him a method for deciding.

What it is used for

People use weighted pros and cons for personal and group decisions where several factors matter but some matter more than others. It is a simple ancestor of the decision matrices used in engineering and business.

Industries Personal decision-makingEngineering design reviewsBusiness managementEducationCounseling and coaching

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • For choosing among several options, turn it into a decision matrix: list criteria, weight each criterion, and score every option against it.
  • Watch for the same reason appearing twice under different names, which double counts it.
  • If you find yourself changing weights to get the answer you want, that tells you your real preference.
  • Franklin admitted the weights cannot be exact, but said the method helps you judge better and avoid rash decisions.
Watch out

A list of many small pros can outweigh one fatal con. Read the heaviest item on each side.

Rambots example (made up)

The team is deciding whether to buy a second set of swerve modules for a practice robot. Pros: more driver practice (5), spare parts at events (4), programmers can test without stealing the comp bot (4). Cons: about $2,000 out of the grant budget (5), more maintenance (2). After cancelling, the pros win by 6 points, so the business lead adds it to the next grant request.

RICE Scoring

20-30 min · 2-6 · Well documented

Prioritize features or projects by Reach, Impact, Confidence, and Effort.

What it is

Created at Intercom for product teams. RICE score = Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort. It rewards things that help many people a lot, that you are sure about, and that are cheap.

When to use it

Choosing between software features, scouting app improvements, or team projects.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Reach: how many people or matches it affects in a set period.
  2. Impact: 3 massive, 2 high, 1 medium, 0.5 low, 0.25 minimal.
  3. Confidence: how sure you are about those numbers.
  4. Effort: person-weeks. The highest score goes first.
In depth
  1. List the candidate projects, each described clearly enough that people agree on what is included. You cannot estimate effort for a fuzzy project.
  2. Reach: estimate how many people or events the project affects in a set time period (for example, matches per event or students per month). Use real numbers when possible so the score is not just a guess.
  3. Impact: score the effect on each person or event using McBride's scale: 3 massive, 2 high, 1 medium, 0.5 low, 0.25 minimal. A fixed scale keeps people from inventing huge numbers.
  4. Confidence: rate how sure you are about reach and impact: 100 percent (solid data), 80 percent (some data), or 50 percent (mostly a hunch). This lowers the score of exciting ideas that have no evidence.
  5. Effort: estimate total work in person-months (or person-weeks for a team), counting everyone who will work on it. Effort goes on the bottom of the formula, so cheap wins rise to the top.
  6. Calculate RICE = (Reach x Impact x Confidence) / Effort, then sort from high to low. Comparing the numbers makes trade-offs visible.
  7. Review the ranking as a group and override it when there is a good reason, such as a hard deadline or a safety need. McBride said the score should guide decisions, not make them.

History

Who created it

Created by Sean McBride on the product team at the software company Intercom, and described in his Intercom blog post 'RICE: Simple prioritization for product managers' (January 2018).

What it was created for

Intercom's product team needed a consistent way to compare very different project ideas, so that the loudest or most excited person's idea did not win by default.

What it is used for

Product and project teams use it to rank backlogs and roadmaps by expected value per unit of effort. It is also used for marketing experiments and internal process improvements.

Industries Software product managementStartupsMarketing and growth teamsUX designProject managementOperations

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Keep the same time period for Reach across all projects, or the scores are not comparable.
  • Scores below 50 percent confidence usually mean you should run a quick test before scoring again.
  • Write down the reasoning for each number. Later, when results come in, you can see which estimates were off and improve.
  • For an FRC team, measure effort in student-weeks, since build season time is the real limit.
Watch out

Confidence is the easiest number to inflate. Use 50% when you are guessing.

Rambots example (made up)

During build season, programming scores three projects. Auto-align for scoring reaches 12 matches per event, impact 2, confidence 80 percent, effort 2 student-weeks, so RICE is 9.6. A new driver dashboard reaches 12 matches, impact 0.5, confidence 100 percent, effort 1, so RICE is 6. A full path-planning rewrite reaches 12, impact 3, confidence 50 percent, effort 6, so RICE is 3, and the team does auto-align first.

Kano Model

20-30 min · 2-6 · Well documented

Sort features into must-haves, performance features, and delighters.

What it is

Noriaki Kano's model. For each feature, ask two questions: how do you feel if it is there, and how do you feel if it is missing? The pair of answers tells you what kind of feature it is.

When to use it

Choosing robot features or scouting app features, or planning an outreach event experience.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. List the features.
  2. Answer both questions from the user's point of view (driver, scout, visitor).
  3. Must-be features are required. Performance features scale with effort. Attractive ones are delighters. Indifferent ones can go.
In depth
  1. Name the customer before listing anything (drive team, alliance partners, judges, sponsors). Categories change depending on who you ask, so mixing groups in one survey blurs the results.
  2. List 10 to 20 specific candidate features, written so a respondent can picture them ('auto-align to the scoring target', not 'better vision'). Vague features get vague answers.
  3. For each feature, ask two questions: 'How would you feel if the robot had this?' and 'How would you feel if it did not?' Offer the same five answers for both: I like it, I expect it, I am neutral, I can live with it, I dislike it. Asking both sides reveals the asymmetry that a single importance rating hides.
  4. Survey at least 10 to 15 people per customer group and keep wording neutral. Leading questions push everything into 'attractive'.
  5. Use the standard Kano evaluation table to turn each answer pair into a category (Attractive, One-dimensional, Must-be, Indifferent, Reverse, or Questionable), then take the most common category for each feature.
  6. Calculate two coefficients per feature: satisfaction = (A+O)/(A+O+M+I) and dissatisfaction = -(O+M)/(A+O+M+I). Plotting them shows which features move people most in each direction.
  7. Prioritize in order: cover every must-be first, then invest in performance features based on how competitive you need to be, then add one or two attractive features if time allows. Drop indifferent features to free up build hours.
  8. Repeat the survey each season. Attractive features decay into must-bes as everyone adopts them, so last year's priorities go stale.

History

Who created it

Developed by Noriaki Kano, a quality management professor at the Tokyo University of Science, with colleagues Seraku, Takahashi, and Tsuji. Their paper 'Attractive Quality and Must-Be Quality' was published in April 1984 in the journal of the Japanese Society for Quality Control, building on work Kano's group did in the late 1970s and early 1980s.

What it was created for

Kano challenged the common assumption that customer satisfaction rises in a straight line as quality improves. He wanted to show that some features only cause anger when missing, while others cause delight when present but are not missed when absent.

What it is used for

Product teams use it to sort possible features into must-be, performance (one-dimensional), attractive, indifferent, and reverse categories so they can decide what to build first. It is usually run as a short survey with paired questions about each feature.

Industries Product managementSoftware and UX designAutomotiveConsumer electronicsHospitality and servicesHealthcare service design

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • A 'Questionable' result (someone likes having it and likes not having it) usually means the question was confusing. Rewrite it rather than counting it.
  • Respondents cannot react to features they cannot imagine, so show a sketch, CAD screenshot, or match video clip with each question.
  • For a quick version, have leads place each feature on a 2x2 of 'missing it hurts' versus 'having it delights' before running a full survey.
  • Swerve drive is a good teaching case: it went from a rare delighter to close to a must-be for competitive FRC teams in a few seasons.
Watch out

Answer from the user's point of view (driver, scout, visitor), not the builder's.

Rambots example (made up)

After kickoff, the strategy subteam lists 15 robot features and surveys 14 drive team members, scouts, and mentors with the paired questions. A drivetrain that never browns out comes back as must-be, teleop cycle speed as one-dimensional, a three-piece autonomous routine as attractive, and LED status lights as indifferent. The team commits to drivetrain reliability first, puts most build hours into cycle speed, and treats the three-piece auto as a stretch goal for the second regional.

Decision Tree and Expected Value

20-30 min · 2-5 · Well documented

Compare risky options by multiplying each outcome by its chance.

What it is

Each option has possible outcomes, each with a chance and a value. Expected value adds up chance × value. It shows which option pays off best on average, and the tree shows how risky each one is.

When to use it

Strategy calls: which autonomous routine to run, whether to attempt a risky climb, which award to aim for.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. List the options.
  2. For each option, list outcomes with a chance (adding to 100%) and a value.
  3. Compare expected values, then ask whether the team can live with the worst outcome.
In depth
  1. Write the decision as one sentence with a deadline and draw a square (decision node) on the left. One decision per tree keeps it readable.
  2. Draw a branch for each real option, including 'do nothing' or 'keep doing what we do now'. The status quo is the baseline every other option has to beat.
  3. After each option, add a circle (chance node) for each uncertain event that matters, with one branch per possible outcome. Outcomes at a node must not overlap, and their probabilities must add to 100%.
  4. Estimate probabilities from data where you can (practice logs, scouting data, past seasons) and write next to each number where it came from. Visible sources let others challenge weak guesses.
  5. Put a payoff at the end of every branch in one consistent unit (match points, ranking points, build hours, or dollars), with costs already subtracted. Mixing units makes the math meaningless.
  6. Roll back from right to left: at each chance node multiply each payoff by its probability and add them up; at each decision node keep the branch with the best expected value.
  7. Run a sensitivity check on the shakiest probability by moving it up and down until the best choice flips. That break-even number tells you how much the decision depends on a guess.
  8. Look at the worst case as well as the average. Expected value assumes you get many tries, but a single elimination match is a one-shot bet.

History

Who created it

Expected value goes back to Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat, who worked out how to fairly split the stakes of an interrupted game in letters in 1654; Christiaan Huygens published the first treatise on it in 1657. Decision trees as a business tool have no single inventor and spread in the 1960s with decision analysis: John F. Magee's 1964 Harvard Business Review article 'Decision Trees for Decision Making' introduced them to managers, Ronald Howard coined the term 'decision analysis' in 1966, and Howard Raiffa's 1968 book 'Decision Analysis' taught the method widely.

What it was created for

Expected value was invented to settle a gambling dispute about dividing a pot fairly. Decision trees were built to help managers lay out a chain of choices and uncertain outcomes so risky investments could be compared with numbers instead of gut feel.

What it is used for

People use them to compare options whose payoffs depend on chance, such as investments, medical treatments, project go/no-go calls, and game strategy. (Decision trees in machine learning are a related but separate tool.)

Industries Business strategy and financeOil and gas explorationHealthcare and medical decision makingEngineering project managementInsurancePharmaceutical R&DSports strategy

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Report the break-even probability as the main result, for example 'attempt the climb if our success rate is above 50%'. It is easier to act on than a list of expected values.
  • Build the tree in a spreadsheet so you can update probabilities with real scouting numbers after each event.
  • Prune branches that have both a tiny probability and a tiny payoff so the tree fits on one page.
  • When a bad outcome is unrecoverable (like being eliminated), weigh the downside more heavily than plain expected value does.
Watch out

Expected value hides risk. Two options with the same average can have very different worst cases.

Rambots example (made up)

With 20 seconds left in a playoff match, the drive coach can attempt a deep climb (12 points, 60% success in practice logs), keep cycling (scouting shows about two more pieces at 3 points each), or park (2 points, 98% success). Expected values are 7.2 for the climb, 6.0 for cycling, and about 2.0 for parking, so the climb wins. The break-even point is 50% climb success, so if the climb drops below that at a new venue, the plan switches to cycling.

Harada Chart

30-45 min · Solo · Partly documented

Turn one big goal into 8 pillars and 64 concrete actions.

What it is

Takashi Harada, a Japanese track coach and teacher, built this 9 by 9 chart. One goal sits in the middle, 8 pillars support it, and each pillar gets 8 actions. Shohei Ohtani filled one out at 16; his pillars included control, speed, mentality, and luck, and his luck actions were things like picking up trash and greeting people.

When to use it

Personal season goals, or a team goal at the start of the year.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write one goal in the red center square. Make it specific and give it a date.
  2. Write 8 pillars in the blue squares around it. Each one copies out to its own block.
  3. Fill each block with 8 small, checkable actions: "run 10 sim matches a week," not "get better at driving."
  4. As a team: project it and have one person type what the room agrees on.
In depth
  1. Write one central goal in the middle square of a 9x9 grid. Make it a single, clear outcome with a time frame, because everything else branches from it.
  2. Fill the 8 squares around the center with the 8 areas you must develop to reach the goal. Include non-technical areas (character, habits, luck) as Ohtani did, since these are often what separates success from failure.
  3. Copy each of the 8 areas into the center of one of the 8 outer 3x3 blocks. This creates 8 smaller goals, each with its own block.
  4. Fill the 8 cells around each area with specific actions or measures, for a total of 64. Write behaviors you can actually do or check, such as "30 minutes of CAD practice on weekdays," not wishes like "get better at CAD."
  5. Mark which of the 64 are daily habits and which are one-time tasks or milestones. Harada's method pairs the chart with a routine check sheet and a daily journal, and the habits are what get tracked.
  6. Put the chart where you see it daily and review it with a mentor or coach on a regular schedule. The chart is a starting plan; Ohtani reportedly made many updated versions as his goals changed.
  7. Rewrite the chart when the goal is reached or changes. Old charts are useful records of what worked.

History

Who created it

The Open Window 64 chart is part of the Harada Method, developed by Takashi Harada, a Japanese junior high school physical education teacher and track coach in Osaka whose students won national championships; he founded the Harada Education Institute in 2001. Shohei Ohtani filled one out at about age 16 at Hanamaki Higashi High School, with the center goal of being picked in the first round of the draft by all 8 NPB teams. The nested 3x3 grid shape is older: Japanese "mandala chart" thinking tools from the late 1970s and 1980s use the same layout, so calling Ohtani's chart a mandala chart is common but not quite accurate.

What it was created for

Teaching students to become self-reliant by turning one large goal into concrete daily habits they could track, first in school athletics and then in Japanese companies.

What it is used for

Personal and team goal setting, athlete development, and employee training. Since Ohtani's success it has become popular as a way to break a big goal into 64 specific actions.

Industries Sports coachingEducationManufacturing and lean managementCorporate trainingPersonal development coaching

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Filling all 64 cells is hard on purpose. If you get stuck, ask a teammate or mentor what they see you missing.
  • Include "service" actions (helping others, cleaning the shop), which are part of Harada's emphasis on character and are easy to skip.
  • For a team version, the center is the season goal and each outer block belongs to a subteam lead who fills it in.
  • Pick 3 to 5 of the 64 as daily habits at first. Tracking all of them at once usually fails within a couple of weeks.
Watch out

64 actions is a lot. A chart nobody looks at again is wasted: pick a few actions to track weekly.

Rambots example (made up)

A sophomore who wants to be drive team captain next season puts that goal in the center and picks 8 areas: driving skill, game knowledge, robot knowledge, communication, scouting, fitness, reliability, and leadership. Under driving skill she writes actions like "20 minutes of practice field driving twice a week" and "run the cycle-time drill and log the time," and she reviews the chart with the drive coach every two weeks.

SMART Goal

10-15 min · Solo · Well documented

Rewrite a vague goal so it is specific, measurable, and dated.

What it is

Start with a rough goal, then test it against five questions. The rewrite at the bottom is the goal you actually commit to.

When to use it

Setting a personal or subteam goal, or when a goal keeps slipping because nobody can tell if it is done.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write the goal the way you would say it out loud.
  2. Answer each letter.
  3. Combine the answers into one sentence at the bottom.
In depth
  1. Start with the rough goal in plain words, such as "get better at autonomous." It is fine for the first draft to be vague; the checklist does the sharpening.
  2. Make it Specific by naming exactly what changes and where. "Score two game pieces in autonomous" is specific; "improve auto" is not.
  3. Make it Measurable by naming the number and how you will check it, such as "in 8 of 10 practice runs, logged on the practice sheet." If you cannot say how you will check it, you will not know when you are done.
  4. Make it Assignable (Doran's original A) by naming one owner. A goal owned by "the team" usually has no one pushing it.
  5. Make it Realistic by checking it against people, hours, money, and parts you actually have. A goal that needs a second CNC router you do not own is not realistic.
  6. Make it Time-related by giving a deadline tied to the calendar, such as "by the Saturday before our first regional." The deadline creates check-in points.
  7. Add one or two checkpoints between now and the deadline, and write the goal where the team will see it. SMART goals still fail if no one looks at them until the due date.

History

Who created it

Introduced by George T. Doran in a November 1981 article in Management Review titled "There's a S.M.A.R.T. way to write management's goals and objectives." His original letters were Specific, Measurable, Assignable, Realistic, and Time-related; later writers changed A to Achievable or Attainable and R to Relevant.

What it was created for

Helping managers write corporate objectives that were clear enough to act on and check, at a time when management by objectives was popular but many written goals were vague.

What it is used for

Writing goals for individuals, teams, projects, grants, lesson plans, and performance reviews. It is the most common checklist for turning a wish into a goal.

Industries Business managementEducationHealthcare and public healthNonprofits and grant writingHuman resourcesCoaching and sports

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Use the original "Assignable" alongside "Achievable." Naming an owner fixes more goals than any other single change.
  • Doran himself wrote that not every objective has to meet all five criteria. Use it as a checklist, not a rule that blocks reasonable goals.
  • Pair one ambitious outcome goal with SMART process goals (practice hours, drills run) that the team fully controls.
  • Write goals as a single sentence. If it needs "and" twice, it is probably two goals.
Watch out

A SMART goal can still be the wrong goal. Check it actually matters before polishing it.

Rambots example (made up)

The outreach lead turns "do more outreach" into: "Maya will run 4 robot demos at elementary schools, reaching at least 200 students, by March 1, tracked in the outreach log." The team checks progress at the January 15 leads meeting, sees only one demo booked, and assigns two members to call schools that week.

Ikigai

15-20 min · Solo · Well documented

Find the role where what you love, what you are good at, and what is needed overlap.

What it is

Four circles: what you love, what you are good at, what the team or world needs, and what you can be paid or recognized for. Where they overlap is a role worth growing into.

When to use it

A student choosing a subteam or leadership role, or a senior thinking about what comes after.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Fill the four circles first. Lists are fine.
  2. Look for things that show up in two circles and write the overlaps.
  3. Write one sentence in the center.
In depth
  1. Draw four overlapping circles labeled: what you love, what you are good at, what the world needs, and what you can be paid for (or, for a team member, what the team needs and will recognize). Explain up front that this is a Western purpose diagram so no one treats it as the full Japanese concept.
  2. Fill "what you love" with specific activities, not categories. "Tuning PID loops until the arm stops oscillating" tells you more than "programming."
  3. Fill "what you are good at" with evidence, such as what teammates ask you for help with or what you have done well in competition. This keeps it honest.
  4. Fill "what the world needs" with real needs you have seen, from the team, school, or community. This circle connects personal interest to service.
  5. Fill "what you can be paid for" (or what the team values) with roles, jobs, or responsibilities that actually exist. For students, think internships, majors, or team roles, not only salaries.
  6. Look for items that appear in two or three circles. The overlaps (passion, mission, profession, vocation in the common version) show where to try things next, not a final answer.
  7. Pick one small experiment from an overlap, such as leading a subteam workshop, and revisit the diagram after trying it. Purpose usually becomes clear by doing, not by drawing.

History

Who created it

The four-circle ikigai Venn diagram is a Western creation, not a traditional Japanese tool. In 2014 blogger Marc Winn placed the word "ikigai" in the center of a purpose Venn diagram made earlier by Andres Zuzunaga, and the combined image spread widely. The Japanese word ikigai means roughly "a reason for living" and often refers to small daily joys, not career planning.

What it was created for

Zuzunaga's diagram was meant to show how a sense of purpose comes from the overlap of what you love, what you are good at, what the world needs, and what you can be paid for. Winn's blog post used it to talk about finding meaning in life and work.

What it is used for

Career reflection, choosing a path, and team or personal values exercises. Schools and coaches use it to help people talk about what they enjoy and are good at.

Industries Career counselingEducationLife and executive coachingHuman resourcesPersonal development

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Pair it with the traditional idea of ikigai by asking, "What small thing makes you look forward to a build day?" That often surfaces more honest answers.
  • Do it individually first, then share in pairs. Group brainstorming tends to push people toward answers that sound good.
  • For high school students, swap "paid for" with "what someone would trust you with," since most are not paid yet.
  • Expect it to change. Redo it at the end of each season.
Watch out

It is a reflection tool, not a career test. Keep it light with younger students.

Rambots example (made up)

At the end-of-season retreat, each member fills in the four circles. A junior finds that "teaching CAD to rookies" shows up under love, good at, and what the team needs, so the leads make her the fall CAD training lead with a goal of getting 6 rookies through the Onshape basics course before kickoff.

5W1H

10 min · 1-6 · Well documented

Describe a situation completely before you jump to solutions.

What it is

Who, What, When, Where, Why, and How. Six questions that make sure a problem or plan is fully described before anyone argues about fixes.

When to use it

Writing up an incident, planning an event, or briefing someone who was not there.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write the topic.
  2. Answer all six with facts, not guesses.
  3. Mark anything you are not sure about with a question mark and find out.
In depth
  1. Write the topic or problem at the top in one sentence. All six questions are asked about that one thing, so a fuzzy topic gives fuzzy answers.
  2. Answer What: describe exactly what happened or what is planned, using observable facts. For a failure, say what broke, not what you think caused it.
  3. Answer Who: name the people involved, affected, or responsible. This shows who to talk to and who owns the next step.
  4. Answer When and Where: give the time, match number, location on the robot, or place in the schedule. Precise time and location often point straight at the cause.
  5. Answer Why: list the reasons or suspected causes, and mark which are confirmed and which are guesses. For deeper causes, follow up with a 5 Whys analysis.
  6. Answer How: describe how it happened or how the plan will be carried out, step by step. This is where gaps in a plan usually show up.
  7. Review the six answers for blanks and contradictions, then turn the gaps into action items. A blank "who" or "when" in a plan means it is not ready to announce.

History

Who created it

No single inventor. The questions trace to the "elements of circumstance" in Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics and were used as rhetorical tools by Hermagoras of Temnos and later Roman and medieval writers. Rudyard Kipling's 1902 poem "I keep six honest serving-men" (in Just So Stories) made the list of What, Why, When, How, Where, and Who famous, and journalism teaching adopted the Five Ws by the early 1900s.

What it was created for

Ancient writers used the circumstances to judge actions and to build legal and rhetorical arguments: who did what, when, where, why, and how. Journalism later used them to make sure a news story covered the basic facts.

What it is used for

Gathering facts, writing reports, scoping projects, investigating problems, and checking that a plan or announcement is complete. In manufacturing and quality work it is a standard first step in problem analysis.

Industries JournalismManufacturing and quality managementLaw and investigationProject managementEducationIncident response

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Some versions add "How much" (5W2H) for cost or quantity, which is useful for budget and parts requests.
  • For event logistics announcements, use 5W1H as a checklist before you post. Most follow-up questions from parents come from a missing W.
  • Separate facts from guesses with different colors or a "(?)" mark so the team does not act on an assumption.
  • Ask "Who else?" and "Where else?" to check whether a problem is happening in more than one place.
Watch out

Answers must be facts. Mark guesses with a question mark and go find out.

Rambots example (made up)

After the robot browns out in quarterfinal 2, the electrical lead writes a 5W1H: What, the roboRIO rebooted and the robot stopped for 6 seconds; Who, noticed by the drive coach and logged by the pit crew; When and Where, 1:42 into the match while climbing against the far wall; Why (suspected), the battery dropped below 7 volts under the climber and drive motors at once; How, current spikes stacked when the driver drove and climbed together. The team adds a current limit on the climber and a battery voltage check before each match.

SWOT

20-30 min · 3-10 · Partly documented

Size up strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats.

What it is

Strengths and weaknesses are inside the team; opportunities and threats come from outside. The strategy box is where you use one to handle another.

When to use it

Season planning, grant writing, or deciding whether to take on something new.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Fill the four boxes. One item per line.
  2. Be honest in Weaknesses. It stays in the room.
  3. Write strategies: use a strength to grab an opportunity, or fix a weakness before a threat hits.
In depth
  1. State the goal or decision the SWOT serves, such as "plan next season" or "decide whether to attend a second regional." A SWOT with no goal produces a list nobody uses.
  2. List Strengths: internal things the team does well or has, backed by evidence like match data, awards, or sponsor retention. Evidence keeps it from becoming a list of compliments.
  3. List Weaknesses: internal gaps, such as few trained machinists or a fragile drivetrain. Be blunt; this quadrant is only useful if it is honest.
  4. List Opportunities: outside factors the team could use, such as a new local sponsor, a new grant, or a game that favors a design you are good at. These are external and not fully in your control.
  5. List Threats: outside factors that could hurt, such as rising registration fees, a lost shop space, or stronger teams at your event. Keep internal problems in Weaknesses, not here.
  6. Cut each quadrant to its top 3 to 5 items by impact. Long lists hide the items that matter.
  7. Turn the grid into actions by pairing quadrants: use strengths to grab opportunities, fix weaknesses that threats would exploit. This step (sometimes called a TOWS matrix) is where SWOT becomes a plan.

History

Who created it

Disputed and often oversimplified. Recent historical research traces it to a 1960s long-range planning research group at Stanford Research Institute that used a framework called SOFT (Satisfactory, Opportunities, Faults, Threats), later renamed SWOT; Albert Humphrey, a member of that group, is often named as the creator, though it was a group effort. Similar strengths and weaknesses thinking was also taught at Harvard Business School in the 1960s, and the familiar 2x2 grid layout appeared in John Argenti's 1974 book Systematic Corporate Planning.

What it was created for

Helping company leaders with long-range corporate planning by looking at what was good and bad now (internal) and what was good and bad ahead (external), at a time when many corporate plans were failing.

What it is used for

Strategic planning, reviewing a season or project, preparing grant and business plans, and comparing an organization to its competitors.

Industries Corporate strategyMarketingNonprofitsEducation administrationSmall businessHealthcare management

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Run it with people outside the leadership group, including rookies and mentors. Leaders tend to underrate weaknesses they are close to.
  • Write each item as a specific statement. "Programming" is not a strength; "auto scored in 90 percent of qualification matches" is.
  • Do it after a debrief survey so it starts from data, not memory.
  • Revisit last year's SWOT before writing a new one and check which threats actually happened.
Watch out

Lists alone change nothing. The strategy box is the point.

Rambots example (made up)

At the spring debrief the leads run a SWOT. Strengths: reliable swerve drivetrain and strong scouting. Weaknesses: only two students can run the CNC router. Opportunities: a local machine shop offered training. Threats: three senior machinists graduate in June. The pairing leads to an action: schedule summer CNC training at the shop for four sophomores.

RACI Chart

15-20 min · Leads · Partly documented

Make it clear who does the work and who owns the outcome.

What it is

For every task, mark who is Responsible (does it), Accountable (owns it, exactly one person), Consulted (asked first), and Informed (told after). The Check column catches tasks with no owner.

When to use it

Kickoff, event prep, or any time something fell through because "I thought you had it."

How to use it

Quick version
  1. List tasks or decisions as rows and people or roles as columns.
  2. Click cells to assign letters.
  3. Fix every flag: each task needs exactly one A and at least one R.
In depth
  1. List deliverables or decisions down the left side at a level that can actually be finished, such as 'intake roller CAD released' rather than 'intake'. Vague rows produce vague ownership.
  2. List people across the top. On a small team use names instead of roles, because roles hide the fact that one person is holding five jobs.
  3. Give each row exactly one A (Accountable): the person who signs off and answers for the result. Two A's on a row means nobody owns it when it goes wrong.
  4. Mark R (Responsible) for whoever does the work. The A can also be the R on small tasks, but every row needs at least one R or the work will not happen.
  5. Mark C (Consulted) for people whose input is needed before the work is done (a two-way conversation) and I (Informed) for people who only need the result (one-way). Keep C's few, since each one adds a meeting or a wait.
  6. Read down each column. A person with many R's and A's is a bottleneck, and a person with no letters may be underused. Rebalance before build season gets busy.
  7. Read across each row. Too many C's slows decisions, and a row with no I's often means the drive team or business team gets surprised later. Fix the gaps and post the chart where everyone can see it.

History

Who created it

There is no single inventor. Responsibility charts (sometimes called linear responsibility charts) show up in project management around the 1950s, and the name 'responsibility assignment matrix' became common in the 1970s. It is now standard material in PMI's PMBOK Guide.

What it was created for

Large projects where many people and departments touched the same work, and nobody was sure who owned a decision or who needed to be told about it.

What it is used for

Making clear who does the work, who owns the result, who gives input, and who only needs updates on any task that crosses subteams.

Industries Project managementSoftware developmentConstructionIT service managementHealthcare administrationConsulting

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Variants exist for different needs: RASCI adds S for Support, and DACI (Driver, Approver, Contributors, Informed) works better for one-off decisions than for ongoing tasks.
  • Revisit the chart at milestones (end of kickoff week, before the first event), because roles shift as the robot moves from design to fabrication to drive practice.
  • The A only works if that person really has the authority. If a student lead is listed as A but a mentor overrides every call, the chart is fiction; write down what is actually true.
  • Keep it to one page. If it needs scrolling, split it by subteam or by mechanism.
Watch out

More than one A means nobody owns it. A long row of Rs means too many cooks.

Rambots example (made up)

For the climber, a team made a RACI where the mechanical lead is A for the design review, two students are R for CAD and prototyping, the programming lead is C because motor and sensor choices affect code, and the drive coach is I for the expected climb time. Reading the rows, they found three tasks with no A at all (gearbox ordering, wiring harness, and bumper fabric) and assigned owners before the week 2 ordering deadline instead of finding the gap in the shop.

OKRs

30-45 min · Subteam · Well documented

Set objectives with measurable key results and track progress.

What it is

Objectives and Key Results. An objective is where you want to go, in words. Key results are 2 to 5 numbers that prove you got there. Progress is tracked from a starting value to a target.

When to use it

Season or semester goals for the team or a subteam.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write 1 to 3 objectives. Keep them short and motivating.
  2. Give each 2 to 5 key results with a start, a target, and the current value.
  3. Update Current every few weeks. Around 70% is a good result: 100% every time means the targets were too easy.
In depth
  1. Pick 2 to 4 Objectives for the period (a season or a quarter). An Objective is qualitative and motivating, such as 'Become a reliable playoff alliance pick'; too many Objectives means nothing is a priority.
  2. Write 2 to 5 Key Results under each Objective as numbers with a deadline, such as 'Average 8 game pieces per match by our second regional.' Grove's test is that at the end you can answer yes or no without debate.
  3. Measure outcomes, not activities. 'Hold 10 drive practices' is a task; 'Cut average cycle time to 9 seconds' is a result, and the tasks belong in the plan underneath it.
  4. Mark which Key Results are stretch goals and which are firm commitments. A common convention is that hitting about 70% of a stretch goal counts as good, but that only works if everyone knows which kind each one is.
  5. Give each Key Result one owner and post the full set where the whole team can see it. Public OKRs let subteams line up their own goals and spot conflicts early.
  6. Check in weekly or every two weeks with a quick confidence score (for example 0 to 10) on each Key Result. Short check-ins catch slipping goals while there is still time to react.
  7. Grade every Key Result at the end of the period and write down what you learned before setting the next set. The grading conversation is where the team gets better at setting goals.

History

Who created it

Andy Grove developed OKRs at Intel in the 1970s, building on Peter Drucker's Management by Objectives, and described the approach in his 1983 book High Output Management. John Doerr learned the system from Grove at Intel in 1975, introduced it to Google around 1999 while at the venture firm Kleiner Perkins, and popularized it widely with his 2018 book Measure What Matters.

What it was created for

Grove wanted to focus a fast-growing chip company on a few priorities and make progress measurable, so anyone could check whether a goal was met without argument.

What it is used for

Organizations use OKRs to set a small number of ambitious goals for a quarter or season and track them with measurable results, so every group pulls toward the same priorities.

Industries TechnologyStartupsVenture capitalNonprofitsEducationGovernment

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Keep OKRs separate from judging individual students. When roles or recognition depend on hitting them, people set easy targets.
  • Let subteams draft their own OKRs that connect to the team Objectives instead of handing them down. Ownership raises follow-through.
  • If a Key Result could be hit without moving the Objective, rewrite it. This is the most common OKR mistake.
Watch out

Key results must be measurable outcomes, not task lists. "Hold 3 trainings" is a task; "6 students certified" is a result.

Rambots example (made up)

Objective: 'Fund the season without last-minute scrambling.' Key Results: secure $25,000 in sponsorships by December 15, submit 4 grant applications by January 10, and get 3 returning sponsors to commit before kickoff. 12.8

WOOP

10-15 min · Solo · Well documented

Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan: a goal method that plans for what will get in your way.

What it is

Developed by psychologist Gabriele Oettingen. Positive thinking alone can make people less likely to act. WOOP pairs the dream with your real inner obstacle, then makes an if-then plan for it.

When to use it

Personal goals for students, especially ones that keep slipping.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Wish: something challenging but doable.
  2. Outcome: imagine the best result. Really picture it.
  3. Obstacle: what inside you gets in the way? Not other people.
  4. Plan: "If [obstacle], then I will [action]."
In depth
  1. Wish: name one goal that is challenging but possible in a set time (for example, 'finish the intake CAD by Friday'). If it is too easy or impossible, the method has nothing to work with.
  2. Outcome: picture the best result of reaching it, in detail, for about a minute. This builds the motivation you will need.
  3. Obstacle: picture the main thing inside you that will get in the way (procrastination, getting distracted, avoiding asking for help). Focus on what you control, not on bad luck or other people.
  4. Plan: write an if-then sentence: 'If [obstacle happens], then I will [specific action].' The if-then form makes the response automatic when the moment comes.
  5. Make the 'then' small and concrete, like 'open Onshape and work for 20 minutes' instead of 'try harder.' Vague plans do not trigger action.
  6. Do it in writing, alone, in about five minutes, and reread the plan before the situation comes up. The research version is quick and private; group pressure changes how honestly people name obstacles.
  7. If the obstacle cannot be beaten, change or drop the wish. Mental contrasting is meant to help you let go of unrealistic goals as well as commit to realistic ones.

History

Who created it

Psychologist Gabriele Oettingen developed mental contrasting from her research on positive fantasies, starting in the late 1990s, and combined it with implementation intentions, an if-then planning method from psychologist Peter Gollwitzer. The combination is called MCII; the easier name WOOP (Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan) was popularized with her 2014 book Rethinking Positive Thinking.

What it was created for

Oettingen found that just imagining a good outcome can make people less likely to work for it, because the brain feels like it already got the reward. WOOP was designed to keep the positive goal but force people to face the obstacle and plan for it.

What it is used for

People use it to set personal goals, build habits, and follow through on hard tasks. Schools and health programs use it to help with studying, exercise, and behavior change.

Industries EducationHealthcare and wellnessCoachingSports psychologyWorkplace trainingBehavioral research

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • The order matters. Thinking about the outcome first and then the obstacle works better than the reverse in Oettingen's research.
  • Find your 'inner' obstacle by asking 'what is it in me that stops me?' Most people first name an outside cause.
  • Use it before a known hard moment, such as the week before a competition or the night before a deadline.
  • Leads can teach it by doing one WOOP out loud with their own goal, so newer members see that admitting an obstacle is normal.
Watch out

The obstacle must be inside you ("I procrastinate"), not other people.

Rambots example (made up)

A programmer wants to get the vision-aligned auto working before the Sacramento Regional. Outcome: the robot scores 3 game pieces in auto and the team climbs in rankings. Obstacle: she keeps tweaking unrelated code instead of testing the hard part. Plan: 'If I open a different file during shop time, then I will close it and run one vision test on the practice field.'

GROW Coaching

20-30 min · Pairs · Partly documented

Goal, Reality, Options, Will: a coaching conversation for mentors and leads.

What it is

A coaching model used by managers and mentors. The coach asks questions instead of giving answers, and the person being coached does the thinking.

When to use it

Mentor one-on-ones with leads, or leads checking in with rookies.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Goal: what do you want from this conversation and this season?
  2. Reality: where are things now? Facts, not judgments.
  3. Options: what could you do? Get at least three.
  4. Will: what will you do, and by when?
In depth
  1. Goal: ask what the person wants to get out of this conversation and out of the situation. A clear, specific goal ('be able to wire a PDH alone by week 3') keeps the talk focused.
  2. Reality: ask what is happening now, what they have tried, and what got in the way. Push for facts and examples, because people often jump to solutions before they understand the problem.
  3. Options: ask what they could do, and keep asking 'what else?' until there are at least three ideas. The person's own ideas are more likely to get done than ones you hand them.
  4. Will (or Way forward): ask which option they will do, when, and what support they need. Getting a specific first step and date turns the talk into action.
  5. Ask for a commitment rating from 1 to 10. If it is under 7, ask what would raise it, since low commitment predicts the step will not happen.
  6. Mostly ask, do not tell. Use open questions starting with 'what' and 'how' and save advice for when they are truly stuck.
  7. Follow up at the agreed time. Coaching without a check-in feels nice but rarely changes behavior.

History

Who created it

Developed in the UK business coaching world in the late 1980s, with disputed credit. John Whitmore said Max Landsberg coined the name during a conversation with Graham Alexander, and Whitmore was first to publish it in Coaching for Performance (1992). Alan Fine later said he co-developed it with Whitmore and Alexander.

What it was created for

It gave managers and coaches a simple structure for a coaching conversation, so they could help someone think through a problem by asking questions instead of giving orders.

What it is used for

Managers, mentors, and coaches use it for one-on-ones, goal-setting talks, and helping someone get unstuck. It is one of the most widely used coaching models.

Industries Corporate managementExecutive coachingSports coachingEducation and mentoringHealthcareHuman resources

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • You can loop back. If Reality shows the goal was wrong, go back to Goal before moving on.
  • Some versions read O as Obstacles, then Options. Naming obstacles first can make the options more realistic.
  • For quick hallway coaching, a two-minute GROW with one question per step still works.
  • Mentors coaching students should resist fixing the robot for them. The Options step is where students learn to solve it themselves.
Watch out

The coach asks; the learner answers. If the coach starts giving answers, it becomes a lecture.

Rambots example (made up)

A new electrical lead is frustrated that the robot keeps browning out. Goal: zero brownouts in the next practice match. Reality: battery voltage drops to 6.8 V when the shooter and drivetrain run together, and the team has no battery tracking. Options: current limits, a battery log, fewer motors spinning at once. Will: she sets 40 A current limits on the drive motors and starts a battery log by Thursday.

Gap Analysis

30-45 min · 3-6 · Partly documented

Where we are, where we want to be, and what closes the gap.

What it is

For each area, describe the current state and the desired state honestly. The gap between them becomes the action list.

When to use it

Season planning, after a debrief, or before writing a grant.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Pick a few areas that matter.
  2. Describe current and desired states with facts or numbers.
  3. Write the actions that close each gap, with an owner.
In depth
  1. Pick one area and define the desired state in measurable terms (for example, 'cycle time under 8 seconds' or 'four students trained on the CNC router'). A vague target makes the gap impossible to measure.
  2. Measure the current state honestly with real data, such as match videos, scouting numbers, or a skills checklist. Guessing the current state is the most common way gap analyses go wrong.
  3. List each gap as the difference between the two (for example, 'cycle time is 11 s, target is 8 s, gap is 3 s'). Writing the number makes progress trackable.
  4. Find the cause of each gap. Ask 'why' a few times, since the obvious cause (slow intake) may hide the real one (driver has 2 hours of practice).
  5. Brainstorm actions that would close each gap and estimate the effort for each one. This turns a list of problems into a plan.
  6. Prioritize which gaps to close first, based on impact and effort. You rarely have time to close every gap before the next event.
  7. Assign an owner and deadline for each action and re-measure later. A gap analysis is only useful if you check whether the gap closed.

History

Who created it

No single inventor. Comparing where you are to where you want to be is an old planning idea, and the 'planning gap' is often traced to strategic planning work of the 1960s, with H. Igor Ansoff's 1965 book Corporate Strategy commonly cited. The method has since been adapted to many fields, from quality management to IT and compliance.

What it was created for

In business planning, it helped companies see the difference between where their current path would take them and the targets they had set, so they could plan new strategies to close it.

What it is used for

Organizations use it to plan improvements, check skills against job needs, prepare for audits or standards, and set priorities. Teams use it after a season or event to plan what to fix next.

Industries Business strategyInformation technologyQuality and complianceHuman resources and trainingHealthcareEducation

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Use a simple table with columns: Area, Current, Target, Gap, Cause, Action, Owner, Due.
  • Check that the target is right before chasing the gap. Sometimes the best fix is changing an unrealistic target.
  • Combine it with RICE or weighted pros and cons to decide which gaps to work on first.
  • Run it right after an event while the data and memories are fresh, then again mid-season.
Watch out

Vague desired states ("better programming") make the gap unclear. Use numbers or examples.

Rambots example (made up)

After the first regional, the team's goal is a 12-point average endgame, but scouting shows it averaged 4 points because the climb succeeded in only 3 of 12 matches. The cause is that the hook misses the bar when the robot is not square. Actions are a driver alignment camera overlay and a guide funnel on the hook, owned by programming and mechanical, due before the second event.

Work Breakdown Structure

30-45 min · Leads or planning group · Well documented

Break an event or project into pieces, then into tasks with owners and dates.

What it is

A Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) splits a project into its big pieces, then splits each piece until every line is a task one person can finish. It is the standard way project managers make sure nothing gets missed. Each piece must be fully covered by the tasks under it, and every task ends up with one owner and a due date. The task list at the bottom becomes your checklist.

When to use it

Planning anything with many moving parts: an outreach event, a competition trip, a fundraiser, the first build week, or a big meeting. Use it before RACI or a Kanban board, which both assume you already have the full task list.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write what you are delivering, in one sentence.
  2. Add the 5 to 8 big pieces (venue, people, publicity, supplies, after).
  3. Under each piece, add tasks until each one is a job for one person.
  4. Give every task an owner and a due date, then run the missing-piece check.
In depth
  1. Start from the finished outcome, not the first task: write one sentence describing the meeting or event as done (for example, "60 guests saw the robot drive and 10 families signed up for our newsletter") and put it at the top of the tree.
  2. Split that outcome into 5 to 8 big pieces, named as nouns or deliverables rather than verbs (Venue, Robot demo, Presentation, Promotion, Supplies, Volunteers, Follow-up), so the group argues about what is needed before arguing about how.
  3. Keep splitting each piece until every item is one person's task that a student can finish in a single work session or a few short ones; if a task needs two people to agree on every step, split it again.
  4. Check each level against its parent (the 100 percent rule): ask "if every child item is done, is the parent completely done?" and also "is anything here outside what we actually agreed to do?" Add what is missing and cut what is extra.
  5. Assign exactly one owner and one due date to every lowest-level task; helpers are fine, but one named student is answerable for each line, and no task leaves the meeting with "the team" as owner.
  6. Do a missing-pieces sweep before you finish: walk through setup, transport, permissions, communication with families and the host, photos, cleanup, thank-you notes, and the debrief, since these are the items students forget most often.
  7. Hand the finished task list to the next tool: put owners and roles into a RACI chart if responsibilities are unclear, or turn each task into a card on a Kanban board (To Do, Doing, Done) so progress is visible at every meeting.

History

Who created it

The Work Breakdown Structure grew out of US defense project management around PERT, which the US Navy introduced in the late 1950s for the Polaris missile program; early PERT work grouped tasks into product-oriented categories before the term WBS was in use. In 1962 the Department of Defense and NASA published the "DOD and NASA Guide: PERT/COST Systems Design," which described the WBS approach (secondary sources date it to June 1962 and say it was developed with the aerospace industry). On 1 November 1968 the Department of Defense issued MIL-STD-881, a military standard for work breakdown structures on defense materiel items. The Project Management Institute carried the idea into civilian practice: its 1987 Project Management Body of Knowledge and later PMBOK Guide editions define the WBS as a deliverable-oriented hierarchical decomposition of the work, and PMI also publishes a separate practice standard for WBS.

What it was created for

It was created so that large, complex defense and aerospace programs could define all of their work in one hierarchy and tie cost and schedule tracking to specific deliverables.

What it is used for

Teams use it to turn a big goal into a complete list of deliverables and then into small tasks that each have one owner, a due date, and a size that can be estimated and tracked.

Industries Defense and aerospaceConstruction and engineeringSoftware and ITEvent planningGovernment programsManufacturing

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Use sticky notes or a shared whiteboard so pieces can be moved and split easily; build the tree as a group, then have one person type it up the same day.
  • A common sizing heuristic (often called the 8/80 rule in industry) says a work package should take roughly 8 to 80 hours; for students, scale it down so each task fits in a few hours or one week.
  • Name deliverables as results ("Printed flyer, 50 copies") instead of activities ("Work on flyer") so everyone can tell when an item is done.
  • Number the items (1, 1.1, 1.1.2) so people can refer to tasks quickly in chat and on the Kanban board.
Watch out

Stopping too early. A line like "Publicity" is not a task; keep splitting until one person knows exactly what to do, and check for cleanup, communication, and permissions.

Planning an outreach demo night at the public library, the Rambots outreach lead breaks the event into Venue, Robot demo, Presentation, Promotion, Supplies, Volunteers, and Follow-up, then splits each until tasks like "confirm the library room booking by Oct 20" and "pack spare batteries and charger" each have one student owner and a due date.

Backward Plan

20-30 min · Leads or planning group · Well documented

Start at the event date and work backward to schedule every lead-time task.

What it is

A Backward Plan (reverse timeline) starts from the day of the event and works backward: what has to be done the day before, the week before, a month before. It catches lead-time tasks such as approvals, permission slips, orders, and reminders that a plain to-do list misses until it is too late. The plan turns "3 weeks before" into a real date and flags anything already overdue.

When to use it

Any event with a fixed date: a planning meeting, an outreach demo, a competition, a fundraiser, a parent night. Pair it with a Work Breakdown Structure to find the tasks, then use this to time them.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Set the event and its date.
  2. List what must be true on the day, then work backward, task by task.
  3. For each task, enter how long before the event it must be done, using real lead times plus a buffer.
  4. Sort by date, assign owners, and check the timeline every week.
In depth
  1. Fix the event date and time first and write it at the right edge of the timeline; if the date is not confirmed with the host or the school calendar, confirming it is the first task, because everything else depends on it.
  2. List what must be true on the day as finished conditions ("robot passes inspection checklist," "room is booked and unlocked," "slides are loaded on the laptop," "families got a reminder") rather than as activities.
  3. Work backward from the date for each condition using real lead times: how long the school takes to approve a room or event request, how long parts take to ship, how long it takes to get a reply from a busy adult, and when the last full build session falls before the event.
  4. Add buffer to every chain, especially anything that depends on an adult, a vendor, or shipping; a simple rule is to add at least one regular meeting's worth of time before each hard deadline, and borrow the Army idea of leaving most of the time for the people doing the work.
  5. Flag any task whose calculated start date has already passed: mark it in red, decide right away whether to compress it, find a workaround, or cut it, and tell the group so no one assumes it is on track.
  6. Review the plan at the start of every weekly meeting: check the next two weeks of dates, move finished items to done, and adjust later dates if something slipped instead of letting the whole timeline quietly go stale.
  7. Assign one owner to every dated task and have each owner confirm out loud that the start date works with their schedule (tests, games, other clubs); a backward plan without owners is only a calendar.

History

Who created it

Planning backward from a fixed end point shows up independently in several fields. In US Army doctrine, FM 5-0 (Army Planning and Orders Production, January 2005) defines reverse planning as starting with the envisioned end state and working backward in time, notes it is common at lower echelons and in troop leading procedures, and pairs it with the one-third/two-thirds rule: leaders use no more than one-third of the available time for their own planning and give the remaining two-thirds to subordinates. In energy and sustainability planning, John B. Robinson coined "backcasting" in his 1982 Energy Policy article "Energy Backcasting: A Proposed Method of Policy Analysis," building on the soft energy path thinking of the mid-1970s. In education, Grant Wiggins and Jay McTighe's Understanding by Design (ASCD, 1998; expanded second edition 2005) popularized backward design, where teachers start from the desired learning results and plan instruction to reach them. Event planners' reverse timelines (counting back from the event date to set deadlines for invitations, bookings, and orders) are common practice rather than a single documented invention.

What it was created for

It was created for situations with a fixed deadline or a clearly defined end state, where the question is not what can be done next but what must happen, and by when, for the goal to be met on time.

What it is used for

Teams use it to turn an event date into a dated checklist of lead-time tasks, so slow items like room bookings, permissions, and shipping start early enough instead of being discovered too late.

Industries Military operationsEducation and curriculum designEvent and wedding planningEnergy and sustainability policyProduct launches and marketingConstruction scheduling

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Draw the timeline right to left on a whiteboard (event date on the right) so students physically see the work moving away from the deadline.
  • Ask an adult or alum for real lead times on unfamiliar items; students usually underestimate approvals, printing, and shipping.
  • Put every dated task in the shared team calendar with the owner's name in the title so reminders happen automatically.
  • Pair the backward plan with a WBS: the WBS tells you what all the pieces are, and the backward plan tells you when each one has to start.
Watch out

Guessing lead times. Ask the people involved how long approvals, orders, and printing really take, then add a buffer.

Rambots example (made up)

Six weeks before a robot demo at the school open house, the Rambots start from the open house date, list that the robot must drive reliably, the demo area must be approved, and a three-minute talk must be rehearsed, then count back to find the facilities request is due this Friday and the last drive-practice session is the Tuesday before the event.

13.1

5 Whys

10-20 min · 2-6 · Partly documented

Ask why until you reach a cause you can actually fix.

What it is

Start with the problem and ask why it happened. Then ask why that happened. By the fourth or fifth answer you usually reach a process or system cause instead of a part or a person.

When to use it

Something broke or went wrong and you want it to stay fixed.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. State the problem as a fact.
  2. Answer each why with something you can check, not a guess.
  3. Stop when the answer is something the team controls and can change.
  4. Write a countermeasure and how you will know it worked.
In depth
  1. Write the problem as a specific observed fact: what failed, when, and how you know. 'Robot lost drive in Qual 14 with 1:10 left' beats 'drivetrain is bad', because a vague start gives vague answers.
  2. Ask why it happened and write an answer you can check with evidence (logs, photos, the broken part). If you are guessing, mark it as a guess and go verify before asking the next why.
  3. Ask why again about that answer, not about the original problem. Each step should move exactly one link down the chain of cause and effect.
  4. Stop when you reach a cause you can act on that, once fixed, would prevent this failure and others like it. Five is a rule of thumb; sometimes it takes three, sometimes seven.
  5. If an answer has two real causes, branch and follow both. Forcing a single line hides contributing causes.
  6. Check the chain backward by reading from the root cause to the symptom with 'therefore' between each step. If a link does not follow, the chain is broken and needs another look.
  7. Assign a countermeasure to the root cause with an owner and a date, then check later that the problem has not come back.

History

Who created it

Commonly attributed to Sakichi Toyoda, founder of the Toyoda companies, and built into the Toyota Production System. Taiichi Ohno, the main architect of that system, promoted it, saying that repeating 'why' five times makes the nature of a problem and its solution clear.

What it was created for

Solving problems on the Toyota factory floor, where workers were expected to find the real cause of a stoppage instead of patching the symptom.

What it is used for

Fast root cause analysis of a single, specific failure, often as a first pass before a bigger tool like a fishbone diagram.

Industries Automotive manufacturingLean manufacturingSoftware operationsHealthcare qualityAviation maintenanceProduct design

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Ask 'why did our process allow this?' rather than 'who did this?' A chain that ends at a person ('Alex forgot') almost never reaches the root cause.
  • Critics, including former Toyota executive Teruyuki Minoura, point out that the answers are limited by what the people in the room already know. If nobody understands CAN wiring, the chain will stall at symptoms, so bring in someone who knows the system.
  • It works best when the failure is a fairly straight chain. When many causes could be involved, start with a fishbone and run 5 Whys on the top few branches.
  • Run it on near misses too, not just failures at events. Near misses are cheaper lessons.
Watch out

It can stop at a person ("he forgot"). Keep asking until you reach a process the team can change.

Rambots example (made up)

Problem: the intake stopped spinning in two practice matches. Why? The motor controller browned out. Why? Battery voltage sagged hard under load. Why? The team was using a battery that had been in service for three seasons. Why? There was no battery log, so old batteries were never retired. Countermeasure: number every battery, log cycles and internal resistance with a battery analyzer, and retire any that test poorly before events.

13.2

Fishbone (6M)

20-30 min · 3-8 · Partly documented

Brainstorm possible causes in six categories so nothing gets missed.

What it is

Also called an Ishikawa diagram. The problem is the head of the fish. Each bone is a category of causes: People, Method, Measurement, Machine, Material, and Environment (the 6Ms). Fill the bones, then test the likely causes.

When to use it

A problem with many possible causes, like accuracy dropping or a mechanism that fails sometimes.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write the problem in the head.
  2. Brainstorm causes under each category, one per line.
  3. Circle the likely ones and test them, or run each through 5 Whys.
In depth
  1. Write the effect at the head of the fish as a measurable problem, such as 'shooter range varies from 2.5 m to 4 m at the same setpoint'. A clear effect keeps the brainstorm on target.
  2. Draw six category bones: Man (people, skills, training), Machine (equipment and tools), Method (procedures), Material (parts and stock), Measurement (sensors, gauges, data), and Mother Nature (environment). The categories push the team past the first obvious cause.
  3. Brainstorm causes under each bone and add smaller sub-bones that answer 'why does this happen'. Capture everything at this stage, because critiquing ideas too early makes people stop offering them.
  4. If a cause fits two bones, place it where you would act on it. Exact placement matters less than covering every category.
  5. Circle the causes the team thinks are most likely, using evidence you already have (match logs, inspection notes, video). This turns a big diagram into a short test list.
  6. Test the circled causes one at a time and write the results on the diagram. A fishbone only lists possibilities; it does not prove anything until you test.
  7. Keep the finished diagram with the fix. It records what you ruled out, which saves time if the problem comes back.

History

Who created it

Credited to Kaoru Ishikawa, a Japanese quality control expert, and named after him. Sources disagree on when he first used it (1943 at Kawasaki is often repeated, while others say the early 1950s), but it became widely known through Japanese quality circles in the 1960s. The category lists (4M, 5M, 6M, 8M) were added and expanded by practitioners over time, so there is no single official set.

What it was created for

Helping factory teams in Japan sort the many possible causes of a quality problem so they could see which factors to investigate.

What it is used for

Brainstorming and organizing possible causes of a problem as a group, before testing which ones are real.

Industries ManufacturingHealthcareAutomotiveFood processingSoftwareService operations

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Change the categories to fit the problem. For team process problems like missed deadlines, try People, Process, Tools, Communication, Schedule, and Resources instead of the 6M.
  • Use sticky notes or a shared whiteboard so causes can be moved while people talk.
  • Run a quick 5 Whys on the top two or three causes to push them deeper.
  • If one bone collects nearly all the ideas, the team may have already decided on an answer. Spend a few minutes forcing ideas onto the empty bones.
Watch out

It lists possible causes, not proven ones. Test the likely causes before fixing anything.

Rambots example (made up)

Effect: the shooter's range varied from 2.5 m to 4 m at one flywheel setpoint. Under Machine the team listed worn flywheel tread and a loose shaft collar; under Material, game pieces with different amounts of wear; under Measurement, a flywheel speed reading that was never checked against a tachometer; under Mother Nature, a cold practice space. Testing showed worn game pieces flew about 0.5 m shorter, so they tuned with a sorted set of pieces and added closed-loop velocity control so the wheel recovered faster between shots.

13.3

Is / Is Not

15-20 min · 2-5 · Well documented

Narrow a problem by comparing where it happens with where it doesn't.

What it is

For each dimension, write what the problem IS and what it could be but IS NOT. The differences point straight at the cause.

When to use it

An intermittent problem, like a dropout that only happens sometimes or on one subsystem.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. State the problem.
  2. Fill the Is and Is Not columns with facts.
  3. Ask what is different between them.
  4. List causes that explain every row, then test the strongest one.
In depth
  1. Write a one-line deviation statement naming the object and what is wrong with it, such as 'autonomous path drifts left'. Use one deviation per sheet, because mixing problems blurs the comparison.
  2. Fill the WHAT row: what IS affected, and what IS NOT affected even though it could be. The IS NOT column is where most of the clues are.
  3. Fill the WHERE row: where on the object and where physically the problem shows up and does not show up (on the competition carpet versus the shop floor, on the left side versus the right).
  4. Fill the WHEN row: when it was first seen, how often it happens, and at what point in a cycle (only after a repair, only after two minutes of driving, never at power-on).
  5. Fill the EXTENT row: how many, how big, and whether it is getting worse or holding steady. A growing problem points to different causes than a constant one.
  6. For each IS and IS NOT pair, write what is different about the IS side, then list what changed in or around that difference. Most causes come from a change.
  7. Test every possible cause against every row. A real cause must explain both the IS and the IS NOT facts. Then confirm the surviving cause on the robot before calling it fixed.

History

Who created it

Developed by Charles Kepner and Benjamin Tregoe, who studied how managers solve problems and make decisions while working at the RAND Corporation. They founded Kepner-Tregoe, Inc. in 1958 and published the method in The Rational Manager (1965).

What it was created for

Helping managers diagnose problems systematically instead of jumping from a symptom straight to a favorite cause.

What it is used for

Narrowing down the cause of a problem by comparing where, when, and how it happens with closely related cases where it does not happen.

Industries ManufacturingIT operationsAerospacePharmaceuticalsUtilitiesAutomotive service

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Write only facts in the specification. Words like 'probably' and 'I think' belong in the possible-causes step.
  • The method works best on a problem you can observe more than once. For a single event, a detailed timeline is often more useful.
  • IS / IS NOT is the problem analysis part of a larger Kepner-Tregoe process that also includes situation appraisal, decision analysis, and potential problem analysis.
  • Do it as a group. The person who saw the failure often knows an IS NOT that nobody else would think to write down.
Watch out

Vague entries ("sometimes") make it useless. Be specific about where, when, and how much.

Rambots example (made up)

On a Saturday at a regional, the robot's autonomous path started drifting left. IS: autos run after the lunch break; IS NOT: morning autos running the same code on the same field. The difference was that the IMU had been remounted during a lunch repair, using double-sided tape on a flexible plate. Swapping to a rigid, screwed-down mount explained every IS and IS NOT fact, and the drift was gone the next match.

13.4

FMEA

45-90 min · 3-6 · Well documented

Find how the robot could fail before it does, and fix the worst risks first.

What it is

Failure Mode and Effects Analysis. For each part or function, list how it could fail, the effect, and the cause. Rate Severity, Occurrence, and Detection (10 = hard to detect). Their product is the Risk Priority Number (RPN).

When to use it

Before a competition, after a design freeze, or when a subsystem keeps giving trouble.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. List each function or part and how it could fail.
  2. Rate S, O, and D. Detection is backwards: 10 means you would never see it coming.
  3. Sort by RPN and plan actions for the top items.
  4. Any severity of 9 or 10 needs an action even if its RPN is low.
In depth
  1. Pick the scope and break it into parts or process steps, such as an intake's pivot, rollers, belt, beam-break sensor, and wiring. An FMEA without a clear boundary never ends.
  2. For each part, write its function and the ways it could fail to do it (failure modes): belt skips, pivot bolt backs out, sensor cable unplugs. Focus on how it fails, not just that it fails.
  3. For each failure mode, write the effect on the robot or the match and rate Severity from 1 to 10. Severity is about consequence, so a failure that disables the robot rates higher than one that slows cycles.
  4. List likely causes and rate Occurrence from 1 to 10 based on evidence: past failures, similar mechanisms, or other teams' experience.
  5. List the current controls that would catch the problem before it costs you (a pit checklist, a sensor value on the dashboard) and rate Detection from 1 to 10, where 10 means you would not see it coming.
  6. Multiply Severity x Occurrence x Detection to get the RPN (1 to 1000) and sort the list. Also review every Severity 9 or 10 item on its own, because a low RPN can hide a disabling failure that just happens to be rare.
  7. Assign actions, owners, and dates to the top items, then re-rate after the fix. The new ratings show whether the action actually lowered the risk.

History

Who created it

Started in the US military with the procedure MIL-P-1629, issued in 1949. NASA used it during the Apollo program in the 1960s, the auto industry picked it up in the 1970s, and the Automotive Industry Action Group (AIAG) published its first FMEA manual in 1993. The 2019 AIAG-VDA handbook replaced the Risk Priority Number (RPN) with Action Priority tables.

What it was created for

Finding and ranking the ways military equipment could fail before it was fielded, so engineers could fix the worst problems first.

What it is used for

Listing how a design or process could fail, rating each risk, and choosing which failures to address before they happen.

Industries AutomotiveAerospaceMedical devicesDefenseManufacturingNuclear power

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Agree on the 1 to 10 scales in writing before rating, with an FRC example for each score. Without that, ratings drift from person to person.
  • RPN treats the three ratings as equally important, so very different risks can get the same number (10x2x2 and 2x4x5 are both 40). The AIAG-VDA Action Priority method fixes this by looking at Severity first.
  • Keep a design FMEA (the mechanism as built) separate from a process FMEA (how the pit crew assembles, charges, and inspects).
  • Lowering Occurrence through better design is usually worth more than improving Detection through more inspection.
Watch out

Teams argue about whether something is a 6 or a 7. Close enough is fine: the ranking matters more than exact numbers.

Rambots example (made up)

Before their first regional, a team ran an FMEA on the elevator. 'Cascade rope frays and snaps' got Severity 9 (carriage drops, no scoring), Occurrence 4 (seen once in practice), and Detection 6 (only visible with the carriage removed), for an RPN of 216, the highest on the sheet. They cut an inspection window into the carriage plate and added a pre-match rope check, which brought Detection to 3 and the RPN to 108, and they packed a pre-cut spare rope in the pit.

13.5

Three Tiers of Prevention

15-20 min · 2-6 · Partly documented

Stop a problem at three levels: prevent it, catch it, and limit the damage.

What it is

Tier 1 prevents the problem from happening at all. Tier 2 detects it before it causes harm. Tier 3 limits the damage if it gets through. Tier 1 is strongest; good plans use all three.

When to use it

Safety hazards in the shop, or quality problems that keep reaching the robot.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Name the hazard or defect.
  2. Write at least one control for each tier.
  3. Ask which tier you are relying on most. If it is Tier 3, push for a Tier 1 fix.
In depth
  1. Name one specific risk, such as 'battery comes loose and the robot browns out mid-match' or 'student cut on the bandsaw'. Each tier is planned against that one risk.
  2. Prevent: change the design or the process so the problem cannot happen or is much less likely, like a battery strap plus a captured bracket. This lines up with the top of the hierarchy of controls (elimination, substitution, engineering controls) and is the most reliable tier.
  3. Detect: add a way to notice the problem early, before it causes harm, such as a tug test on the pit checklist or battery voltage on the dashboard. This is similar to secondary prevention in medicine, which catches disease early while it is still easy to treat.
  4. Mitigate: plan what limits the damage if it happens anyway, such as a spare battery and strap in the pit cart, a stocked first aid kit with a trained adult, or code that keeps the robot driving during a brownout. This is similar to tertiary prevention and to the last layers of defense in depth.
  5. Check that the tiers are independent. If prevention and detection both depend on the same person remembering, one bad day defeats both; the Swiss cheese model is about holes in separate layers lining up.
  6. Put the most effort into the prevent tier, but do not skip the other two, because no prevention is perfect.
  7. After every incident or near miss, ask which tier failed and which one caught it. That tells you where to invest next.

History

Who created it

This is a team's simplified model, not a published framework with an inventor. It borrows its shape from older safety ideas: primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention in public health (introduced by Hugh Leavell and E. Gurney Clark in the mid-20th century), defense in depth (a military idea later used in nuclear safety and cybersecurity), James Reason's Swiss cheese model of layered defenses (1990), and the hierarchy of controls promoted by NIOSH.

What it was created for

Giving a team a short set of three questions to ask about any risk: how do we stop it, how do we notice it early, and how do we limit the damage if it happens anyway.

What it is used for

Planning safety and reliability measures for the shop, the robot, and events so the team never depends on a single safeguard.

Industries Public healthOccupational safetyNuclear powerCybersecurityAviationHealthcare

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Be honest about what this model leaves out. The hierarchy of controls has five levels ranked by effectiveness, with procedures (administrative controls) and PPE at the bottom. Three tiers flatten that ranking, so do not treat 'wear safety glasses' as equal to 'put a guard on the machine'.
  • Public health uses more detailed definitions and extra levels (such as primordial and quaternary prevention). The team model borrows the shape, not the full definitions.
  • Pair it with an FMEA. The Detection rating maps to the detect tier, and any high-Severity failure mode should have measures in all three tiers.
  • It works for non-safety risks too, such as missing the order deadline for a critical gearbox.
Watch out

Teams lean on Tier 3 (gloves, first aid) because it is easiest. Push for a Tier 1 fix.

Rambots example (made up)

Risk: a CAN cable unplugs during a match and the shooter stops. Prevent: strain relief and locking connectors on every CAN joint. Detect: a dashboard indicator that turns red when any device drops off the CAN bus, checked in the queue line before every match. Mitigate: code that still lets the robot drive and play defense if the shooter controller goes missing.

13.6

Pareto Chart

15-20 min · 1-4 · Well documented

Find the few causes behind most of your problems.

What it is

Count how often each cause happens and sort from most to least. The line shows the running total. Usually a few causes (the vital few) make up about 80% of the problems.

When to use it

After an event, with match notes or scouting data: penalties, breakdowns, missed shots.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Decide what you are counting and over what period.
  2. Enter each cause with its count.
  3. Fix the orange bars first. They are the vital few.
In depth
  1. Decide what you are counting and over what period, for example 'robot problems that cost us points, across all qualification matches.' A clear scope keeps the categories comparable.
  2. Collect real data, not memories. Pull from match video, scouting sheets, pit logs, or a failure log, because memory overweights dramatic failures and underweights boring frequent ones.
  3. Group the data into 5 to 10 categories plus a small 'Other' bucket. Too many categories flattens the chart, and if 'Other' is one of the biggest bars, split it up.
  4. Sort the categories from largest to smallest and draw them as bars. Sorting is what makes the vital few stand out.
  5. Add a cumulative percentage line on a second axis. The point where the line passes about 80% shows which categories together account for most of the problem.
  6. Weight by impact when raw counts mislead, for example by points lost or minutes of downtime instead of number of incidents. One failure that disables the robot can matter more than many small ones.
  7. Fix the top one or two categories, then collect new data and redraw the chart. The chart should change after a real fix; if it does not, the root cause was not found.

History

Who created it

Economist Vilfredo Pareto observed in his Cours d'économie politique (1896 to 1897) that about 80% of the land in Italy was owned by about 20% of the people. Quality pioneer Joseph Juran turned this into a general 'Pareto principle' and the phrase 'the vital few and the trivial many,' and spread it through his Quality Control Handbook (1951). In a mid-1970s essay Juran admitted the name was a mistake: Pareto only studied wealth, and the cumulative curves Juran used should have been credited to economist Max Lorenz.

What it was created for

Juran applied it to manufacturing quality control to show managers that a few defect types or causes produce most of the losses, so improvement effort should go there first.

What it is used for

Teams use Pareto charts (bars sorted from largest to smallest, with a cumulative percentage line) to decide which problems, causes, or customers to tackle first.

Industries ManufacturingQuality engineering and Six SigmaHealthcareSoftware bug triageCustomer supportEquipment maintenance

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • The split is rarely exactly 80/20. The point is the imbalance, not the exact ratio, so do not force the numbers to fit.
  • Drill down with a second Pareto chart inside the biggest bar, for example splitting 'electrical failures' into connectors, breakers, and CAN faults.
  • Show before-and-after Pareto charts side by side to prove to mentors or sponsors that a fix worked.
Watch out

Counting the wrong thing (minor annoyances) points you at the wrong fix. Count what actually cost matches or time.

Rambots example (made up)

After a regional, the team logs 31 match problems from video: 14 loose or unplugged connectors, 7 intake jams, 4 brownouts, 3 driver errors, and 3 other. The Pareto chart shows connectors and jams make up 68% of all problems, so the team adds a pre-match connector tug test to the pit checklist and redesigns the intake's entry flare before the next event.

13.7

Incident Report

20-30 min · Mentor + people involved · Partly documented

Record a safety incident or near miss so the team learns from it.

What it is

A structured record of what happened, what was done right away, why it happened, and what will stop it happening again. Near misses count: they are free lessons. It is the team's internal record and does not replace the school's official form.

When to use it

Any injury, near miss, or equipment damage, as soon as everyone is safe.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Get help first. Fill this in once everyone is safe.
  2. Write facts in order, without blame.
  3. Find the root cause and add actions at all three tiers.
  4. Have a mentor review it and check that follow-ups happen.
In depth
  1. Take care of the person first: first aid, call for help if needed, and tell the supervising mentor right away. The report never comes before care.
  2. Follow the school's official injury reporting process and form for any injury. A team incident report is an internal learning tool and does not replace the school's form or any required report.
  3. Write the team report within 24 hours: date, time, location, the task underway, tools or machines involved, who was present, and whether anyone was hurt. Details fade fast, so speed matters.
  4. Report near misses too, such as a drill bit snapping or a robot moving when people thought it was disabled. OSHA describes a near miss as an event where no one was hurt but a slight shift in time or position could have caused injury, which makes it a free warning.
  5. Ask what happened, how it happened, and why, and keep asking why until you reach a system cause (no guard on the bandsaw, no rule before enabling the robot) rather than stopping at 'someone was careless'. OSHA's root cause guidance stresses fixing the system, not just the symptom.
  6. Choose a corrective action with an owner and a due date, and prefer fixes that remove the hazard (a guard, a jig, a lockout rule) over reminders. Reminders fade; physical fixes stay.
  7. Share a short, blame-free summary with the team and check later that the fix is actually in place. Teams that punish reporters soon stop getting reports.

History

Who created it

No single inventor. Workplace incident reporting grew alongside industrial safety efforts in the 20th century; H. W. Heinrich's 1931 book Industrial Accident Prevention popularized the idea that no-injury and minor incidents far outnumber serious ones (his 300-29-1 ratio, which later researchers have criticized as poorly supported). Near-miss reporting is now a standard safety-culture practice encouraged by OSHA and seen in programs like the Aviation Safety Reporting System, started in 1976.

What it was created for

Learning from injuries and close calls so the underlying causes get fixed before someone is hurt. Near-miss reports exist because close calls are cheap lessons and everyone involved is still there to explain what happened.

What it is used for

Recording injuries and near misses, finding root causes, and tracking fixes in workplaces, labs, schools, and makerspaces.

Industries ManufacturingConstructionAviationHealthcareLaboratoriesSchools and makerspaces

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Make near-miss reporting easy and blame-free, for example a QR code in the shop that opens a short form.
  • Review the past season's reports at the start of each season to spot repeat hazards.
  • Treat a rising number of near-miss reports as a good sign; it usually means people are paying attention, not that the shop got more dangerous.
  • Have the safety captain and a mentor review every report together before closing it.
Watch out

Never delay care to fill out a form. It does not replace the school's official report.

Rambots example (made up)

During a pit check, the robot's arm swings while a student is reaching into the frame because someone enabled the robot for a quick test. No one is hurt, but the safety captain files a near-miss report that afternoon. The root cause is that the team had no rule for calling clear before enabling, so they add a spoken 'Clear? Enabling!' callout, and a mentor confirms the rule is followed at the next event.

14.1

PDCA Cycle

15 min per cycle · 2-6 · Well documented

Plan, Do, Check, Act: improve something in small tested loops.

What it is

Plan a change and predict the result. Do it on a small scale. Check what actually happened. Act: keep it, change it, or drop it, then start the next cycle.

When to use it

Improving a process like battery swaps, pit checks, or meeting length.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write what you want to improve and the cycle number.
  2. Make a prediction in Plan so Check has something to compare.
  3. Keep each cycle small enough to finish in a week or two.
In depth
  1. Plan: state the problem, the change you will try, and a prediction with a number, such as 'a pre-match checklist will cut late-to-queue calls from 5 per event to 1'. The prediction is what gives the Check step something to compare against.
  2. Also in Plan, decide how you will measure the result and for how long before you start. Choosing the measure after seeing results invites cherry-picking.
  3. Do: try the change on a small scale first (one event, one subteam, one week) and record what actually happened, including surprises and places where you did not follow the plan.
  4. Check (or Study): compare the results with the prediction and ask why they matched or did not. A missed prediction teaches as much as a hit.
  5. Act: adopt the change as the new standard, adjust it, or drop it. Write the new standard down so it survives when students graduate or switch roles.
  6. Start the next cycle from the new baseline. PDCA is meant to repeat, with each loop small and quick.

History

Who created it

It grew out of Walter Shewhart's work on quality control at Bell Labs. In his 1939 book he described specification, production, and inspection as a cycle linked to the scientific method. W. Edwards Deming taught a version of it in Japan in the early 1950s, and the Japanese participants shortened it to Plan, Do, Check, Act; Deming himself preferred Plan, Do, Study, Act (PDSA) because 'study' better describes analyzing results.

What it was created for

Improving manufacturing quality by treating production as a series of experiments rather than a fixed plan carried out once.

What it is used for

Running small, repeated improvement cycles on any process, from a factory line to a hospital unit to a team's pit routine.

Industries ManufacturingHealthcare quality improvementEducationSoftwareGovernment servicesLean operations

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Deming's PDSA wording is a reminder to study why something worked, not just check a box that it was done.
  • Keep cycles short. During build season one cycle can be a week; during competition season it can be one event.
  • You can run several PDCA loops at once on different processes, but change only one thing per process at a time so you know what caused the result.
  • PDCA works well as a wrapper around other tools: a fishbone or 5 Whys in Plan, a debrief in Check.
Watch out

Skipping the prediction in Plan leaves Check with nothing to compare against.

Rambots example (made up)

Plan: the pit crew predicted that a laminated pre-match checklist would cut 'robot not ready at queue' calls from 5 to 1 at their next event. Do: they used it for all 12 qualification matches at their second regional. Check: they had 2 late calls, both caused by a battery that was not charged in time. Act: they kept the checklist, added a battery rotation board, and started the next cycle aiming for zero late calls.

14.2

Engineering Design Cycle

One loop per week · Subteam · Well documented

Walk a design from problem to tested prototype, then loop.

What it is

Define the problem, research, brainstorm, choose, prototype, test, and iterate. Each loop teaches you something the next one uses.

When to use it

Any new mechanism, tool, or fixture.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write the requirements in Define so you can test against them.
  2. Brainstorm before you choose. Use a Pugh matrix or Decision Matrix to choose.
  3. Test against the requirements and write what you will change.
In depth
  1. Define the problem with requirements and constraints, using numbers where you can: the intake must grab a game piece off the floor in under 0.5 s, weigh under 4 lb, and stay inside the frame perimeter rules. Clear requirements settle later arguments.
  2. Research before designing: read the game manual, look at past FRC games with similar tasks, and study other teams' public build threads. Most good mechanisms are borrowed and adapted.
  3. Brainstorm several concepts without judging them, then sketch the strongest few. Comparing at least three options keeps the team from locking onto the first idea.
  4. Choose with a decision matrix weighted by the requirements. Written scoring makes the choice explainable to the rest of the team.
  5. Prototype fast and cheap (wood, polycarbonate, drill motors) and test the riskiest assumption first, such as roller compression or wheel speed.
  6. Test against the requirements and record numbers, not opinions. A failure in the shop is far cheaper than a failure on the field.
  7. Iterate: feed test results back into the design and CAD, and repeat until the requirements are met or you decide the requirements themselves need to change.
  8. Document the final design, test data, and lessons learned. This helps next season's team and gives you evidence for judges' interviews.

History

Who created it

No single inventor. Step-by-step models of the design process came out of engineering education and the design methods movement of the 1960s, including Morris Asimow's Introduction to Design (1962) and a 1962 design methods conference in London. In US schools, the Next Generation Science Standards (2013) teach engineering design as defining problems, developing solutions, and optimizing them.

What it was created for

Making engineering design something that could be taught and repeated, instead of relying only on the intuition of experienced designers.

What it is used for

Guiding a project from problem definition through prototypes to a tested solution, with repeated loops of testing and improvement.

Industries Mechanical engineeringProduct designAerospaceSTEM educationConsumer electronicsRobotics

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Treat it as a loop, not a line. Expect to jump back to the problem definition when testing shows a requirement was wrong.
  • Spend early time on the biggest unknown. A polished CAD model of an untested roller geometry is wasted effort.
  • Time box each loop so prototyping does not eat the build schedule.
  • Different sources show anywhere from 5 to 10 steps with different names. The labels matter less than defining the problem, testing, and iterating.
Watch out

It is a loop, not a line. Expect to go around several times.

Rambots example (made up)

After kickoff, the intake group defined success as picking up a game piece from any angle in under half a second. In the first week they built three wood-and-polycarbonate prototypes (an over-the-bumper roller intake, an under-the-bumper roller intake, and a claw), timed 20 pickups with each, and chose the over-the-bumper design at a 0.35 s average. Testing on the practice robot then showed bent roller shafts after hard hits, so they looped back and switched to stiffer shafts with compliant wheels.

14.3

Design of Experiments

30 min + tests · 2-4 · Well documented

Test several factors at once and find which one matters most.

What it is

Pick factors you can change, each with a low and high setting. A full factorial test tries every combination, then compares the average result at high versus low for each factor. That difference is the main effect.

When to use it

Tuning a shooter, intake, or autonomous path when several settings interact.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Add up to 4 factors with low and high levels.
  2. Shuffle the run order and run every test in that order.
  3. Enter the results. The biggest bar is the factor to tune first.
In depth
  1. Choose one response you can measure as a number, such as shots made out of 10 from 4 m, or cycle time in seconds. A fuzzy response like 'looks better' cannot be analyzed.
  2. Pick 2 to 4 factors you control and set each to a low (-) and a high (+) level far enough apart to matter, such as flywheel speed 3000 vs 3800 RPM or hood angle 32 vs 38 degrees. Levels that are too close hide the effect in normal shot-to-shot noise.
  3. Write the full factorial table with every combination of levels. With k factors there are 2^k runs, so 3 factors give 8 runs.
  4. Randomize the run order, and repeat runs if you have time. Randomizing protects you from slow drift, such as battery voltage dropping over the session, being mistaken for a factor's effect.
  5. Run the tests and record every result while holding everything else as steady as possible (same distance, same set of game pieces, fresh batteries on a schedule).
  6. Calculate each main effect: the average response at the factor's high level minus the average at its low level. The factors with the largest effects, positive or negative, are the ones that matter.
  7. Check for interactions by seeing whether one factor's effect changes depending on another factor's level. Then run a confirmation test at the predicted best settings before you trust the result.

History

Who created it

Developed by statistician Ronald A. Fisher while he analyzed agricultural trials at the Rothamsted Experimental Station in England in the 1920s. His 1935 book The Design of Experiments laid out the ideas, including factorial designs that test several factors at once instead of one at a time.

What it was created for

Getting reliable answers from crop experiments where soil, weather, and plots varied, and each trial took a full growing season.

What it is used for

Finding which settings matter most in a process or product, and how they interact, using as few test runs as possible.

Industries AgricultureManufacturingPharmaceuticalsChemical engineeringSemiconductorsAutomotive testing

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Compared with changing one factor at a time, a full factorial uses the same runs to estimate every factor's effect and also reveals interactions that one-at-a-time testing misses.
  • With 5 or more factors, a fractional factorial cuts the number of runs, at the cost of some detail about interactions.
  • Add a few center-point runs (every factor halfway between low and high) to check for curvature, which a 2-level design cannot detect on its own.
  • Plot the results. A simple main effects chart is much easier for the whole team to read than a table of numbers.
Watch out

Run the tests in the shuffled order and change only the planned factors. Otherwise drift hides the real effect.

Rambots example (made up)

A team tested its shooter with three factors at two levels: flywheel speed (3000 or 3800 RPM), hood angle (32 or 38 degrees), and game piece compression (0.75 or 1.0 in), giving 8 combinations of 10 shots each, run in random order with a fresh battery every two runs. Comparing the average of the 4 high-speed runs with the 4 low-speed runs gave a main effect of +3.1 shots for speed, while angle added only +0.4 and the higher compression cost 1.8 shots. They locked in the higher speed and lower compression, then confirmed 9 of 10 shots at those settings.

14.4

5S Shop Audit

20-30 min · 3-6 · Partly documented

Score the shop on Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardize, and Sustain.

What it is

5S comes from Japanese manufacturing. Each S is a habit that makes a workspace safer and faster. Score each one, write what you found, and repeat the audit to see the trend.

When to use it

Start of the season, before kickoff, and every few weeks during build.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Walk the area as a group.
  2. Score each S from 1 to 5 and write findings and actions.
  3. Add a new audit column every few weeks and watch the total climb.
In depth
  1. Sort: tag everything in the area and remove what is not needed for current work, such as broken tools, old-season parts, and mystery hardware. Every unneeded item costs search time and floor space.
  2. Set in order: give every remaining item a marked home near where it is used, using shadow boards, labeled bins, and floor tape. The goal is that anyone can find or return a tool in seconds without asking.
  3. Shine: clean the area and inspect while you clean, looking for metal chips in the mill, frayed cords, or dull bits. Cleaning doubles as inspection, which is why it comes before standardizing.
  4. Standardize: write down what 'good' looks like with a photo of each station and a short end-of-session checklist. Standards turn a one-time cleanup into a repeatable routine.
  5. Sustain: run a short scored audit on a fixed schedule, such as every Saturday, rotate who does it, and post the score. Most 5S efforts fail at this step because nobody checks.
  6. Build the audit sheet with 3 to 5 items per S, scored yes/no or 1 to 5, and photograph the area at each audit. Scores and photos make slow drift obvious over several weeks.
  7. When an audit score drops, fix the system rather than blaming a person. If a tool keeps ending up in the wrong place, its assigned home is probably in the wrong spot.

History

Who created it

No single inventor. 5S grew out of Japanese factory practice linked to the Toyota Production System and just-in-time manufacturing, and was later codified by Japanese consultants; Hiroyuki Hirano's 5 Pillars of the Visual Workplace (English edition 1995) helped spread it to Western factories. Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardize, and Sustain are English versions of the Japanese terms seiri, seiton, seiso, seiketsu, and shitsuke.

What it was created for

Factories running just-in-time production needed clean, organized workstations so that defects, waste, and missing tools showed up immediately instead of hiding in clutter.

What it is used for

Shops, hospitals, labs, warehouses, and offices use 5S audits to keep workspaces organized and safe, and to make problems visible at a glance.

Industries ManufacturingAutomotiveHealthcareWarehousing and logisticsLaboratoriesSchool shops and makerspaces

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Many shops add a sixth S, Safety, and audit it directly: eye protection at the door, clear access to the fire extinguisher, and open walkways.
  • Use a red-tag zone. Items with no clear owner sit there for a set time, such as two weeks, and are then claimed or thrown out.
  • Start with one zone, like the hand-tool wall or the electrical bench, and get it to a high standard before expanding.
Watch out

The first cleanup is easy. Sustain is the hard S, so schedule the next audit before leaving.

Rambots example (made up)

Before kickoff, the fabrication lead runs a 5S pass on the shop: three bins of old polycarbonate scraps and unlabeled bolts get red-tagged, every hand tool gets a foam shadow cutout in the toolbox, and the CNC router area gets a taped no-storage zone. A 15-item audit sheet is scored every Saturday at cleanup, and when the score drops from 13 to 8 in week 4, the team finds that drill bits have no home near the drill press and adds one.

14.5

Kanban Practice Board

Ongoing · Subteam · Well documented

Learn Kanban: visible work, pull instead of push, and a work-in-progress limit.

What it is

A board with columns for each stage of work. Two rules make it work: work moves by being pulled when someone has room, and the Doing column has a limit. Limiting work in progress finishes things faster than starting everything at once.

When to use it

Teaching how to manage build tasks. Run the team's real board in a shared tool everyone can edit.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Add cards to the backlog.
  2. Move cards right as work happens.
  3. When Doing is full, help finish something instead of starting something new.
  4. Agree on a Definition of Done.
In depth
  1. Map the real stages your work goes through (for example Backlog, CAD, CNC Queue, Machining, Assembly, Test, Done) and make each one a column. Columns that match reality are what make bottlenecks visible.
  2. Write each task as a card with an owner and a clear done condition ('Shooter hood side plates cut and deburred'). A clear done condition stops cards from sitting in a column forever.
  3. Set a WIP (work in progress) limit on each active column, such as 2 cards in Machining. The limit forces people to finish work before starting more, which shortens how long any single part takes.
  4. Pull work instead of pushing it: a card moves into the next column only when that column has room. Pulling is the core Toyota idea and prevents piles of half-finished parts.
  5. When a column hits its limit, people upstream help clear it instead of starting new work. This 'swarming' is where most of the speedup comes from.
  6. Hold a short standup at the board and walk it from right to left, closest to done first. Starting on the right focuses the team on finishing, not starting.
  7. Track how many days each card takes from start to done and look at the numbers weekly. If times grow, lower the WIP limits or split cards into smaller pieces.

History

Who created it

Kanban (Japanese for signboard or card) comes from the Toyota Production System, where industrial engineer Taiichi Ohno, starting in the late 1940s, used cards to signal when parts should be made or moved. He was inspired by how supermarkets restock shelves only as customers take items, and he described the system in his 1978 book Toyota Production System. For knowledge work, David J. Anderson built a pull system for a Microsoft IT team in 2004 and later laid out the Kanban Method (boards, WIP limits, classes of service) in his 2010 book Kanban: Successful Evolutionary Change for Your Technology Business.

What it was created for

At Toyota, the goal was to cut waste by making only what the next step actually needed (a pull system) instead of piling up inventory. Anderson adapted the idea so software teams could see their work, limit how much was in progress, and improve flow without a major reorganization.

What it is used for

Visual task boards for teams that handle a steady stream of work, with a column for each stage and a limit on how many tasks can be in progress at once.

Industries ManufacturingSoftware developmentIT operationsMarketingHealthcare administrationConstruction

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Start with WIP limits a little lower than feels comfortable. If nobody ever hits a limit, it is too high to do anything.
  • Mark blocked cards with a red sticker instead of moving them to a 'Blocked' column, so blocked work still counts against the limit and gets attention.
  • Add an expedite lane with a limit of one card for emergencies, like a part broken during a match.
  • Keep a physical board in the shop even if you also use a digital tool. The point is that everyone sees it without logging in.
Watch out

A board nobody updates is worse than no board. Keep the real team board in a shared tool.

Rambots example (made up)

During build season the team runs a shop board with columns CAD Released, CNC Queue (limit 4), Machining (limit 2), Assembly (limit 3), and Test. When CNC Queue fills with 4 plates, the CAD students stop releasing new parts and help machinists deburr and tap holes instead. Over two weeks, average time from CAD release to an installed part drops from about 6 days to 3.

15.1

Blameless Postmortem

30-45 min · People involved · Partly documented

Learn from a failure by looking at systems, not people.

What it is

A written review after something went wrong. "Blameless" means you ask what in the process allowed the mistake, not who made it. People share more when they are not afraid, and the fixes go deeper.

When to use it

After any failure that cost a match, money, time, or safety.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write the timeline first, with facts and times.
  2. Ask "what made this possible?" not "who did this?"
  3. Every action item gets an owner and a date.
In depth
  1. Decide ahead of time what triggers a postmortem, such as the robot being disabled in a match, a shop injury, or a missed shipping deadline. Preset triggers keep it from feeling like a punishment aimed at one person.
  2. Gather facts quickly while memories are fresh: match video, driver station logs, photos, and short written notes from everyone involved.
  3. Build a timeline of what happened, when, and what each person saw and knew at that moment. Judging decisions with hindsight is the most common way a postmortem turns into blame.
  4. Describe the impact plainly (lost two qualification matches, dropped six ranking spots) so everyone understands why the effort matters.
  5. Look for contributing causes in the system: missing checks, confusing labels, time pressure, unclear ownership. Ask 'what made this the reasonable choice at the time?' instead of 'who messed up?'
  6. Write action items that change the system, each with one owner and a due date. 'Be more careful' is not an action item.
  7. Share the write-up with the whole team and review the action items at a later meeting. A postmortem nobody reads teaches one person, and untracked actions do not get done.

History

Who created it

No single inventor. The blameless approach comes from safety science in fields like aviation and healthcare, especially 'just culture' ideas associated with Sidney Dekker. In software, John Allspaw's 2012 Etsy post 'Blameless PostMortems and a Just Culture' and Google's Site Reliability Engineering book (2016) made it standard practice.

What it was created for

Learning from accidents and outages without punishing people, because people who fear blame hide the details needed to prevent the next failure.

What it is used for

Writing up what happened after a serious incident, why each decision made sense at the time, and what system changes will prevent a repeat.

Industries Software operationsAviationHealthcareEmergency servicesManufacturingOnline services

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Blameless does not mean anything goes. It separates honest mistakes from reckless or deliberate behavior, and the facilitator should say that clearly at the start.
  • The facilitator should not be the person most involved, or someone who evaluates the people involved.
  • Make good postmortems visible. Google's SRE book describes postmortem reading clubs and highlighting well-written reviews.
  • Write the timeline in neutral language: 'the battery was not swapped' rather than 'Jordan forgot the battery'.
Watch out

One blaming comment ends honest reporting for the season. The facilitator must stop it immediately.

Rambots example (made up)

In a playoff match the robot sat dead for 15 seconds after the radio rebooted. The timeline showed the radio's power cable had been reconnected in a rush between matches and was held in only by friction. Instead of blaming the student who plugged it in, the team found the system gaps: no strain relief on the radio power, no checklist line for it, and a 7-minute turnaround with no assigned roles. Actions: a locking power connector (owner: electrical lead, before the next event), a new checklist line, and role cards for playoff turnarounds.

15.2

Debrief

15-20 min · Whole team · Thin record

Capture what to keep and what to change while it is fresh.

What it is

A quick structured look back after an event or build week. Plus/Delta is the fastest version; Start, Stop, Continue turns it into behavior changes.

When to use it

Right after a competition, outreach event, or the end of a build week.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Do it within a day or two.
  2. Everyone adds at least one Plus and one Delta.
  3. Pick the top three changes and give each an owner.
In depth
  1. Hold it soon, ideally the same day (after the last match, before everyone scatters), and time box it to 10 to 20 minutes. Feedback fades fast, and long debriefs get skipped.
  2. Say the ground rule out loud: talk about actions and systems, not people. This keeps quieter members willing to speak up.
  3. For Plus/Delta, use two columns: Plus (what worked and should continue) and Delta (what to change). Delta means change, not minus, so phrase items as improvements ('load the pit cart the night before') rather than complaints.
  4. For Start/Stop/Continue, use three columns. Pick this version when you need to drop habits, since Plus/Delta has no explicit 'stop' column.
  5. Collect input silently first, with sticky notes or a shared doc for about 3 minutes, then share. Silent writing gets ideas from everyone instead of only the loudest voices.
  6. Group duplicates and vote on the top 2 or 3 deltas. A debrief that produces 25 changes produces none.
  7. Turn the chosen items into actions with owners and dates, and read the previous debrief's actions at the start of the next one.

History

Who created it

Neither format has a documented inventor. Plus/Delta grew out of continuous improvement and facilitation practice (the Gamestorming site cites The Boeing Co. around 1980 as the earliest known use), and Start/Stop/Continue spread through management and agile retrospectives with no clear origin. Both are close relatives of the US Army's After Action Review.

What it was created for

Getting quick, honest feedback at the end of a meeting, workshop, or project so the next one goes better.

What it is used for

Short reviews at the end of a meeting, event, or season where a team lists what worked and what to change.

Industries EducationMilitary trainingLean manufacturingAgile softwareConstructionEvent planning

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Make plus items specific too. 'Pit crew was fast' is less useful than 'battery swaps took under 60 seconds because of the labeled cart'.
  • For an end-of-season debrief, bring data from scouting, match results, or the team's goals tracker so the discussion is not based on memory alone.
  • Rotate who facilitates so more student leads get practice running it.
  • Use an anonymous form for sensitive topics, such as team culture, where people may not speak up in a circle.
Watch out

Collecting deltas without owners just produces a list. Pick the top three and assign them.

Rambots example (made up)

After the Sacramento regional, the team ran a 15-minute Plus/Delta in the hotel lobby. Pluses included 'scouting app synced every match' and 'extra driver practice paid off in eliminations'; deltas included 'pack spare bumper covers in both alliance colors' and 'assign one person to watch the queue schedule'. They voted the queue watcher as the top delta, gave it an owner, and checked on it at the next event.

15.3

Pre-mortem

20-30 min · 4-12 · Well documented

Imagine the project already failed, then work out why.

What it is

Before a project starts, everyone assumes it failed and writes down the reasons. It surfaces risks people would not raise in a normal planning meeting.

When to use it

Kickoff, before a big event, or before a large purchase.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Set the scene: "It is the end of the project and it failed."
  2. Everyone writes reasons alone for a few minutes, then share.
  3. Pick warning signs to watch and preventions to start now.
In depth
  1. Finish the plan first and brief everyone on it. The premortem tests a concrete plan, so people need to know exactly what is being committed to.
  2. Announce the frame: 'It is the day after the event. The project failed badly. What happened?' Stating failure as fact, not as a possibility, gives people permission to name problems they would otherwise keep quiet about.
  3. Give everyone 2 to 3 minutes to write reasons for the failure silently and on their own. Writing alone keeps the loudest or most senior person from anchoring the group.
  4. Go around the room collecting one reason per person per round until the lists run out, with the leader going first. Hearing the leader name a weakness in their own plan shows that criticism is welcome.
  5. Cluster the reasons and rank them by how likely they are and how much damage they would do. Not every failure mode deserves action, so focus on the few that are both plausible and costly.
  6. For each top risk, assign an owner and a specific change to the plan: a fix, an early warning sign to watch for, or a backup. A premortem that only produces a list of worries changes nothing.
  7. Revisit the list at checkpoints during the project. Some risks fade, and new ones appear once real work starts.

History

Who created it

Psychologist Gary Klein introduced the premortem in a 2007 Harvard Business Review article, 'Performing a Project Premortem.' It builds on 1989 research by Mitchell, Russo, and Pennington on 'prospective hindsight,' which found that imagining an outcome has already happened makes people about 30% better at identifying reasons for it.

What it was created for

Klein designed it for project teams about to commit to a plan, where optimism, groupthink, and reluctance to criticize the leader keep people from voicing doubts.

What it is used for

Teams run it right before launching a project, event, product, or big decision to surface risks early and change the plan while changes are still cheap.

Industries Software and techHealthcareMilitary planningConsultingProduct managementEvent planning

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Make the imagined failure vivid and specific ('We went 2-8 in quals and nobody picked us' beats 'things went wrong'). Specific failures produce specific causes.
  • Run a second round asking 'What did we not see coming?' to push past obvious first answers like 'we ran out of time.'
  • Pair it with a 'pre-parade': imagine a huge success and ask why it happened, to find the strengths the plan must protect.
Watch out

People hold back if a lead dismisses ideas. Write silently first, then share.

Rambots example (made up)

In week 3 of build season, the leads imagine it is the end of their first regional and the robot never scored a game piece in a single match. Students silently write causes: the intake was built last and never tested, the only CAN wiring diagram lived on one student's laptop, and the drive team got 2 hours of practice total. The team moves intake prototyping to week 2, puts the wiring diagram in the shared drive, and books 3 drive practice sessions before the event.

15.4

After Action Review

15 min · People involved · Partly documented

Four fast questions after any event, from the U.S. Army.

What it is

What was supposed to happen, what actually happened, why there was a difference, and what we will do next time. It takes 15 minutes and works best right after the event.

When to use it

Right after a match, a practice, an outreach event, or a drive-team scrimmage.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Answer in order. Facts first, then causes.
  2. Keep it short. Do it within the hour if you can.
  3. End with one or two things to sustain and one or two to improve.
In depth
  1. Hold it soon, ideally the same day, with everyone who took part. Details fade within hours, and anyone left out takes their view of what happened with them.
  2. Set ground rules out loud: talk about what happened, not who is to blame, and everyone speaks as an equal. People only admit their own mistakes when it is safe to do so.
  3. Ask 'What did we set out to do?' and agree on the plan and goals. Teams often discover that people understood the goal differently, which is a lesson in itself.
  4. Ask 'What actually happened?' and rebuild a factual timeline from evidence such as match video, logs, and scouting data. Agree on the facts before arguing about causes.
  5. Ask 'Why did it happen?' for each gap between plan and result, including things that went better than planned. Look for causes in process and preparation, not in personal traits.
  6. Ask 'What will we do next time?' and spend about half the session here. A common split is roughly a quarter of the time on the first two questions, a quarter on why, and half on next steps.
  7. Write down 2 to 4 specific changes with owners and check them at the next event. Lessons that are never acted on were only observed, not learned.

History

Who created it

The US Army developed the After Action Review starting in the mid-1970s to capture lessons from simulated battles at its training centers, including the National Training Center at Fort Irwin, California. Army leaders said it took about a decade to be fully accepted, and the Army formalized it in Training Circular 25-20, A Leader's Guide to After-Action Reviews (1993).

What it was created for

Units needed a fast, honest way to learn from training exercises by comparing what was planned with what actually happened, without turning the discussion into a blame session.

What it is used for

Teams run AARs after any event, project phase, or incident to capture lessons while memories are fresh and turn them into specific changes.

Industries MilitaryWildland firefighting and emergency responseHealthcareSoftware incident reviewsAviationEvent management

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Keep AARs short for small events (10 minutes after each practice) and longer for big ones. Frequent short AARs build the habit.
  • Have someone who did not lead the event facilitate, so the leader can take part honestly.
  • Record 'sustains' (what to keep doing), not only 'improves.' Teams that list only problems lose good practices by accident.
Watch out

It works best within the hour. A week later, memories have already changed.

Rambots example (made up)

After Saturday practice matches, the drive team runs a 15-minute AAR. Plan: score 6 game pieces per match. Actual: they averaged 3, because the driver and operator went for the same game piece twice and the autonomous path missed its second pickup by about 20 cm. Next time, the drive coach calls the target piece out loud, and the programmers retune the path's odometry before Tuesday's practice.

15.5

Sailboat Retro

20-30 min · Whole team · Partly documented

Wind, anchors, rocks, and the island: a retro with a picture.

What it is

The team is a boat sailing to an island (the goal). Wind pushed us forward, anchors held us back, and rocks are risks ahead. The picture makes it easier for people to speak up.

When to use it

End of a build week, an event, or a season. A fresh change from Plus/Delta.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write the island: the goal.
  2. Everyone adds wind, anchors, and rocks.
  3. Pick one anchor to cut and one rock to steer around.
In depth
  1. Draw a sailboat with an island (the goal), wind (what pushes the team forward), anchors (what slows it down), and rocks (risks ahead). Agree on what the island is before anything else so everyone is aiming at the same target.
  2. Give everyone 5 to 8 minutes to write sticky notes silently for each area. Silent writing captures quieter members before the discussion starts.
  3. Have people place their notes and cluster duplicates. Big clusters show which issues many people noticed.
  4. Discuss wind first. Starting with what works builds trust and reminds the team what to protect.
  5. Discuss anchors and rocks next, asking 'why' until you reach a cause the team can change. This keeps the talk on systems instead of blaming people.
  6. Dot-vote to pick the top 2 or 3 anchors or rocks to act on. Picking too many almost guarantees none get done.
  7. Turn each chosen item into an action with an owner and a date, and review those actions at the start of the next retro. Follow-up is what makes retros worth the time.

History

Who created it

It grew out of 'Speed Boat', a game in Luke Hohmann's 2006 book Innovation Games, where a group draws a boat and lists the anchors slowing it down. Agile coaches adapted it into a team retrospective, and coaches such as Marc Loeffler (2015) added wind for the positive forces. Later variants added rocks for risks and an island for the goal; there is no single author of the full sailboat version.

What it was created for

Speed Boat was designed to get customers to say what they disliked about a product in a safe, playful way. The retrospective version lets a team discuss problems and strengths through a picture instead of a blame-heavy list.

What it is used for

Agile and project retrospectives where a team looks back at a sprint, season, or event and decides what to change.

Industries Software developmentProduct managementAgile coachingEducationNonprofitsConsulting

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Change the picture now and then (race car, hot air balloon) to keep retros fresh. The structure matters more than the drawing.
  • For tense topics, have a mentor or a lead from another subteam facilitate so no one is judging their own work.
  • Add a sun for things the team is proud of when morale is low after a rough event.
  • Leave the board up until the next retro so the chosen actions stay visible.
Watch out

The metaphor is fun, but the actions at the end matter most.

Rambots example (made up)

After the team's second event, the leads draw the boat with the island 'Make playoffs at our next regional'. Wind notes include 'Autonomous scored 3 game pieces consistently'; anchors include 'Code freeze was ignored and new bugs showed up Friday'; rocks include 'Only one trained drive coach'. The team votes to enforce a Thursday 8 pm code freeze and train a backup drive coach, assigning each to a lead with a date.

15.6

Quick Debrief

10-15 min · Whole team · Thin record

Go around the room with the team's favorite debrief formats.

What it is

One row per person, so everyone speaks. Pick a format: What went well, What went wrong, What can we do better; High, Low, Buffalo (good, bad, random); Rose, Thorn, Bud (good, bad, has potential); or Plus/Delta. The summary collects everyone's answers by column.

When to use it

End of a meeting, a build day, or a competition day. Two minutes per person.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Pick a format and add everyone's name.
  2. Go around in order. Use the timer if people run long.
  3. Read the summary columns together and write two or three takeaways with owners.
In depth
  1. Pick one format before the meeting and state its prompts clearly: went well / went wrong / do better; Plus/Delta; Rose (a good thing), Thorn (a problem), Bud (an opportunity); or High, Low, Buffalo (something random or funny). A clear format keeps answers short.
  2. Set a time box, such as 30 seconds per person or 10 minutes total. A short limit keeps the debrief quick enough that people will actually do it after every practice.
  3. Go around in order so every person speaks, with the option to pass once. A full go-around surfaces views from people who would never raise a hand.
  4. Have one person write responses where everyone can see them, for example a whiteboard with a plus column and a delta column. Visible notes show people they were heard and leave a record.
  5. Phrase negatives as changes, not blame ('next time we charge batteries before lunch', not 'Alex forgot the batteries'). That is the reason Plus/Delta uses delta, meaning change, instead of minus.
  6. Close by picking one or two changes to try next time and naming who owns each. Without that step the debrief is just venting.
  7. Start the next debrief by checking the last one's changes. Closing the loop shows the team that speaking up leads to action.

History

Who created it

No single origin. 'What went well, what went wrong, what to do better' is a generic after-action pattern with no known author. Plus/Delta (what worked, and what to change, using the Greek letter delta for change) is widely used in quality improvement, education, and healthcare simulation debriefing, but no inventor is documented. Rose-Thorn-Bud was popularized as a design method by the LUMA Institute, which says it adapted a reflection habit taught in the Boy Scouts of America; High-Low-Buffalo is an informal family, camp, and classroom check-in game with no known creator.

What it was created for

Getting quick, honest reflection from everyone after an activity without the time cost of a full retrospective. Fixed prompts make it easy for nervous or quiet people to speak.

What it is used for

End-of-meeting check-outs, post-match or post-event huddles, classroom closings, simulation debriefs in medical training, and family dinner conversations.

Industries EducationHealthcare simulation trainingDesignQuality improvementYouth programs and campsSoftware teams

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Use High-Low-Buffalo for bonding or with new members; use Plus/Delta when you need actionable changes.
  • Have the leader or mentor speak last so they do not set the tone for everyone else.
  • If the same delta shows up three sessions in a row, move it to a full retrospective or a task card with an owner.
  • For large groups, debrief in subteams first, then each subteam shares its top plus and top delta.
Watch out

One person talking for five minutes kills the round. Use the timer.

Rambots example (made up)

After each qualification match at a regional, the drive team runs a 2-minute Plus/Delta in the pit. Plus: 'Autonomous path picked up all 3 game pieces'. Delta: 'Driver and operator both called for intake; from now on only the operator calls it'. The pit lead writes both on a small whiteboard, and the new callout rule is tested in the next match.

15.7

Recognition Board

Ongoing · Whole team · Partly documented

Collect shoutouts and caught-in-the-act moments at events, so nobody gets missed.

What it is

A running log of who did something great, during a competition or a build week. Prompt cards help people remember. The roster check shows who has not been recognized yet, so quiet contributors are not overlooked.

When to use it

During competitions and big build days. Read the list aloud at the end of the day.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Paste the roster, one name per line.
  2. Add shoutouts as they happen. Tap a prompt if you are stuck.
  3. Before the end-of-day huddle, check the "Not recognized yet" list and look for something real to recognize.
  4. Copy as text and read it out.
In depth
  1. Decide which values the board recognizes, such as safety, helping other subteams, gracious professionalism, and finishing what you start. Tying shoutouts to values tells everyone what the team actually cares about.
  2. Make it easy to post: a whiteboard by the shop door, a Slack or Discord channel, or a card box, open to everyone including mentors and new members. Recognition from peers matters as much as recognition from leads.
  3. Require specifics: who, what they did, and what it made possible ('Maya re-crimped every loose connector, so we passed inspection on the first try'). Specific praise teaches others what good work looks like; vague praise feels empty.
  4. Post shoutouts close to the moment, ideally the same day. Quick recognition links the praise to the action.
  5. Read a few aloud at the end of the week or meeting. Public recognition spreads the effect and models the habit for new members.
  6. Once a month, have a lead check for people who never get recognized and make sure their work gets noticed. Boards tend to drift toward the most visible roles, like drivers and CAD.
  7. Keep it non-competitive: no leaderboards or prizes for the most shoutouts. Counting turns recognition into a popularity contest.

History

Who created it

No single inventor, and it is not an official FIRST program (FIRST's own culture centers on Gracious Professionalism and event awards, not team shoutout boards). Team recognition boards borrow from school positive-behavior systems such as PBIS (Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports), which grew out of University of Oregon research in the 1980s and gained a federally funded national center through the 1997 IDEA reauthorization, and from 'caught being good' style acknowledgment programs. They also mirror the peer-to-peer recognition programs common in workplaces.

What it was created for

PBIS-style acknowledgment was designed to increase good behavior by noticing and reinforcing it often, instead of relying mainly on punishment. Workplace peer recognition aims to keep people engaged and make good work visible.

What it is used for

Public boards, chat channels, or meeting segments where anyone can thank a teammate for specific help, effort, or behavior that matches the team's values.

Industries K-12 educationWorkplace HRYouth sportsNonprofits and volunteer groupsHealthcareSoftware companies

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Seed it: leads should post the first few shoutouts each week until the habit forms.
  • Recognize effort and behavior, not only results. A well-documented failed test deserves credit too.
  • Save photos of the board; they help when writing award essays and choosing end-of-year awards.
  • Offer a private thank-you option for people who dislike public attention.

Sponsor Value Canvas

30-45 min · 2-4 · Well documented

Match what a sponsor cares about with what the team can offer.

What it is

Based on the value proposition canvas. On the left, describe the sponsor: what they are trying to do, what frustrates them, and what would delight them. On the right, describe what the team offers that relieves those pains and creates those gains.

When to use it

Before a sponsor meeting or a grant application.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Research the sponsor first: their website, values, and community programs.
  2. Fill the sponsor side before the team side.
  3. Only claim what the team can actually deliver. Then write your ask.
In depth
  1. Pick one specific sponsor or sponsor type, such as a local machine shop versus a large company's community-giving program. Different sponsors want different things, so one generic canvas leads to a generic pitch.
  2. Fill in the sponsor's jobs: what they are trying to accomplish, such as finding future engineers, meeting community-giving goals, building local reputation, or giving employees volunteer opportunities. Check their website, annual report, or past giving before guessing.
  3. List their pains: what makes those jobs hard or risky, such as not knowing whether a donation made a difference, paperwork, or risk to their brand. Pains are often the real reason a sponsor says no.
  4. List their gains: outcomes that would please them, such as photos for their social media, students visiting their facility, or a mention in an award submission. Ask past sponsors directly what they valued most.
  5. On the team side, list what you offer (logo placement, demos, outreach events, impact reports), then write a pain reliever for each sponsor pain and a gain creator for each gain. Every offer should connect to something on the sponsor side; offers that connect to nothing are clutter.
  6. Check the fit: circle the sponsor's top 2 or 3 jobs, pains, and gains and make sure your pitch opens with how you address them. The pitch should talk about the sponsor's goals first and the robot second.
  7. Test the canvas in a real conversation and update it after every meeting. Until a sponsor confirms them, everything on the canvas is a guess.

History

Who created it

The Sponsor Value Canvas is this guide's adaptation, not a published framework. It reuses the structure of the Value Proposition Canvas, which Alexander Osterwalder, Yves Pigneur, and co-authors published in Value Proposition Design (2014) as a zoomed-in view of the value proposition block of their Business Model Canvas.

What it was created for

The original canvas helps companies match products to customer needs by mapping customer jobs, pains, and gains against the products, pain relievers, and gain creators that answer them. This adaptation treats a sponsor (a company, foundation, or donor) as the customer.

What it is used for

Teams use the original to design products and pitches that fit what customers actually need; this version is used to plan sponsor pitches and renewals by working out what a sponsor wants from supporting a robotics team.

Industries Nonprofit fundraisingStartupsProduct managementMarketingSalesStudent organizations

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Make separate canvases for the person who approves the money and the employee who champions you inside the company; they often want different things.
  • Turn each gain creator into a dated item on the team calendar, such as a mid-season update email with photos, so promises actually get delivered.
  • At renewal time, ask which benefits mattered most and drop the ones nobody valued.
Watch out

Do not invent sponsor pains. Research them or ask.

For a local CNC machine shop, the canvas shows the shop's main job is finding future machinists, its pain is that local students never see manufacturing careers, and a gain would be seeing its parts on a competition robot. The team offers a shop tour for 10 students, a 'machined by' label on the robot's gearbox plates, and a list of interested students for summer internships, and asks for $3,000 in machining time instead of cash.

Business Model Canvas

45-60 min · 3-6 · Well documented

See how the team sustains itself: who supports it, what it offers, and what it costs.

What it is

Alexander Osterwalder's nine-block canvas, adapted for a robotics team. It shows on one page how value flows from the team to the people who support it, and how money and help flow back.

When to use it

Season planning, sustainability plans, Impact award essays, and grant narratives.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Start with the middle: what value the team creates.
  2. Fill who it is for, how you reach them, and how you keep them.
  3. Then the engine: activities, resources, partners. End with costs and funding.
In depth
  1. Print the canvas large and work with sticky notes. Early drafts change fast, and the canvas works best when everything is visible at once.
  2. Start with Customer Segments: who you serve. For a robotics team this can include students and families, sponsors, the school, and the community, each with different needs.
  3. Fill in Value Propositions for each segment, then Channels (how you reach them) and Customer Relationships (how you keep them). Use one sticky note color per segment so you can trace each one across the canvas.
  4. Fill in Revenue Streams: where money comes from, such as sponsors, grants, fundraisers, and team fees. Note how reliable each one is, since one-time sources must be replaced every year.
  5. Fill in the infrastructure blocks: Key Resources (shop, mentors, tools), Key Activities (build season, outreach, fundraising), and Key Partners (sponsors who donate machining, the school, nearby teams).
  6. Fill in Cost Structure (registration, parts, travel, tools) and compare it to Revenue Streams. The gap tells you how much fundraising has to cover.
  7. Read the finished canvas for weak links: a segment with no channel, a resource with no owner, or a cost with no revenue behind it. Then sketch an alternative canvas and compare the two.

History

Who created it

Alexander Osterwalder proposed the canvas's nine building blocks in 2005, based on his 2004 PhD work on business model ontology supervised by Yves Pigneur. The 2010 book Business Model Generation, by Osterwalder and Pigneur, was co-created with 470 practitioners from 45 countries and made the canvas widely used.

What it was created for

Osterwalder wanted a shared visual language for describing how an organization creates, delivers, and captures value, so teams could discuss and redesign a business model on one page instead of in a long business plan.

What it is used for

Startups, companies, and nonprofits use it to map, test, and compare business models and to spot weak links, such as a value proposition with no way to reach customers or no revenue behind it.

Industries Startups and entrepreneurshipCorporate strategyNonprofits and social enterprisesBusiness educationConsultingProduct management

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Draw a current-state canvas and a next-season canvas, and turn the differences into projects.
  • Make each sticky note specific enough to test ('3 local manufacturers donate machining') rather than generic ('partners').
  • Use the Value Proposition Canvas (or the Sponsor Value Canvas) to zoom in on any segment where the value is unclear.
Watch out

For a team, "customers" means supporters. Do not force business language that confuses students.

The team maps its program and finds that 70% of revenue comes from two corporate sponsors, while travel to a second regional is its biggest new cost. Empathy Map

20-30 min · 2-5 · Partly documented

Understand the person you are designing for or reaching out to.

What it is

A design-thinking tool. Pick one real person or a realistic persona and write what they say, think, do, and feel. Pains and gains show what to design for.

When to use it

Designing an assistive device, planning an outreach event, or recruiting new members.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Pick one specific person, not "everyone".
  2. Fill Says and Does from what you have actually seen or heard.
  3. Guess carefully at Thinks and Feels, then check your guesses with them.
In depth
  1. Put one specific person or role at the center, such as 'a 9th grader at the club fair.' A map about 'everyone' tells you nothing.
  2. Write the goal at the top: what this person needs to do and what action you want them to take. Gray's updated canvas starts here because it ties the map to a real situation.
  3. Fill in what they See and Hear: their surroundings, what friends, parents, and teachers say, and what they notice online. This shows what is influencing their choices.
  4. Fill in what they Say and Do in public, using real quotes and observed behavior where possible. Observations beat guesses, so interview or watch people first if you can.
  5. Fill in what they Think and Feel, including worries they may not say out loud and what they hope for. The gap between what people say and what they feel is often where the useful insight is.
  6. Summarize their Pains (fears, frustrations, obstacles) and Gains (wants and what success looks like to them). These become requirements for whatever you design.
  7. Pick the 2 or 3 most important insights and turn each one into a change you will try. A map that does not change what you do was only a poster.

History

Who created it

The empathy map came out of the design firm XPLANE in the mid-2000s, where creative director Scott Matthews drew 'big head' sketches to capture how a client's audience thinks, and founder Dave Gray refined the idea. It spread through the 2010 book Gamestorming by Gray, Sunni Brown, and James Macanufo, and Gray later published an updated canvas that starts with the person's goal.

What it was created for

XPLANE needed a quick workshop tool to build a shared picture of the people a design or message was meant for before anyone started designing.

What it is used for

Design, product, and marketing teams use it to step into a user's or stakeholder's point of view and agree on their needs, often as part of design thinking or persona work.

Industries UX and product designMarketingEducationHealthcareSoftwareNonprofits

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Make one map per audience instead of blending them. Rookies, returning members, and parents each deserve their own.
  • Build it from interview notes, not imagination, and mark guesses in a different color so you know what still needs checking.
  • Revisit the map after the event to see which assumptions turned out wrong.
Watch out

Thinks and Feels are guesses until you check them with the real person.

Rambots example (made up)

Before the fall recruiting fair, the leads map 'a 9th grader who has never built anything.' This student sees a 50 kg robot and hears 'you have to be good at math,' says 'that looks cool,' but thinks 'I would just be in the way' and feels nervous about joining an established group. 10-10-10

5-10 min · Solo · Well documented

How will you feel about this in 10 minutes, 10 months, and 10 years?

What it is

Suzy Welch's tool for decisions that feel huge right now. Looking at three time scales separates short-term discomfort from long-term consequences.

When to use it

Personal decisions: quitting a subteam, taking a leadership role, choosing between commitments.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write the decision and the option you are leaning toward.
  2. Answer each time scale honestly.
  3. Notice where the answers disagree. The longer time scales usually matter more.
In depth
  1. Frame the decision as a clear choice between specific options. 'Do I go to the off-season event or the family trip?' works better than 'What should I do about October?'
  2. Gather the facts first: dates, costs, who is affected, and what each option commits you to. The tool sorts consequences; it does not replace knowing the facts.
  3. For each option, ask what the consequences will be in 10 minutes. Naming the immediate emotions (relief, awkwardness, disappointment) keeps them from secretly driving the decision.
  4. Ask what the consequences will be in 10 months. Medium-term effects such as trust with teammates, skills learned, or grades usually show up here.
  5. Ask what the consequences will be in 10 years. Many short-term worries disappear at this scale, while choices about relationships and values tend to grow.
  6. Compare the answers against what matters most to you. Welch says the method works because it forces you to face your real values, so name them before you decide.
  7. Make the call, and if it affects others, explain your reasoning across all three time frames. People accept a hard decision more easily when they can see you weighed the long term.

History

Who created it

Business writer Suzy Welch created 10-10-10 out of her own struggles with hard choices, first shared it while writing for O, The Oprah Magazine, and expanded it into the 2009 book 10-10-10: A Life-Transforming Idea.

What it was created for

Welch built it for personal and work decisions, especially work-life balance choices, where the pull of the moment hides the long-term consequences.

What it is used for

People use it to slow down hard decisions by asking how each option will play out in 10 minutes, 10 months, and 10 years.

Industries Leadership coachingPersonal decision-makingEducation and counselingHuman resourcesManagement

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • The numbers are not literal. Use 10 days or 10 weeks if that fits the decision better; the point is to look at three very different time scales.
  • Write it as a small table, with options as rows and the three time frames as columns, so you can see exactly where the options differ.
  • Use it for leadership calls, like whether to cut a struggling mechanism, to weigh the team's frustration today against the season result.
Watch out

Works for personal choices. It is not meant for team engineering decisions.

Rambots example (made up)

Two days before a regional, the build lead must decide whether to install an untested climber redesign. In 10 minutes, keeping the old climber feels like giving up; in 10 months, a robot that broke in quals because of an untested part is what the team would remember; in 10 years, nobody will recall the climber, but students will remember whether the team trusted its testing process. The lead keeps the proven climber for this event and schedules the redesign for practice-day testing at the next one.

One-way vs Two-way Door

5 min · Any · Well documented

Check if a decision is reversible, so you know whether to decide fast or slow.

What it is

A two-way door can be walked back through: decide fast, try it, adjust. A one-way door cannot be undone easily: slow down and use a heavier framework. Most decisions are two-way doors that teams treat as one-way.

When to use it

Any time a discussion is dragging on, or a decision feels permanent.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write the decision.
  2. Answer the six questions.
  3. Follow the verdict: decide fast, reduce the risk first, or slow down.
In depth
  1. Write the decision as one specific sentence (for example, 'Switch the intake from 2-inch to 4-inch compliant wheels'). A vague decision cannot be sorted, because part of it may be reversible and part may not.
  2. Ask: if we do this and it goes badly, what would it cost to go back, in money, time, and trust? List the real undo cost, not just whether undoing is technically possible.
  3. Check for hidden one-way parts, such as a non-refundable order, a deadline that will pass, a public promise, or material that gets cut. These are what turn a two-way door into a one-way door.
  4. If it is a two-way door, name one owner, set a short deadline, and decide with the information you have (in his 2016 letter Bezos suggested about 70 percent of the information you wish you had). Waiting for perfect data on a cheap decision wastes build time.
  5. If it is a one-way door, slow down on purpose: gather data, ask the mentors and leads who are affected, and write down the reasoning. The extra time is worth it because you will live with the result.
  6. Look for ways to turn a one-way door into a two-way door, such as prototyping in wood or 3D print before cutting aluminum, or buying one part before buying ten. This lets you move fast on more decisions.
  7. For two-way doors, set a check-back date and a clear 'undo' signal (for example, 'if cycle time is not under 3 seconds by Saturday, we revert'). Without this, reversible decisions quietly become permanent.

History

Who created it

Jeff Bezos laid out the idea in Amazon's 2015 letter to shareholders (published April 2016), calling irreversible decisions 'Type 1' or one-way doors and reversible ones 'Type 2' or two-way doors. Some articles say it came from his 1997 letter, but the door language and quotes they cite come from the 2015 letter.

What it was created for

Bezos was warning that as Amazon grew, people were using a slow, heavy approval process for every decision, including easy-to-undo ones, which made the company slow and afraid to experiment.

What it is used for

Teams use it to decide how much time, data, and sign-off a decision needs before acting. It is common shorthand for 'just try it, we can undo it' versus 'stop and think carefully.'

Industries Tech and softwareStartupsProduct managementOperationsEngineering leadershipNonprofits

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Most decisions are two-way doors. If your team treats everything as a one-way door, that is the problem Bezos was describing.
  • Pair it with 'disagree and commit,' which Bezos described in his 2016 letter: on a two-way door, people can disagree, commit, and revisit with data later.
  • Reversibility changes over time. A design choice is a two-way door in week 1 of build season and often a one-way door the week before your first event.
  • Label decisions out loud in meetings ('this is a two-way door, Maya owns it, decide by Thursday') so the group stops debating small things.
Watch out

Teams treat two-way doors as one-way and stall. If you can undo it cheaply, decide today.

Rambots example (made up)

In week 2 of build season, the team debates whether to use a single-jointed arm or an elevator for scoring. Choosing which to prototype first is a two-way door, so the mechanical lead picks the arm and they test it by Saturday. Ordering a $600 custom gearbox and machining the elevator tubes is closer to a one-way door, so they wait until both prototypes are tested and the build mentor signs off.

Six Thinking Hats

20-40 min · 4-10 · Well documented

Have the whole group think the same way at the same time.

What it is

Edward de Bono's method. Instead of arguing, everyone wears the same hat at once: facts, then feelings, risks, benefits, new ideas, and process. It stops one loud voice from steering the discussion.

When to use it

A group discussion that keeps going in circles, or a big design review.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. The leader wears the Blue hat and picks the order.
  2. Spend a few minutes per hat. Everyone contributes under that hat only.
  3. End with the Blue hat: summary and next steps.
In depth
  1. Learn the six hats: White (facts and missing information), Red (feelings and gut reactions, no justification needed), Black (risks and why it might fail), Yellow (benefits and why it might work), Green (new ideas and alternatives), Blue (managing the process). Everyone must know these before starting, or the session turns into normal arguing.
  2. Start with Blue: the facilitator states the question and the order of hats. A clear order keeps the group from jumping straight to criticism.
  3. Run White next so everyone shares the same facts, such as test results, budget, and dates, and lists what they do not know yet. This stops arguments that are really about different data.
  4. Use Green and Yellow before Black for new ideas, so fragile ideas get developed before they get attacked. Use Black before Yellow when evaluating a nearly final plan, so risks are not skipped.
  5. Keep Red short (about 30 seconds per person) and do not let anyone argue with a feeling. This gets hidden concerns into the open without a debate.
  6. Time-box each hat (2 to 5 minutes) and have everyone wear the same hat together. If someone raises a risk during Yellow, the facilitator writes it down for the Black round.
  7. End with Blue: summarize what was learned and decide the next step or who owns the decision. Without this the session feels productive but nothing changes.

History

Who created it

Edward de Bono, a Maltese-born physician and writer on thinking, published Six Thinking Hats in 1985. He had used a colored-hat metaphor earlier, in his 1971 book Lateral Thinking for Management.

What it was created for

De Bono wanted to fix meetings where people argue past each other, with one person attacking an idea while another is still describing it. His answer was 'parallel thinking': everyone looks at the problem from the same angle at the same time.

What it is used for

Groups use it to run structured discussions, review proposals, and keep critics and optimists from talking over each other. Teachers also use it to teach critical thinking.

Industries Corporate trainingEducationGovernment and civil serviceProduct designHealthcare administrationConsulting

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Rotate a known critic into the Yellow role and a known optimist into Black when you discuss in turns. It builds empathy and breaks habits.
  • You do not need all six hats every time. A quick 'White, Black, Green' pass is enough for a small decision.
  • Research evidence that the method improves thinking in general is limited, so treat it as a meeting structure, not a proven skill booster.
  • Physical props (colored cards or sticky notes) help newer members remember which mode the group is in.
Watch out

It fails if people switch hats freely. The facilitator enforces one hat at a time.

Rambots example (made up)

The team is deciding whether to add a second climber hook for the endgame. White: the current climb takes 9 seconds and works 7 of 10 tries. Black: a second hook adds 1.2 lb and another failure point. Yellow: a double climb is worth extra ranking points at regionals. Green: someone suggests a passive latch instead. Blue closes by assigning a two-day prototype test of the latch.

Dot Voting

5-10 min · Any · Well documented

Let the group prioritize quickly with a fixed number of votes each.

What it is

Each person gets a few dots and places them on the options they like most, more than one on the same option if they feel strongly. It is fast, and quieter people get an equal say.

When to use it

After a brainstorm, to pick which ideas to work on.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. List the options.
  2. Set the number of voters and dots per person.
  3. Everyone votes silently first, then tally here. Discuss the top few, not the whole list.
In depth
  1. Get all options posted where everyone can see them, one idea per sticky note or line, and merge duplicates first. Similar ideas left separate will split votes and hide real support.
  2. Make sure everyone understands each option by giving each author 30 seconds to explain it. People cannot vote well on an idea they misunderstand.
  3. Give each person a fixed number of dots, usually about a third of the number of options (for example, 4 dots for 12 options). Too many dots makes everything look popular; too few hides second choices.
  4. Decide the rules before voting: can people stack dots on one option, and do they vote for 'most important' or 'do first'? Unclear rules make results meaningless.
  5. Have everyone vote at the same time, or privately first, then place dots. Voting in sequence lets people copy whoever went first (a bandwagon effect).
  6. Count and rank, then discuss the top few and any surprises. The dots start the decision; they are not the final answer.
  7. Record the results and the decision owner. A photo of the board is enough, and it helps when people later ask why something was chosen.

History

Who created it

No single inventor. Voting with dot stickers (also called multi-voting) was a common facilitation technique before anyone named it, and it is a form of cumulative voting. Jason Diceman created the 'Dotmocracy' sheet format in 2004 and published a handbook starting in 2006, which made the name popular.

What it was created for

It was meant to let a large group quickly show which of many ideas have the most support, without long debate or one loud voice deciding.

What it is used for

Teams use it to narrow a long list of ideas, pick priorities, and see where the group's energy is. It shows up in workshops, retrospectives, and design sprints, often with digital dots on online whiteboards.

Industries Agile software teamsDesign and UXCommunity planningNonprofitsEducationWorkshops and facilitation

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Use different colors for different questions (green for value, red for concerns) or for different groups (students vs mentors) to see disagreements.
  • Diceman's Dotmocracy version uses an agreement scale (strongly agree to strongly disagree) on each idea instead of just counting votes, which shows opposition as well as support.
  • If a leader's vote could sway others, have leaders vote last or not at all.
  • Check for a long tail: if no option gets more than about 20 percent of dots, the group has not converged and needs more discussion.
Watch out

People copy the first dots they see. Vote silently and reveal together.

Rambots example (made up)

After the season debrief, 30 students list 18 off-season project ideas. Each gets 5 dots and votes on 'what we should do before kickoff.' Swerve drive practice gets 41 dots, a new pit cart gets 28, and a scouting app rewrite gets 25, so leads pick the top two and assign owners while the app rewrite goes to a small volunteer group.

Brainwriting 6-3-5

30 min · 4-8 · Well documented

Silent brainstorming: 6 people, 3 ideas each, 5 minutes per round.

What it is

Invented by Bernd Rohrbach. Everyone writes 3 ideas silently in 5 minutes, then passes the sheet and builds on what is already there. Six people and six rounds can produce 108 ideas in 30 minutes, and quiet people contribute as much as loud ones.

When to use it

When the same few voices dominate brainstorms, or when you need lots of ideas fast.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write the problem at the top.
  2. Each round, write 3 ideas on your sheet in 5 minutes.
  3. Pass sheets to the right and build on the ideas above.
  4. Afterward, sort the ideas with an Affinity Diagram.
In depth
  1. Write one clear problem statement at the top of every sheet. If people answer slightly different questions, building on each other's ideas falls apart.
  2. Give each of the six participants a sheet with a grid of 3 columns and 6 rows. Each row is one round and each column is one idea.
  3. In round one, everyone silently writes three ideas in the first row within 5 minutes, using short phrases or quick sketches. Silence stops the group from anchoring on the first idea said out loud.
  4. Pass each sheet to the person on the right. Read the ideas already on it, then add three new ideas that extend, combine, or contrast with them. Later rounds can run a little longer since there is more to read, but keep it to 10 minutes at most.
  5. Repeat until all six rows are filled. Six rounds gives up to 108 ideas in about 30 minutes.
  6. Post every sheet, merge duplicates, and give each person 3 to 5 dot votes. This turns a pile of ideas into a short list.
  7. Evaluate the top ideas with a separate method such as a decision matrix. 6-3-5 is for generating options, so judging during the rounds defeats the purpose.

History

Who created it

Developed by Bernd Rohrbach, who published it in 1968 in the German sales magazine Absatzwirtschaft. The name describes the format: 6 people, 3 ideas each, 5 minutes per round.

What it was created for

Rohrbach wanted a structured way for a group to generate many ideas without the problems of spoken brainstorming, where a few loud voices dominate and ideas get lost. Writing lets everyone contribute at once and build on each other's ideas.

What it is used for

Teams use it for silent, parallel idea generation in workshops, engineering concept work, and remote meetings using shared documents. It is popular when a group has strong personalities or quiet members.

Industries Engineering and product developmentMarketing and advertisingManufacturingEducationConsulting and facilitationSoftware teams

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • The numbers are flexible: 4 to 8 people works fine as long as the grid has one row per person.
  • For hybrid meetings, use a shared spreadsheet with one tab per person and rotate tabs each round.
  • Allow sketches. For mechanism problems, a 10-second drawing often says more than a sentence.
  • Mix subteams (mechanical, programming, business) so each sheet collects different points of view.
Watch out

Bad handwriting and vague ideas slow it down. Ask for short, specific phrases.

Crazy 8s

10-15 min · 3-8 · Partly documented

Sketch 8 different ideas in 8 minutes.

What it is

A design-sprint exercise from Google Ventures. One minute per sketch is too fast to polish, so people stop judging their ideas and get past the obvious first one.

When to use it

Mechanism concepts, logo or shirt designs, pit layouts, app screens.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write what you are designing.
  2. Start the timer. The highlighted box moves every minute.
  3. Sketch anything, fast. Add a caption.
  4. Share, then pick ideas worth prototyping.
In depth
  1. Each person folds a sheet of paper in half three times to make 8 panels and grabs a thick marker. A thick marker blocks fine detail, which keeps people from polishing one idea.
  2. Spend 2 to 3 minutes reviewing the problem and any notes or research first. Crazy 8s works best when people bring raw material instead of starting cold.
  3. Set a timer for 8 minutes and have the facilitator call out each minute. One minute per panel keeps the pace steady.
  4. Sketch one variation per panel. Small changes to the same idea are fine. The first two or three panels usually hold the obvious ideas, and the value is in panels four through eight.
  5. Do not erase or tidy. Boxes, arrows, stick figures, and a few words are enough.
  6. When time is up, each person circles their best one or two panels. Choosing your own favorite is a quick filter before group discussion.
  7. Share the circled panels in about a minute each, or post them silently on a wall, then develop the strongest into a more detailed solution sketch.

History

Who created it

There is no documented single inventor. The exercise was in use at Google Ventures (GV) by 2013, when Fast Company described GV's 'Crazy 8s' as eight sketches at 40 seconds each. It became widely known through the design sprint process Jake Knapp developed at Google and GV around 2010 to 2012 and his 2016 book 'Sprint', and the common form today is eight sketches in eight minutes.

What it was created for

It was used on the sketching day of a design sprint to push people past their first idea and get several rough variations on paper quickly, before the team commits to one direction.

What it is used for

Designers, product teams, and classrooms use it as a fast warm-up or idea stretch for screen layouts, product concepts, and any problem that can be sketched.

Industries UX and product designSoftwareStartupsMarketingEducationGame design

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Run a second round after people see each other's sketches. Borrowing and remixing in round two often gives the best ideas.
  • For beginners, start at 90 seconds per panel and tighten to 60 once they get used to it.
  • Give each panel a constraint ('cheapest', 'fewest motors', 'one hand') to force a wider range.
  • It also works for non-robot problems like pit layouts, outreach flyers, or social media post designs.
Watch out

People try to draw well. Remind them stick figures are the goal.

Worst Possible Idea

10-15 min · 3-10 · Partly documented

Come up with terrible ideas on purpose, then flip them.

What it is

Bad ideas are easy and fun, so everyone relaxes and joins in. Then you look for what is hidden in each bad idea or flip it into its opposite. The flips are often surprisingly good.

When to use it

Warming up a quiet group, or when the team is stuck on the same safe ideas.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write the challenge.
  2. List the worst ideas you can think of. Laughing is the point.
  3. For each one, ask what is good hidden in it, or what its opposite would be.
In depth
  1. State the real challenge, then ask for the worst possible solutions, one per sticky note. Writing them down makes them easy to sort later.
  2. Set a short timer (5 to 10 minutes) and push for volume and exaggeration. When everyone is trying to be bad, nobody risks looking foolish.
  3. Post all the notes and have the group pick the most interesting bad ideas, not just the funniest.
  4. For each chosen idea, list what specifically makes it bad. Naming the bad properties is where the useful information appears.
  5. Flip each bad property into its opposite. The opposite often points to a real requirement or a fresh idea.
  6. Check whether any bad idea is only bad in one context. Some contain a piece worth keeping, such as an unusual material or channel.
  7. Rewrite the flipped ideas as real options and send them into your normal evaluation process.

History

Who created it

No single documented inventor; deliberately generating bad ideas is an old creativity and improv trick. The Interaction Design Foundation credits innovation consultant Bryan Mattimore with coining the name 'worst possible idea', and it is now taught as a standard design thinking ideation method.

What it was created for

It was meant to unstick groups whose brainstorms had stalled because people were afraid to suggest anything that might sound dumb. Asking for terrible ideas removes that fear.

What it is used for

Facilitators use it as a warm-up, to restart a stale session, and to expose hidden assumptions by flipping bad ideas into good ones.

Industries Design thinking and UXAdvertisingEducationMuseums and cultural organizationsCorporate innovation workshopsProduct management

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Use it as a 10-minute warm-up before a real brainstorm, especially with new members who are still shy.
  • The facilitator should offer the first ridiculous idea to show it is safe.
  • Set one rule: bad ideas can make fun of the problem but never of people.
  • Try combining two awful ideas. The combination is sometimes surprisingly workable.
Watch out

Without the flip step, it is just jokes. Save time for rescuing ideas.

For the fall club fair, the outreach team lists the worst ways to recruit freshmen, such as 'require a calculus test to join', 'only talk about wiring', and 'keep the robot under a tarp'. Flipping them gives 'no prerequisites, printed on the poster', 'let visitors drive the robot', and 'run the robot in the open next to a sign-up QR code'. 18.4

How Might We

10-15 min · 2-8 · Partly documented

Turn a problem into questions that invite ideas.

What it is

A design-thinking habit. "Our intake jams" leads to blame. "How might we pick up game pieces from any angle?" leads to ideas. Write several versions, from broad to narrow, then pick one to brainstorm on.

When to use it

Right before a brainstorm, so everyone answers the same well-framed question.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write the problem or insight.
  2. Write several How Might We questions. Too broad gives no direction; too narrow already contains the answer.
  3. Pick one and brainstorm.
In depth
  1. Start from an observed problem or insight, not a solution, and write it plainly ('The pit crew cannot find tools during a short turnaround'). Starting from a solution locks in one answer.
  2. Rewrite it as 'How might we...'. 'How' assumes it can be solved, 'might' gives permission for imperfect ideas, and 'we' makes it a shared job.
  3. Check the scope. 'HMW add a pegboard' hides a solution and is too narrow; 'HMW make the pit better' is too broad to spark ideas. Aim for the middle.
  4. Write 5 to 10 variations using different angles: amplify what works, remove what hurts, flip the problem, question an assumption, or break it into smaller parts. Different angles lead to different idea pools.
  5. Pick one to three questions that the group finds most energizing and that fit your real constraints (time, budget, rules).
  6. Use the chosen question as the header for the actual idea session, such as brainwriting or Crazy 8s, and post it where everyone can see it.
  7. After the first round of ideas, check the spread. If every idea looks the same, the question was too narrow, so widen it and go again.

History

Who created it

The exact first use is disputed. Sidney Parnes's 1967 'Creative Behavior Guidebook' used question 'invitation stems' to open problem statements, and Min Basadur, who learned creative problem solving from Parnes and Alex Osborn around 1971, says he coined 'How might we' and brought it to Procter & Gamble in the early 1970s. IDEO, the Stanford d.school, and Google's design sprint later made it a standard part of design thinking.

What it was created for

Basadur saw that capable P&G teams often defined problems poorly and ended up solving the wrong one. The phrase was meant to turn complaints and negative problem statements into open, hopeful questions that invite many answers.

What it is used for

Teams use HMW questions to turn research findings or pain points into brainstorming prompts that are neither too narrow nor too broad.

Industries Design thinking and UXConsumer productsHealthcare innovationEducationSoftwarePublic sector and nonprofits

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Quick test: if you cannot think of five different answers in a minute, the question is too narrow.
  • Name the person you are helping ('HMW help a rookie driver...') to keep the question grounded.
  • Keep the original problem statement next to the HMW so the group can check they have not drifted.
  • Avoid stacking goals ('faster, cheaper, and lighter'). One clear aim per question works better.
Watch out

Too broad ("How might we win?") or too narrow (already contains the answer). Aim for the middle.

At a regional, the robot nearly misses two matches because battery swaps and fixes run late. The pit lead rewrites the complaint as 'How might we get the robot match-ready within 4 minutes of returning to the pit?' instead of 'HMW buy a battery cart'. Reverse Brainstorming

15-20 min · 3-8 · Partly documented

Ask how to cause the problem, then reverse each answer.

What it is

It is easier to imagine how to make things worse. Each way to cause the problem points at something to prevent.

When to use it

Safety, reliability, and process problems.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write the problem.
  2. Ask: how could we cause this, or make it worse?
  3. Reverse each answer into a solution.
In depth
  1. Write the real goal clearly, for example 'Keep the robot connected for every match'. The reversal only works if the original goal is precise.
  2. Reverse it: 'How could we make the robot lose connection?' or 'How could we make this problem worse?'
  3. Generate as many causes as possible without judging. Push for specifics ('leave the radio power cable loose') over general ones ('bad wiring'), since specific causes flip into specific fixes.
  4. Group the reversed ideas into themes such as hardware, preparation, and communication. Themes show where risk is concentrated.
  5. Flip each idea into a solution or preventive action ('pull-test the radio connector before every match').
  6. Mark which 'make it worse' items are already happening. Those are your top priorities because they are real, not hypothetical.
  7. Choose the most important fixes, assign an owner to each, and set a date to check that they are done.

History

Who created it

No single inventor. It grew out of the group brainstorming that Alex Osborn popularized in the 1940s and 1950s, combined with the much older problem-solving habit of inversion (asking how to cause a problem instead of how to fix it). It is also called negative brainstorming.

What it was created for

It helps groups find solutions when direct brainstorming stalls, because people often find it easier to list ways things go wrong than ways to make them go right.

What it is used for

Teams use it to spot risks and failure modes, improve processes and customer service, and prepare checklists before high-stakes events.

Industries Quality and process improvementCustomer serviceProduct designProject managementHealthcare safetySoftware testing

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Run it as a pre-mortem a week before competition.
  • Turn the flipped items directly into pit and pre-match checklists.
  • Keep the reversed list and the solution list side by side so people see how each fix connects to a cause.
  • Aim the reversals at processes and equipment, not at teammates, so the session does not turn into blame.
Watch out

Keep it about systems, not people. "Let Ben drive" is not a cause.

The team lists about 30 ways, including a loose radio power cable, an unsecured battery, a tripped drivetrain breaker, and wires rubbing through near the swerve modules. 18.6

Round Robin

10-20 min · 3-10 · Partly documented

Go around in order. Each person adds an idea or passes.

What it is

A simple structure that gives everyone a turn. It ends when everyone passes in a row. A short turn timer keeps it moving.

When to use it

Small groups, team meetings, or online calls where people talk over each other.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write the question and add everyone's name.
  2. On your turn, give one idea or pass.
  3. Keep going until everyone passes in a row.
In depth
  1. State the question, then give 2 to 3 minutes of silent individual writing. People arrive at their turn with ideas ready instead of copying the person before them.
  2. Go around the circle in a fixed order, one idea per person per turn. One idea at a time stops the first speaker from using up the best material.
  3. Have a scribe write each idea word for word on a board and number it. Exact wording prevents the scribe from accidentally filtering ideas.
  4. Allow a person to say 'pass', but come back to them next round. This lowers pressure without letting anyone opt out for good.
  5. Allow no discussion or criticism during the rounds, only quick clarifying questions afterward.
  6. Keep going until a full round passes with everyone saying 'pass'.
  7. Then discuss, merge duplicates, and vote (for example, three votes per person) to pick the ideas to act on.

History

Who created it

No single inventor. Round robin is a turn-taking version of the group brainstorming Alex Osborn began developing in 1939 and popularized in 'Applied Imagination' (1953). Taking turns to share ideas one at a time was formalized in the Nominal Group Technique, published by Andre Delbecq and Andrew Van de Ven in 1971.

What it was created for

It was meant to fix a common brainstorming problem: a few people talk the most while others stay quiet or coast. Fixed turns give everyone equal airtime.

What it is used for

Classrooms, staff meetings, and retrospectives use it to collect input from every person, especially when the group mixes experience levels.

Industries EducationHealthcare and nursing teamsCorporate meetingsAgile software retrospectivesNonprofit and community planningGovernment

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Reverse the order on alternate rounds so the same person is not always first.
  • Put a 20 to 30 second timer on each turn to keep the pace up.
  • With more than 7 people, split into smaller circles and merge the lists at the end.
  • Captains and mentors should go last so their ideas do not anchor everyone else.
Watch out

Passing is allowed. Pressure to always contribute makes ideas worse.

Rambots example (made up)

At the end-of-season debrief, 18 students split into three circles of six to answer 'What should we change about build season meetings?' Each circle keeps going until a full round of passes, and rookies who rarely speak suggest things like posting each day's task list on the shop door. The captains merge the three lists and the whole team votes on the top five changes.

18.7

Morphological Chart

30-45 min · 3-6 · Well documented

List what the robot must do and the options for each, then mix and match.

What it is

An engineering design tool. Rows are functions; cells are ways to do each one. A concept is one option per function. It turns a giant design space into combinations you can compare, and pairs well with a Pugh matrix.

When to use it

Kickoff weekend, when the team is choosing a robot architecture.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. List the functions the robot needs.
  2. Fill several options for each.
  3. Build concepts by clicking Use on one option per row. Random combo can spark odd ideas.
  4. Compare the concepts in a Pugh Matrix.
In depth
  1. List the functions the system must perform in solution-neutral words, such as 'acquire game piece', 'hold it', 'raise it', and 'release it'. Writing 'roller intake' instead of 'acquire' locks in an answer too early.
  2. Keep it to about 4 to 7 functions. Every extra row multiplies the number of combinations.
  3. For each function, fill a row with 3 to 6 different ways to do it, drawn from past robots, other teams' reveal videos, and a few unusual ones. Small sketches in the cells help.
  4. Multiply the number of options in each row to show how many total combinations exist. This reminds the team how much of the design space it has not considered.
  5. Draw paths through the chart, picking one option per row, to build 3 to 5 distinct concepts. Make at least one deliberately unusual to stretch the range.
  6. Check compatibility between pairs of options and cross out combinations that conflict, such as a mechanism that cannot fit with another within the frame perimeter. Zwicky called this cross-consistency assessment.
  7. Compare the surviving concepts with a decision matrix and prototype the top one or two.

History

Who created it

Developed by Fritz Zwicky, a Swiss astrophysicist at Caltech, who advanced his 'morphological approach' from the 1940s until his death in 1974 and applied it to astronomy and to jet and rocket propulsion while also serving as a research director and consultant at Aerojet (1943 to 1961). Engineering design courses later simplified it into the morphological chart, a table of functions and options.

What it was created for

Zwicky wanted a systematic way to explore every possible configuration of a complex system, such as all the possible types of jet engine, instead of relying on whatever ideas people happened to think of first.

What it is used for

Engineers and designers use the chart to generate full concepts by mixing and matching options for each function. Policy and futures analysts use the general version to map scenarios.

Industries Mechanical and aerospace engineeringProduct designRoboticsAutomotiveDefense and policy analysisFutures and scenario planning

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Run the pairwise compatibility check before building concepts. It removes impossible combinations early.
  • Give each concept a theme ('lightest', 'simplest', 'highest scoring') so the paths are truly different.
  • Save the chart after the season. Many rows (drivetrain, intake types, lift types) carry over to the next game.
  • Count options honestly. One row with ten options and the rest with two means the team has only explored one function.
Watch out

Some combinations are physically impossible together. Sanity-check each concept.

Mind Map

15-30 min · 1-6 · Well documented

Branch out from one central idea.

What it is

Start with one idea in the middle and branch out. Each branch can have its own sub-ideas. It shows how ideas connect, which a plain list hides.

When to use it

Planning an event, an award essay, a season, or exploring a topic before narrowing.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write the central idea.
  2. Add main branches, then sub-ideas.
  3. Look for branches that connect, and for empty areas to explore.
In depth
  1. Put the central topic in the middle of a landscape page or whiteboard as a short phrase or small drawing. Starting in the center leaves room to grow in every direction.
  2. Draw 5 to 7 main branches for the major subtopics, each labeled with one or two words. Too many main branches turns the map into a list.
  3. Add sub-branches with single keywords or tiny sketches instead of sentences. Keywords keep the map easy to scan and leave space for more ideas.
  4. Use a different color for each main branch. Color lets people see which ideas belong together at a glance.
  5. Draw arrows across branches where one idea affects another. These cross-links often reveal dependencies that a plain outline hides.
  6. Step back and look for crowded branches (lots of work) and empty ones (gaps nobody has thought about).
  7. Turn the finished map into an outline or task list with owners and dates. A mind map is for thinking, not for tracking progress.

History

Who created it

Tony Buzan, a British author and TV presenter, popularized the term 'mind map' through his 1974 BBC series and book 'Use Your Head'. He did not invent branching diagrams: similar diagrams go back at least to the 3rd-century philosopher Porphyry and the 13th-century writer Ramon Llull, so Buzan's claim to be the inventor is an overstatement.

What it was created for

Buzan promoted it as a note-taking and study method to replace line-by-line notes, using keywords, color, and images branching out from a central topic.

What it is used for

People use mind maps to brainstorm, take notes, plan projects, study, and explain how parts of a topic connect.

Industries EducationProject managementWriting and publishingSoftware and product planningConsultingMarketing

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Draw fast first and tidy later. Redraw once if the map will be shared.
  • For team maps, give each subteam its own branch on a shared online whiteboard so people can work in parallel.
  • Keep a mind map of the robot's subsystems for onboarding new members. It shows how parts connect better than a parts list.
  • If a branch grows past three levels deep, make it the center of its own new map.
Watch out

It can sprawl forever. Set a time limit and pick branches to pursue.

18.9

SCAMPER

20-30 min · 2-6 · Well documented

Seven prompts for improving an existing design.

What it is

Bob Eberle's checklist, built on Alex Osborn's brainstorming questions. Take something that already exists and push it in seven directions. Very good for last year's mechanism or a design that almost works.

When to use it

Improving a mechanism, a process, or an event that already exists.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write what you are improving.
  2. Answer each prompt with at least one idea.
  3. Circle the best ideas and prototype them.
In depth
  1. Pick one existing thing to improve and describe how it works now, with its known problems. SCAMPER works on a baseline, so it is weak for blank-page problems.
  2. Substitute and Combine: ask what part, material, motor, or step could be swapped, and which two parts or functions could be merged into one. Merging parts usually saves weight and failure points.
  3. Adapt: ask where a similar problem has already been solved, such as another team's reveal video, an industrial machine, or a past robot, and how to borrow that idea.
  4. Modify: change size, shape, speed, or ratio, making something bigger, smaller, faster, or slower. Small parameter changes are often the cheapest improvements.
  5. Put to other uses: ask whether the mechanism or its parts could do a second job, or whether leftover parts could solve another problem.
  6. Eliminate: ask what would happen if you removed a part, stage, sensor, or step entirely. In FRC this often gives the biggest gains in weight and reliability.
  7. Reverse or Rearrange: flip the order of steps, the orientation of a mechanism, or the direction of motion. This can expose assumptions nobody questioned.
  8. Collect the ideas from every letter, mark the ones worth testing, and pick one or two to prototype or try at the next practice.

History

Who created it

Created by Bob Eberle, who described it in his 1971 book 'SCAMPER: Games for Imagination Development'. He built it from the idea-spurring checklist in Alex Osborn's 1953 book 'Applied Imagination' and from techniques Frank E. Williams and colleagues developed to encourage creativity in children.

What it was created for

Eberle wrote it as a set of imagination games for developing creativity in children, turning Osborn's longer business checklist into a short, memorable acronym.

What it is used for

Teams use it to improve an existing product, mechanism, or process by running through seven prompts: Substitute, Combine, Adapt (sometimes Adjust), Modify (including magnify or minify), Put to other uses, Eliminate, and Reverse (or Rearrange).

Industries Product designMarketingEducationManufacturingFood and consumer goodsSoftware

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Spend 3 to 5 minutes per letter and move on. Not every letter will produce something useful for every problem.
  • Split the group and give each pair two letters, then rotate, so a large team covers all seven quickly.
  • Use it on processes as well as hardware, such as pit routines, practice schedules, or outreach events.
  • Write the letters in a table with the current design in the first column so ideas stay tied to specific parts.
Watch out

Not every prompt fits every problem. Skip the ones that do not apply.

Starbursting

15-20 min · 2-8 · Thin record

Brainstorm questions, not answers.

What it is

Before solving anything, list every question you can under Who, What, When, Where, Why, and How. Unlike 5W1H, you are not answering yet: you are finding what you do not know.

When to use it

Early in a project, before a plan or a design review.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write the idea in the center.
  2. List questions under each point of the star.
  3. Pick the questions that must be answered first.
In depth
  1. Write the idea or decision as one specific sentence in the center of a six-pointed star, such as 'Switch from tank drive to swerve for next season'. A vague center produces vague questions.
  2. Label the six points Who, What, When, Where, Why, and How. Each point forces a different angle, so the group does not only ask technical questions.
  3. Set a timer for 5 to 10 minutes and have everyone write questions only, one per sticky note, on any point. Banning answers keeps people from defending the idea and stops the loudest person from closing the topic early.
  4. Read the questions aloud point by point and merge duplicates. Questions that many people wrote show where the shared worries are.
  5. Mark each question as 'answered now', 'needs research', or 'blocker'. This turns a pile of questions into a to-do list.
  6. Give every 'needs research' and 'blocker' question an owner and a due date. Unowned questions tend to come back at the worst time, like the week before an event.
  7. Decide whether the idea goes forward, changes, or stops, and write down which blocker questions drove the decision. That record lets next year's leads see why.

History

Who created it

No documented inventor. Starbursting appears in business creativity guides (MindTools, FourWeekMBA, and others) without a named creator. It is built on the six question words Who, What, When, Where, Why, and How, which Rudyard Kipling called his 'six honest serving-men' in a poem from Just So Stories (1902), and which journalists and quality engineers (as 5W1H) have used for a long time.

What it was created for

Stress-testing a new idea before committing to it by generating questions instead of answers. It targets the habit of groups agreeing on a solution before anyone asks what could go wrong.

What it is used for

Project and product kickoffs, evaluating proposals, planning events, and checking a plan for blind spots before money or build time is spent.

Industries Product developmentProject managementMarketingJournalismEducationConsulting

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Push for second-layer questions under each point (Who maintains it? Who fixes it in the pit?), since the first round of questions is usually the obvious stuff.
  • If one point stays empty, spend two extra minutes on it. An empty point often hides the real risk.
  • Invite people outside the subteam, such as outreach or business students, to ask questions about a mechanism. They ask things engineers skip.
  • If you end up with more than about 40 questions, sort them with an affinity diagram before assigning owners.
Watch out

Do not answer questions during the burst. That comes after.

Affinity Diagram

15-30 min · 3-10 · Well documented

Sort a messy pile of ideas into themes.

What it is

Also called the KJ method, after Jiro Kawakita. After a brainstorm, group similar ideas, then name each group. Big groups show where the team's energy is.

When to use it

After brainwriting, a round robin, a debrief, or survey responses.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Add all the ideas.
  2. Move each one into a group. Make new groups as you need them.
  3. Name each group by what its ideas share.
In depth
  1. Put each idea or data point on its own sticky note or card, written as a short phrase that makes sense alone ('intake jams when the game piece enters off-center', not 'intake'). One idea per card is what makes regrouping possible.
  2. Spread all the cards out where everyone can see them at once. Seeing the whole pile prevents early grouping around whichever cards were read first.
  3. Sort in silence: everyone moves cards into clusters that feel related, and anyone can move any card again. Silence keeps senior members from steering the groups and makes the sort much faster.
  4. If a card keeps bouncing between two groups, make a copy and put it in both. The tug-of-war tells you those two themes are connected.
  5. Only after the clusters settle, discuss each group and write a header card that states the theme as a full sentence ('Intake fails when game pieces arrive misaligned'). Writing headers last keeps the categories true to the data instead of to people's assumptions.
  6. Combine related groups into larger supergroups and draw arrows where one theme causes another. This shows which problem is driving the others.
  7. Vote on or rank the headers to choose which themes to act on first, then assign owners. Without this step the diagram is a nice picture with no follow-through.

History

Who created it

Created by Japanese cultural anthropologist Jiro Kawakita (1920-2009), and called the KJ method after his initials (Japanese order, family name first). He developed it during the 1950s and 1960s; ASQ dates it to the 1960s. It later became one of the 'new management and planning tools' used in Japanese and then global quality management.

What it was created for

Making sense of large, messy collections of ethnographic field notes (Kawakita did years of fieldwork in Nepal). He wanted categories to emerge from the data itself instead of forcing the data into the researcher's existing assumptions.

What it is used for

Sorting brainstorm output, survey comments, interview notes, and retrospective feedback into themes so a group can see patterns and choose priorities.

Industries Quality managementUX and design researchSoftware developmentNursing and healthcare researchManufacturingEducation

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Do not start with preset categories like mechanical, electrical, and programming. That defeats the method and hides problems that cross subteams.
  • Aim for groups of about 3 to 10 cards. A group of 20 usually hides two separate themes.
  • Leave odd single cards alone at first instead of forcing them into a group. A lone card is sometimes the most important insight.
  • Photograph the wall before and after writing headers so you can retrace how the themes formed.
Watch out

Do not create the groups first. Let them emerge from the ideas.

Silent sorting produces clusters that become headers like 'Pit battery swaps were slow (about 4 minutes each)', 'Scouting data did not reach the drive coach before matches', and 'Climber failed in 3 of 12 matches'. Practice Bookkeeping

30-45 min · Solo or pairs · Well documented

Learn to keep a ledger, track a budget, and reconcile with a bank statement.

What it is

A practice ledger. Choose a practice month to find planted mistakes and reconcile the book with the bank, or use the blank ledger and budget for exercises you design. All numbers in the practice months are made up.

When to use it

Training the treasurer or business subteam, or anyone curious how team money is tracked.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Pick a practice month from the Mode menu.
  2. Compare the ledger with the bank statement line by line.
  3. Fix mistakes in the ledger, add missing items, and tick lines that appear on the statement.
  4. Get the difference to $0.00, then press Check my work.
In depth
  1. Set up a ledger with one row per transaction: date, description, category (Parts, Registration, Travel, Fundraising, Outreach), amount in or out, payment method, and a receipt link. Consistent categories are what make budget reports possible later.
  2. Record every transaction within a day or two and attach the receipt or invoice. Late entries cause most errors, because memories fade and receipts get lost.
  3. Build a budget by category at the start of the season, using last season's actual spending as a starting point. A budget turns 'can we afford this?' into a quick lookup instead of a debate.
  4. Each month, compare budget versus actual for every category and calculate the difference. A category running 30 percent over in January needs a decision before March, not after the season.
  5. Reconcile monthly: match every ledger line to the bank or school account statement and list items that do not match yet, such as checks not cashed, deposits not cleared, or fees not recorded. The goal is zero unexplained difference, which proves all money is accounted for.
  6. Investigate and fix any unexplained difference right away by adding missing fees or correcting typos. Small mismatches left for months become nearly impossible to trace.
  7. Separate duties: the person who reconciles should not be the person who spends or records. Even on a student team, a mentor reviewing the reconciliation protects everyone from honest mistakes and from suspicion.

History

Who created it

Double-entry bookkeeping had no single inventor. The earliest surviving European double-entry records come from Florentine merchants around 1299 to 1300 (the Farolfi company ledger), and Benedetto Cotrugli described the method in a manuscript written in 1458. Luca Pacioli, a Franciscan friar and mathematician, published the first detailed printed description in his 1494 textbook Summa de arithmetica in Venice, which is why he is called the 'father of accounting' even though he did not invent the system.

What it was created for

Italian merchants needed a way to track goods, debts, and partners' money across many trades and to catch mistakes. Recording every transaction twice (a debit and a credit) means the books must balance, so errors show up.

What it is used for

Businesses, schools, and nonprofits keep ledgers, compare budget against actual spending, and reconcile against bank statements every month to know where money went and to catch errors or theft.

Industries AccountingNonprofitsSmall businessSchools and districtsBankingGovernment

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Practice with a mock month: give students 20 sample transactions and a fake bank statement with 3 planted differences to find.
  • Use separate debit and credit columns even in a simple spreadsheet so students learn double-entry thinking.
  • Track restricted money (for example a grant that can only fund outreach) in its own category so it does not get spent on motors.
  • Keep a reconciliation log with the date, reconciler, reviewer, and ending difference. Grant funders and school business offices may ask for it.
Watch out

Practice numbers only. Do not put real team financial details in a browser that others use.

20.1

Pairwise Comparison

10-20 min · Any · Well documented

Compare every option against every other, one pair at a time.

What it is

Also called paired comparison analysis. Instead of ranking a whole list at once, you only ever choose between two. Every option meets every other option once, and the ranking comes from total wins. It also catches circular preferences, which reveal that the team has not agreed on what matters.

When to use it

Four to ten options where the criteria are hard to write down: fundraiser ideas, event themes, which subsystem to improve first.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write the decision and list the options.
  2. For each pair, pick the better one, or call a tie.
  3. Read the ranking. If a circular preference shows up, stop and agree on criteria.
In depth
  1. List the options and keep the list to about 4 to 8, since 8 options already means 28 pairs. The number of pairs grows quickly, so cut obvious losers first.
  2. Agree on one question for every comparison, such as 'Which gives more points per match for our robot this season?'. Changing the question partway through makes the results meaningless.
  3. Build a grid with the options on both axes and compare each pair once, writing the winner in the cell. The grid makes sure no pair is skipped or judged twice.
  4. Optionally score how strong each preference is (1 = slight, 2 = clear, 3 = strong). Strength scores separate near-ties from runaway winners.
  5. Count wins (or total points) for each option and rank them. The count produces a ranking even when no one could rank all the options in their head.
  6. Look for circles where A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A. A circle means the options are close or the group is mixing two criteria, so clarify the question or split it into separate criteria.
  7. Sanity-check the top result as a group before acting. The method gives a ranking, not a final decision, and a strong second choice may be cheaper or less risky.

History

Who created it

Comparing options two at a time has a long history. The Marquis de Condorcet's 1785 essay on voting used head-to-head majority contests and showed that group preferences can go in a circle (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A), now called the Condorcet paradox. Psychologist L. L. Thurstone turned paired comparison into a measurement method in 'A Law of Comparative Judgment' (Psychological Review, 1927). The paired comparison worksheets used in business are later simplifications with no single author.

What it was created for

Condorcet wanted a fair way to pick a winner from votes; Thurstone wanted to measure things people judge subjectively, like preference, on a real scale. Both rely on the fact that choosing between two options is easier and more reliable than ranking many at once.

What it is used for

Ranking design options, priorities, or candidates by comparing each pair and counting wins; the same idea underlies rating systems like Elo in chess and games.

Industries Psychology and market researchProduct managementEngineering designVoting and electionsSports and game ratingsHuman resources

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Have several people fill in grids independently, then combine them. Cells where people disagree show where the real debate is.
  • Shuffle the order of pairs to reduce fatigue and anchoring on the first few comparisons.
  • For long lists, compare a sample of pairs and use a rating system like Elo instead of judging every pair.
  • Write a one-line reason in each cell; it makes the later discussion much faster.
Watch out

Ten options means 45 matchups. Above about ten, use a bracket first to narrow the list.

Rambots example (made up)

The strategy team compares five ways to score in the season game: high goal shooter, low goal, climb, ground intake, and defense, judging 10 pairs on 'expected points per match at our skill level'. Climb wins 4, while high goal, low goal, and ground intake tie at 2 each and defense gets 0. The tie comes from a circle among those three, which shows the team was mixing 'points' with 'reliability', so they redo those pairs with each criterion judged separately.

Bracket with Second Chance

10-20 min · Any · Partly documented

A single-elimination bracket where first-round losers get one more shot.

What it is

Options face off two at a time and winners advance. Losers of round 1 play each other in a second-chance round, and its winners rejoin the main bracket, so a strong option is not lost to an unlucky early draw. Turn on vote counts to decide each matchup by show of hands.

When to use it

Subjective picks with many options: shirt designs, robot names, team bonding activities, pit themes.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. List the options, one per line.
  2. Start the bracket. Pick a winner for each matchup, or enter vote counts.
  3. Round 1 losers get a second chance before the next main round.
  4. Read the final standings. Options knocked out in the same round share a place.
In depth
  1. Seed the options so the strongest-looking ones do not meet in round one (1 vs 8, 2 vs 7, and so on). Seeding keeps the bracket from wasting a strong option early.
  2. Run the main single-elimination bracket with one clear question for every matchup. Each matchup is a pairwise choice, so the question must stay the same throughout.
  3. Send first-round losers, or only the close losers, into a repechage pool. This second chance catches options that went out because of a bad draw.
  4. Run the repechage as its own mini-bracket and send its winner back into the main bracket at a point agreed in advance, such as the semifinals. Fixing the re-entry point before starting stops anyone from bending the rules to rescue a favorite.
  5. Finish the bracket and record every matchup result with a one-line reason. The record explains the final pick to people who were not in the room.
  6. Review the finalists and the repechage winner together. If the repechage winner goes deep, that is a sign the first-round seeding or criteria were off.

History

Who created it

Single-elimination brackets and repechage both developed in sport rather than from one inventor. Repechage (French for 'fishing out' or rescuing) gives some eliminated competitors a second route forward; Olympic rowing used repechage rounds at the 1924 Paris Games, and wrestling, judo, and taekwondo use repechage brackets to award bronze medals to athletes who lost to the finalists. Track cycling uses repechage heats, and fencing once used it widely.

What it was created for

In rowing, it lowers the chance that a strong crew is knocked out by one bad race or bad conditions like wind. In combat sports, it gives athletes who lost only to the eventual finalists a fair shot at third place.

What it is used for

Tournaments and selection rounds where a single loss should not end a strong contender's chances, including idea selection where a good option might lose one unlucky early matchup.

Industries RowingWrestling and judoTrack cyclingEsports and gamingContest and event designIdea and product selection

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • When time is short, send only close losers to repechage; an option that lost by a landslide rarely deserves a second chance.
  • Use byes when the number of options is not a power of two (4, 8, 16).
  • Do not confuse repechage with double elimination, where every competitor gets a second life; repechage usually rescues only some losers.
  • Keep matchups to 1 or 2 minutes each so energy stays high.
Watch out

The draw matters, and byes can carry an option far. Use it for subjective picks, not engineering tradeoffs.

Rambots example (made up)

The team has 8 robot name ideas for the season. In round one, 'Ram-page' loses 9 to 11 to 'Bolt' in a show of hands, so it goes into a repechage with the three other first-round losers. 'Ram-page' wins the repechage, re-enters at the semifinals, beats 'Bolt' in a rematch, and wins the final 14 to 9.

King of the Hill

5-10 min · Any · Partly documented

The champion takes on challengers one at a time. Winner stays on.

What it is

Start with one option as champion. Each other option challenges it once, and the winner stays on the hill. It is quick and fun, but order matters: an early option has to win many times, while a late one only needs to win once.

When to use it

Icebreakers, low-stakes picks, or narrowing a short list quickly at a meeting.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. List the options. Leave Shuffle on to make the order fair-ish.
  2. Pick the winner of each challenge.
  3. For anything that matters, check the result with Pairwise Comparison.
In depth
  1. List all the options and shuffle their order (draw cards or use a random number generator). Randomizing matters because the order of challengers changes the result.
  2. Make the first option the current champion. It has no advantage except going first.
  3. Bring in the next option and compare it head-to-head with the champion using one agreed question. The winner becomes or stays champion.
  4. Continue down the list until every option has challenged once. With n options this takes only n minus 1 comparisons, which is why it is fast.
  5. Record each result and the reason. A champion that barely beat several challengers is less certain than one that won easily.
  6. Check for order bias: a strong option that appears early can be knocked out by a lucky upset, while a late option may only face a tired or weak champion. Rerun with a different order, or give the runner-up one rematch, to see whether the winner holds.
  7. Treat the result as the shortlist leader, not the final answer, when the decision is expensive. This format trades accuracy for speed.

History

Who created it

No single inventor. The name comes from the children's game where one player holds a hill against challengers, and 'winner stays on' rules are common in pickup basketball, arcade games, and racket sports ('king of the court'). Researchers call the formal version a 'challenge the champ' or stepladder tournament and have shown that the order in which challengers appear strongly affects who wins.

What it was created for

Playground and pickup games needed a simple way to keep play moving when there were more players than spots, with the current winner defending against the next person in line.

What it is used for

Quickly picking a favorite from a list by comparing one new option at a time against the current best; also stepladder finals in bowling and challenge formats in other sports and games.

Industries Youth games and campsBowling and racket sportsVideo games and esportsDesign selectionTeam decision-makingClassroom activities

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Use it when there are many options and little time; switch to a full pairwise comparison for high-stakes choices.
  • Run it twice with the order reversed. If the same winner comes out both times, confidence goes up.
  • In live games, cap how many rounds one champion can hold so everyone gets turns.
  • Explain order bias to the group up front so people trust the process.
Watch out

Order bias is real: early options face more challenges. Shuffle, and check important results with Pairwise Comparison.

Because sketch 6 only faced challengers that had already been weakened, the team reruns in reverse order; sketch 6 wins again, so they build it.

Tier List

10-20 min · Any · Partly documented

Sort a set of options into tiers from S (best) to F (worst).

What it is

A tier list sorts many items into ranked bands instead of a strict 1-to-N order. S tier is the best, then A, B, C, D, and F is the worst. It is fast, it gets everyone talking about why something belongs where it is, and it shows clumps of similar quality that a numbered ranking hides. You can rename the tiers, add or remove them, or switch to Ship it to Scrap for build reviews.

When to use it

Sorting a long list quickly by taste or gut feel: offseason project ideas, shirt designs, scouting app features, snacks for build season. Use it to narrow the field, then use a Decision Matrix or Pairwise Comparison on the top tier if the result decides something real.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Write what "best" means for this list.
  2. Add the items, one per line.
  3. Drag each item into a tier, or tap the item and then tap a tier.
  4. Write one line on why the top tier items are there.
In depth
  1. Agree on the criterion before anyone moves a single item: write "best for X" at the top of the board (for example, "best offseason project for teaching new members"), because most tier list fights are really people ranking against different criteria.
  2. Define what each tier means in plain words before ranking (S: do this for sure, A: strong yes, B: fine if we have time, C: probably not, D: no), so that "A" means the same thing to everyone in the room.
  3. Keep 5 to 7 tiers: fewer than 5 gives you no useful separation, and more than 7 turns the session into arguing over tiny differences.
  4. Have one person (the scribe) physically move the items while the group argues and calls out placements; nobody else touches the board, which keeps things orderly and makes the quiet students' suggestions count as much as the loud ones.
  5. Limit the top tier (for example, no more than 2 or 3 items in S); if everything is S, nothing is, and the cap forces the group to make real tradeoffs.
  6. Record a one-line reason for every item that ends up in the top tier and the bottom tier, so later readers (and next year's team) know why, not just where.
  7. Treat the tier list as a first pass: when the result decides something real (money, build time, which project gets staffed), follow up on the top few items with a Decision Matrix or Pairwise Comparison before committing.

History

Who created it

Tier lists grew out of competitive fighting game communities, which were ranking characters by tournament viability in the years after Street Fighter II (1991); Japanese arcade magazines such as Gamest are often credited with early character rankings, but that link is not well documented. One of the earliest documented posts in the modern format is a Tekken ranking with labels like "God Tier" and "Mid Tier" shared on the Tekken Zaibatsu forum in March 2005. The "S" band above "A" is generally traced to Japanese grading and advertising, where an S grade marks something above A; the S is often explained as "special," "super," or the character shu (exemplary). The format spread beyond gaming on online forums around 2009 and 2010. It went mainstream in the late 2010s as YouTube and Twitch creators made tier list videos, and drag-and-drop sites such as TierMaker made it easy for anyone to rank anything.

What it was created for

Tier lists were created so competitive players could sort a large roster of characters into rough strength bands, without arguing over an exact 1-to-N order. That way people could talk about balance, picks, and matchups.

What it is used for

Today they are used to rank games, media, food, and pop culture, and as a quick group triage tool for sorting product features, project ideas, retro topics, or design options into bands. Teachers also use them to get students to rank items and defend their choices.

Industries Video games and esportsMedia and entertainmentProduct managementEducationMarketing

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Use a shared digital board (TierMaker, a slide, or a whiteboard with sticky notes) so the result can be photographed or saved right away.
  • Start each item in an unranked pool and place the easy, obvious ones first to build momentum before the hard debates.
  • Time-box debate on any single item (about one minute); if the group cannot agree, park it in the middle tier and flag it for later.
  • Remind everyone that a tier list ranks items against one criterion, not people, so nobody's idea is being called bad, just less suited to this goal.
Watch out

A tier list records taste, not reasons. Agree on what best means before sorting, and if the result decides something real, write one line on why the top tier is there or follow up with a Decision Matrix.

Coin Flip Gut Check

1 min · Solo · Partly documented

Flip a coin, then notice whether you are relieved or disappointed.

What it is

A coin flip will not make a decision for you, but it tells you what you already want. The moment it lands, you feel relief or a small pang of disappointment. That feeling is the answer.

When to use it

Two options that are close, low-stakes choices, or when you are overthinking.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Type the two options.
  2. Flip.
  3. Answer honestly: relieved, disappointed, or nothing.
In depth
  1. Reduce the choice to exactly two options and assign them to heads and tails out loud. The method only works if both outcomes are real and clearly defined.
  2. Agree beforehand that the coin does not decide; your reaction does. This keeps people from feeling trapped by a random result.
  3. Flip and pay attention in the first second or two after you see the result. Relief, disappointment, or the urge to say 'best two out of three' is the signal.
  4. Name the reaction in one word and write it down. Feelings fade fast and people rationalize them away.
  5. If you felt disappointed, ask what you were hoping for and why. That 'why' is often the real decision criterion you had not said.
  6. If you felt nothing, treat that as useful too. It usually means both options are close in value, so pick quickly and move on.
  7. Use it only for decisions where both options are acceptable. Do not use it for safety, money that is not yours to risk, or choices that need data.

History

Who created it

The idea is usually traced to the Danish poet and scientist Piet Hein (1905 to 1996) and his short poem 'A Psychological Tip,' one of his 'grooks,' which says that while the coin is in the air 'you suddenly know what you're hoping.' The exact first publication date of the poem is not well documented. Research has since tested related ideas: economist Steven Levitt's 2016 NBER study and a 2019 PLoS ONE study by Jaffe, Reutner, and Greifeneder.

What it was created for

Hein's poem points to a common problem: people stuck between two options often already have a preference they have not admitted. The coin is a tool to surface it, not to make the choice.

What it is used for

People use it to break a tie when they cannot decide, by noticing whether they feel relief or disappointment at the result. Leaders use it to help a group see where its gut feeling already is.

Industries Personal decision-makingCoachingPsychology educationTeam facilitationBehavioral economics

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Levitt's study of about 20,000 coin flips (2016) found people who got heads were about 25 percent more likely to make the change, and those who made a big change (like quitting a job) reported being happier six months later. This suggests many people are biased toward staying put.
  • The 2019 PLoS ONE study found that a random device made people's feelings about the options stronger, and they then decided based on those feelings rather than just obeying the coin.
  • For groups, have everyone silently note their reaction before anyone speaks, then share. This avoids people copying the loudest reaction.
  • Wanting 'best of three' is itself the answer: you wanted the other side.
Watch out

Only for low stakes. Never for safety or money decisions.

The driver coach flips a coin for the 3-piece, and the drive team groans because they remember it missed twice on the practice field, so they run the 2-piece and plan more 3-piece testing at lunch.

Fortune Orb

1 min · Any · Partly documented

Ask a yes or no question, shake, and notice your reaction.

What it is

A glowing orb with 20 short classic-style answers: ten lean yes, five are noncommittal, and five lean no. It is a toy, but your reaction to its answer is real information.

When to use it

Breaking the ice at a meeting, or checking your gut on a small choice.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Type a yes or no question.
  2. Shake the orb.
  3. Notice whether you hoped for a different answer.
In depth
  1. Phrase the question as a yes or no question about one choice ('Should we keep the current bumper design?'). A vague or two-part question makes any answer meaningless.
  2. Say before asking that the orb does not decide anything. This keeps it a game and stops anyone from using it to end a real argument unfairly.
  3. Ask and watch your first reaction to the answer, before you think about it. Relief or a sinking feeling shows what you were hoping for.
  4. If the answer is 'maybe' or unclear, notice whether you wanted it to say yes or no. A vague answer often reveals your preference more clearly than a direct one.
  5. Say your reaction out loud in one sentence ('I wanted it to say no because the bumpers are fine'). Putting it into words turns a feeling into a reason the group can discuss.
  6. Then decide with normal judgment, using the reaction as one input. If the decision has real cost or safety risk, use a proper framework instead.

History

Who created it

Chance-based answer tools are very old, with no single inventor. Lot oracles such as the Sortes Astrampsychi (Greek, 2nd to 3rd century CE) matched a randomly picked number to a list of ready-made answers, and fortune-telling games, cards, paper folding toys, and coin-operated arcade machines were popular in the 19th and 20th centuries. The Fortune Orb in this toolkit is a modern take on that tradition, giving short yes, maybe, or no style answers.

What it was created for

Answer toys were made for entertainment and to give people a playful way to ask about things they were anxious or curious about, such as love, money, and the future.

What it is used for

Today the toolkit uses it as a light, fast way to break a tie or loosen up a stuck discussion. The real value is noticing your reaction to the random answer, the same idea as the coin flip gut check.

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Use it for low-stakes, two-way-door choices only, like team T-shirt color or which practice drill to run first.

  • If you keep asking again until you get the answer you want, stop and go with that answer: you already know your preference.
  • It works well as an icebreaker that teaches the gut-check idea before using it on real decisions.
Watch out

It is a toy. Use it as an icebreaker, not as an answer.

21.4

Decision Wheel

1 min · Any · Partly documented

Spin a wheel of answers: classic, FRC, or your own options.

What it is

A spinning wheel like the ones on toy decision boards. Pick the classic wheel (yes, no, maybe, ask mom), the FRC wheel (ask a mentor, ship it, check the game manual), or type your own options to spin between real choices. As with the coin, your reaction to where it lands is the useful part.

When to use it

Choosing between several low-stakes options, picking who goes first, or breaking a tie at a meeting.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Choose a wheel, or type your own options.
  2. Spin.
  3. Notice whether you hoped it would land somewhere else.
In depth
  1. Put only options the group already accepts on the wheel. A random pick is fair only when every slice is good enough.
  2. Agree before spinning whether the result is binding or just a gut check. Changing the rules after the spin destroys trust in the process.
  3. Make the slices equal unless you deliberately weight them, and say so out loud if you do. Hidden weights make the wheel feel rigged.
  4. Spin where everyone can see it, with a physical wheel or a projected online spinner. Visibility is what makes a random choice feel fair.
  5. Watch how people react when the wheel stops. A 2020 study by Jaffe, Douneva, and Greifeneder found that coin flips often work as a catalyst: the relief or disappointment people feel at the result shows what they actually wanted.
  6. If the reaction reveals a strong hidden preference and you agreed the spin was a gut check, pause and discuss it. Surfacing that preference is often more valuable than the pick itself.
  7. Record what was spun for and the result so repeated spins, like chore rotations, stay fair over time.

History

Who created it

The wheel as a symbol of chance is ancient. The Rota Fortunae (wheel of fortune), spun by the goddess Fortuna, was popularized in medieval Europe largely through Boethius's Consolation of Philosophy (around 520 AD). Spinning prize wheels, such as the Big Six wheel at carnivals and casinos, came much later with no single inventor, and online spinner tools are a modern version.

What it was created for

The medieval wheel was a reminder that luck is unpredictable and fortunes rise and fall. Carnival and prize wheels were built to make random outcomes fun and visible to a crowd.

What it is used for

Breaking ties, assigning chores or turns, choosing among equally good options, and adding a game element to classrooms and meetings.

Industries EducationEvents and entertainmentCasinos and gamingMarketing promotionsMeeting facilitation

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • For rotating chores, remove a slice once it is picked so the same person is not chosen three times in a row.
  • Do not use a wheel for safety, budget, or people decisions with real consequences. The same study found people are less willing to use random aids when others are affected, and for good reason.
  • Use it to break analysis paralysis when two options score within a point of each other.
  • A quick spin works well as an icebreaker or to pick who shares first in a long meeting.
Watch out

A wheel of real options is still random. Use it only when every option on it is acceptable.

Rambots example (made up)

At the end of a leads meeting, the team has two equally rated pit display designs and 10 minutes left. They agree the spin is a gut check, spin, and land on the blue banner design. Several leads groan, so the captain asks why; they realize the red design fits the 10-by-10-foot pit better, and the group picks red with a quick vote.

21.5

Dice Roller

1 min · Any · Well documented

Roll 1d6, 2d6, d20, or any dice you like, or roll between options.

What it is

A dice roller for tabletop games, probability practice, and quick choices. Pick a quick roll like 2d6 or d20, or build your own such as 3d8+2 or a d7. A single d20 can roll with D&D advantage or disadvantage. Every roll shows the odds of what you got. In Between options mode, each option gets a face, and as with the coin, your reaction to the result is the useful part.

When to use it

Game nights, teaching probability (why 7 is the most common 2d6 total), breaking ties between equally good options, or picking a random order.

How to use it

Quick version
  1. Choose Numbers or Between options.
  2. Pick a quick roll, or set how many dice, the die, and a modifier. For options, list them one per line.
  3. Roll. For options, notice whether you hoped for a different result.
In depth
  1. Assign options to faces before anyone rolls: write the list on the whiteboard (for example, d6 face 1 to 2 = drill A, 3 to 4 = drill B, 5 to 6 = drill C) so the mapping is fixed and no one can reinterpret the result afterward.
  2. Agree on reroll rules first: decide in advance what happens on an unassigned face, a die that rolls off the table, or a tie, and whether anyone gets a veto, so the roll stays fair and fast.
  3. Use the gut-check reaction, just like a coin flip: if the roll lands and the group groans or cheers, that reaction tells you which option they actually preferred, and you are allowed to go with the preference instead of the die.
  4. Teach why sums are not flat: with 2d6 there are 36 equally likely combinations and 6 of them make 7 (about 16.7 percent), while 2 and 12 each have only 1 (about 2.8 percent), so the bell shape comes from counting combinations, unlike a single d20 where every face is a flat 5 percent.
  5. Show advantage and disadvantage from D&D 5th edition: roll two d20 and keep the higher (advantage) or the lower (disadvantage); students can count all 400 pairs and see the average rise from 10.5 to about 13.8 with advantage and fall to about 7.2 with disadvantage, a hands-on lesson in how small rule changes reshape a distribution.
  6. Simulate match variance and practice expected value: model an uncertain scoring action (for example, a d6 where 1 to 2 means a missed shot) and roll dozens of fake matches, then compare the average of the rolls to the calculated expected value and talk about why a single match can look very different from the average.
  7. Never use dice for safety, money, or people decisions: anything involving robot safety, power tools, spending team funds, or who gets cut or picked for a role must be decided on merit and by the responsible adult or lead, not left to chance.

History

Who created it

Dice are thought to have grown out of casting astragali (knucklebones, the ankle bones of hoofed animals) for fortune-telling and games. Among the oldest known dice are a game set from Shahr-i Sokhta (the Burnt City) in southeastern Iran, estimated at about 2800 to 2500 BCE, bone dice from Skara Brae in Scotland dated to about 3100 to 2400 BCE, and terracotta cubic dice from Mohenjo-daro in the Indus Valley (about 2500 to 1900 BCE), at least one with opposite faces summing to seven like modern dice. Twenty-sided dice are also ancient: the Metropolitan Museum holds a serpentinite icosahedron inscribed with Greek letters from Egypt, dated about the 2nd century BCE to the 4th century CE (Ptolemaic to Roman period). Dungeons and Dragons, created by Gary Gygax and Dave Arneson and first published by TSR in 1974, is widely credited with popularizing polyhedral dice; the first boxed set shipped without dice, and by most accounts the designers adopted a five-die set (d4, d6, d8, d12, d20) sold as classroom math dice, using a d20 numbered 0 to 9 twice for percentile rolls. The dedicated d10 came later (commonly credited to Gamescience around 1980), and today's standard RPG set has seven dice: d4, d6, d8, d12, d20, and two d10s (one marked in tens) for percentile.

What it was created for

Dice began as tools for divination and chance-based games, letting people hand an outcome to something no one could control or be blamed for. Polyhedral sets were later adopted (and popularized by tabletop role-playing games) to produce different ranges of random numbers for combat, damage, and other game outcomes.

What it is used for

Board games, tabletop role-playing games, and casino games use dice to generate fair random outcomes, while teachers use them to make probability, distributions, and expected value concrete. Teams and individuals also use physical or digital dice rollers for quick random selection among equal options and for simple simulations of uncertain events.

Industries Tabletop and board gamesEducation (math and probability)Casinos and gamingGame designStatistics and simulation

Tips and pitfalls

Tips
  • Only roll between options the group already agrees are all acceptable; dice break ties, they do not replace judgment.
  • Say the face mapping out loud and write it down before the roll so the result is binding and transparent.
  • Use a digital roller or a large set of physical dice when running probability lessons so you can collect enough rolls to see the distribution emerge.
  • Pick the die that matches the number of options (d4 for four, d6 for six, d8 for eight) to avoid awkward reroll rules.
Watch out

Dice are random. Agree on what each result means and on any reroll rule before rolling, and never roll for safety or money.

Glossary

Terms used across the toolkit and this sheet, in the order a reader is likely to meet them.

TermMeaning
FrameworkA repeatable structure for thinking through a type of problem. It does not supply answers; it decides what questions get asked and in what order.
FacilitatorThe person who runs the framework: keeps time, enforces the rules (one hat at a time, silent first), and records results. The facilitator should not dominate the content.
Diverge / convergeDiverging generates many options; converging narrows them. Most failed meetings try to do both at once.
CriterionA dimension you judge options on, such as weight, cost, or reliability. Good criteria are independent of each other and measurable or at least clearly described.
WeightHow much a criterion counts relative to the others. A weight of 3 counts three times as much as a weight of 1.
Heat mapA grid colored by value, so high-risk or high-value areas stand out at a glance.
Baseline (datum)In a Pugh Matrix, the reference concept every other concept is compared against with +, 0, or minus.
Root causeThe deepest cause the team can actually change. Fixing it stops the problem from returning; fixing a symptom does not.
CountermeasureThe action that removes or blocks a root cause.
Failure modeA specific way a part or function can fail, such as a connector loosening or an encoder losing its offset.
Severity, Occurrence, DetectionThe three FMEA ratings. Detection is reversed: a high number means the failure would be hard to notice before it causes harm.
RPNRisk Priority Number in FMEA: Severity times Occurrence times Detection. Used to rank failure modes, not as an exact measurement.
Vital fewIn a Pareto chart, the small number of causes responsible for most of the problems, usually about 80 percent.
Factor and levelIn Design of Experiments, a factor is something you change (hood angle) and a level is a setting of it (30 or 40 degrees).
Main effectThe average change in the result when a factor goes from its low level to its high level.
Expected valueThe average payoff of an option: each outcome's value times its chance, added up.
Two-way doorA decision that can be reversed cheaply. Decide fast. A one-way door is hard to undo and deserves care.
WIP limitWork-in-progress limit on a Kanban board: the maximum number of cards allowed in Doing at once.
Definition of doneThe agreed conditions a task must meet before it counts as finished.
Responsible / AccountableIn RACI, Responsible people do the work; the one Accountable person owns the outcome.
Key resultIn OKRs, a measurable outcome that shows an objective was reached. A result, not a task.
BlamelessA review style that asks what in the system allowed a mistake, not who made it.
TimeboxA fixed amount of time for an activity. When it runs out, the group decides with what it has.
RepechageA second-chance round in a bracket where first-round losers compete to rejoin the main bracket.
ByeAn automatic advance to the next round when a bracket round has an odd number of entrants.
Circular preferenceA beats B, B beats C, and C beats A. It signals that people are judging by different criteria each time.
ReconciliationComparing the team's ledger with the bank statement and explaining every difference until they match.

Common facilitation mistakes

The ten mistakes leads make most often with frameworks, and what to do instead. Most are about process, not the tools themselves.

Using two frameworks in one meeting
The group spends its energy learning tools instead of solving the problem. One tool per problem; save the second for next meeting.
Generating and judging at the same time
Criticizing ideas during a brainstorm stops people from sharing. Separate the diverge and converge steps, ideally with a break between.
Skipping the example
A team seeing a tool for the first time wastes ten minutes on mechanics. Load the example, walk through it for two minutes, then start blank.
No owners at the end
Debriefs, postmortems, and retros that end without names and dates change nothing. The last five minutes are for owners.
Trusting the math over the discussion
A Decision Matrix total can hide a fatal flaw. If the winner surprises everyone, re-examine weights and scores before committing.
Blame in a postmortem
One blaming comment ends honest reporting for the season. The facilitator stops it immediately and redirects to the process.
Using preference tools for engineering
A bracket can pick a shirt, not a drivetrain. Engineering choices need tools that record the reasoning so it can be challenged.
Letting the facilitator decide
The facilitator runs the process. When the facilitator also argues the content, others defer and the tool becomes theater.
Not saving the file
The toolkit autosaves only in one browser. Save the worksheet file so it can be reopened next meeting or shared with a mentor.
Forgetting to revisit
Risk maps, OKRs, stakeholder maps, and 5S audits go stale. Put a revisit date on the calendar before closing the worksheet.
Safety decisions
Never use a preference tool, a coin, an orb, or a wheel for anything involving safety, injuries, or significant money. Use the Incident Report, Three Tiers of Prevention, Risk Matrix, or Decision Matrix, and involve a mentor.
