Part 1 · Soldering

The iron lives in its holder, and you never grab a falling one

Holder

Every time you let go of the iron, even for a few seconds, it goes back in its holder. The tip runs about 600 to 700°F (315 to 370°C), hot enough for a deep burn in under a second.

Falling iron

If it starts to fall, let it go and step back. Most burns come from grabbing for a falling iron. When you walk away, turn it off and leave it in the holder; it stays hot for minutes.

Every time

Safety glasses, since solder spits and clipped ends fly. Fumes cleared by the extractor or good airflow. A surface that won't burn. No food or drink at the bench, and wash your hands after.

Part 1 · Soldering

Heat the wires, let the wires melt the solder, and hold still

Method

Heat the wires with the tip, then feed into the wires, not the tip. Solder only bonds to metal hot enough to melt it. Don't blow on it or move it while it cools.

Hotter isn't better

Too hot burns off the flux before it cleans the joint, oxidizes the tip, melts insulation, and can lift pads off a board. Use your solder's range: about 315 to 370°C for leaded, 340 to 400°C for lead-free.

Read the joint

Good: smooth and shiny, with the strands' shape showing through. Cold: dull, grainy, lumpy. Blob: a ball sitting on top that never wetted the wires. A stray strand pokes through or shorts.

Part 1 · Heat shrink

Slide the tube on first, size it to the splice, and use the heat gun

First

Slide the onto one wire before you twist, , or , and keep it well back from the heat. After the splice is made, the tube can't get over it. This is the most common beginner mistake.

Sizing

A tube's size is its inside width before shrinking. It has to slide over the widest part, then shrink to its size divided by the ratio (3:1 is one third) and still grip the wire's insulation. Pick one 20 to 30% bigger than the widest part.

Worked example

Widest part 0.40 in., insulation 0.15 in. A 1/2 in. (0.50) tube slides over. At 3:1 it shrinks to 0.17, too loose on 0.15. At 4:1 it shrinks to 0.125, so it grips. Adhesive-lined tube also seals the splice.

Heat gun

Never a lighter: a flame scorches one spot and is a fire hazard near wiring and batteries. Move the gun evenly from the middle out. It stays hot after it's off, so stand it on its rest, pointed away.

Use the stripper notch that matches the wire's gauge

Right notch

Strip 14 wire in the 14 notch. A notch that's too small nicks or cuts strands, and every lost strand is less copper and a spot that breaks under vibration.

Too big

A notch that's too big tears the insulation instead of cutting it clean. Flush cutters nick strands, so don't score insulation with them.

Part 1 · Crimping

Crimp with the matching die, then pull-test every single crimp

The tool

The ratcheting , in the die that matches the terminal's color. It squeezes barrel and strands into one solid piece and won't let go until the crimp is complete. Pliers just crush it. Red is 22 to 18 , blue 16 to 14, yellow 12 to 10.

Pull test

Hold the terminal and pull hard by hand. Never yank it with pliers until it breaks; that wrecks a good crimp. If anything moves, cut it off and crimp a new one, never re-crimp. Look too: every strand in the barrel, insulation up to the barrel.

Right terminal

It has to fit the wire and the stud. A ring that's too big touches less area and shifts with vibration. Forks slide off when the nut loosens. Drilling out a ring removes the metal that carries current.

Part 1 · Hydraulic crimper

Match the die and lug to the wire, keep your hands clear, and finish the crimp

Setup

Use the die marked for the wire size and a lug made for that wire size. Strip to the lug maker's length, about the depth of the barrel, and push the wire all the way in.

Hands

Both hands on the handles, fingers away from the head. The head closes with tons of force. Never hold the lug in the head with your fingers.

Finish

Keep pumping until the relief valve pops; no pop means the isn't finished. Then release the pressure before you take the lug out. Never pump it empty. An incomplete battery-cable crimp can start a fire.

Part 1 · Multimeter

Volts across a battery is safe; amps across a battery is a short

Battery voltage

Dial on DC volts. Black lead in COM, red lead in V. Red probe on +, black on -. A voltmeter draws almost no current, so it's always safe across a battery.

The danger

On amps, the meter is nearly a dead short. Probes across a battery can pull hundreds of amps, blow the meter's fuse, melt the leads, or arc. Ohms on a live battery reads nonsense and can damage the meter. AC volts reads nothing useful.

Continuity

and ohms push the meter's own tiny current through the wire. Disconnect the battery first; with power on, the reading means nothing and the meter can be damaged.

Part 1 · Multimeter

Follow the voltage to find a break, then check the frame is isolated

Finding an open

With the robot on and the black probe on battery negative, follow the circuit on DC volts. Where you see voltage on one side of a connector and none on the other, the break is between those two points. Then battery off, and across that spot.

Usually mechanical

Most electrical problems are a loose connector or a bad , not a dead part.

Frame isolation

The frame must never carry current. With the battery out, set the meter on ohms and go from either post of the 's battery plug to bare frame. The rules require more than 120 ohms. A low reading means a wire or device touches the frame, often a chafed wire.

Part 1 · Static

Static you can't feel can kill a chip, so discharge first and keep boards in their bags

Before

Touch bare, unpainted metal to discharge yourself before handling a board. Walking around, especially on carpet in dry weather, builds up more charge.

Handling

Leave the board in its silver anti-static bag until you're ready to install it. Hold it by its edges, not the pins or chips.

Setting it down

A bare board goes back in its bag, never onto the robot's metal frame. The damage from a zap may not show until later.

Part 1 · Heat-set inserts

Set the iron a little above the print temperature and let heat do the work

Temperature

10 to 20°C above the plastic's print temperature: about 225°C for , 245°C for , and 265°C for . Too hot breaks down the plastic around the insert. Too cold and you end up forcing it.

Pressing

Light pressure, iron straight up and down. Melt it in about 90% of the way, then press it flush with a flat tool and hold a few seconds while the plastic sets, or it creeps back out.

After

Forcing it tilts the insert and pushes melted plastic into the threads. The insert and tip stay hot enough to burn, so don't touch it to check. The iron goes straight back in its holder.

Part 2 · Ohm's law

Three letters run most of the math: V = I × R, and P = V × I

Ohm's law

V = I × R. Volts (V) equal amps (I) times ohms (R). Rearrange it: I = V ÷ R, and R = V ÷ I.

Power

P = V × I. Watts equal volts times amps. Rearrange it: I = P ÷ V.

Worked examples

A light draws 10 A at 12 V: P = 12 × 10 = 120 W. A 6 ohm heater across 12 V: I = 12 ÷ 6 = 2 A. A 12 V fan rated 36 W: I = 36 ÷ 12 = 3 A.

Part 2 · Heat and shorts

Heat grows with the square of current, and only the battery limits a short

Heat in a wire

P = I² × R. Same wire, twice the current: 2² = 4 times the heat. Three times the current: 3² = 9 times. That's why high-current circuits need much thicker wire.

Short circuit

I = V ÷ (battery's internal resistance + the short's resistance). Example: 12.0 V, a 0.025 ohm battery, a 0.005 ohm screwdriver: 12.0 ÷ 0.030 = 400 A.

Why it matters

12 V can't shock you through dry skin, but hundreds of amps can weld a tool, burn you, and crack a battery. Only the battery's own resistance holds it back.

Part 2 · Resistors

Series resistors add; parallel resistors always total less than the smallest

Series

Resistances add. 47 + 68 + 100 ohms = 215 ohms.

Parallel

Equal resistors: one value ÷ how many (two 10s give 5). Two different ones: product over sum. 10 and 15: 150 ÷ 25 = 6 ohms. Every resistor added in parallel is another path, so the total drops, always below the smallest.

CAN bus

The robot's CAN data wiring (yellow CAN high, green CAN low) has a 120 ohm terminator at each end. With the robot off, across the two wires they read as two equal resistors in parallel: half of 120.

Part 2 · Cells

Series adds voltage; parallel adds capacity

The rule

Series: voltages add, capacity (Ah) stays the same. Parallel: capacity adds, voltage stays the same. The battery is six 2 V lead-acid cells in series: 12 V.

Pack labels

5S2P means 5 in series, 2 in parallel. Cells: 5 × 2 = 10. Voltage: 5 × cell volts. Capacity: 2 × cell Ah. Example: 4S3P of 3.6 V, 2 Ah cells is 12 cells, 14.4 V, 6 Ah.

Part 2 · Chemistry

Know the nominal cell voltages, and read the labels like an engineer

Cell voltages

Nominal (average working) voltage per cell: lead acid 2.0 V, lithium-ion 3.6 V, NiMH 1.2 V. 4.2 V is a lithium-ion cell's peak right off the charger, not its nominal. 12 V and 18 V are whole packs.

Pack labels

Five lithium-ion cells in series are 5 × 3.6 = 18 V nominal. Some brands print the voltage right off the charger instead: 5 × 4.2 = 21 V, sold as 20V MAX. Same cells, same chemistry: marketing. Our Ryobi 18V packs use the nominal way.

Cell sizes

Round cells are named by size: 18650 is 18 mm across and 65 mm long, and the 0 means cylindrical. 21700 is 21 mm by 70 mm: bigger cells, fewer per pack.

Part 2 · Lithium-ion

A damaged lithium-ion cell can heat itself into a fire that feeds on itself

Thermal runaway

Lithium-ion packs a lot of energy into a small cell with a flammable electrolyte. Damage or an internal short heats one spot, the heat breaks down the separator and electrolyte, that makes more heat, and it spreads cell to cell. A failing cell reaches about 500°C (932°F) and vents with flame. Unplugging may not stop it.

Lead acid

Water-based electrolyte and far less energy per pound, so it basically can't run away. Its hazards are acid and hydrogen gas. Lithium-ion packs need a battery management system (BMS); lead acid doesn't.

Swelling

A puffy pack has gas building up inside as the electrolyte breaks down. It's damaged and more likely to fail. Stop using and charging it, don't press or puncture it, and get it to a mentor.

Part 2 · C rate

C rate turns capacity into current, and amp-hours turn current into time

C rate

Current = C rate × capacity. A 5 Ah pack at 2C is 2 × 5 = 10 A. Backward: C rate = amps ÷ Ah. 1C empties a full battery in about an hour, 2C in about 30 minutes.

Runtime

Ideal hours = Ah ÷ A, and hours × 60 gives minutes. A 6 Ah pack running a 1.5 A light lasts 6 ÷ 1.5 = 4 hours. Real runtime is shorter at high current, especially for lead acid.

Energy used

Ah used = A × hours. Convert time to hours first: minutes ÷ 60, seconds ÷ 3,600. A robot averaging 30 A for 2 minutes: 2 ÷ 60 = 1/30 h, and 30 × 1/30 = 1.0 Ah.

Part 2 · Voltage sag

Under load, the battery loses current times its internal resistance

The math

Sag = I × internal resistance. Voltage at the terminals = resting voltage - sag. Convert milliohms first: 25 mΩ is 0.025 ohms (divide by 1,000). Backward: current = sag ÷ internal resistance.

Worked example

Resting 12.8 V, 25 mΩ, the drivetrain pulls 120 A: sag = 120 × 0.025 = 3.0 V, so the terminals read 12.8 - 3.0 = 9.8 V.

Why it matters

More internal resistance means more sag. A worn battery can brown out the robot under hard driving even when a meter says it's full.

Part 2 · Wire sizing

The breaker protects the wire, so the wire is sized to the breaker, not the load

The reason

In a short, the wire has to carry up to the 's current until it trips. A wire sized only for the load could overheat before the breaker opens.

Worked example

A 12 V, 36 W device draws 36 ÷ 12 = 3 A. On a 15 A breaker it still needs 18 , since 18 covers 6 to 20 A breakers. 22 AWG is only for breakers of 5 A or less.

5.8 · Terminals

Keep metal off the terminals, and bolt the lug straight to the tab

Never short it

When you work on one terminal, keep the other covered. Never set a wrench or tool on top of the battery. Never let a tool touch both terminals, or a terminal and the frame. 12 V won't shock you, but a short pulls hundreds of amps, welds the tool, burns you, and can crack the battery. Our tools aren't insulated.

The lug

The lug and the battery tab touch copper to copper, with no of any kind between them. A locking nut keeps it tight. A loose lug can reboot the robot mid-match.

Cover it

Every bit of copper on the terminals and lugs is covered. WPILib calls for 3:1 at minimum, 4:1 recommended.

5.8 · Damage

A dropped battery waits for a mentor, and acid gets neutralized before cleanup

Dropped

Set it aside, tell a mentor, and tag it so nobody uses or charges it until a mentor inspects it. A drop can bend plates inside or leave a crack you can't see that leaks later. The Battery Beak can't see a cracked case. Never try to repair a damaged battery.

Leaking

Battery acid is sulfuric acid: it burns skin, eyes, and clothes. Tell a mentor, put on safety glasses and acid-resistant gloves, and pour baking soda on everything wet to neutralize it. The battery goes in a leak-proof container that isn't metal; it's hazardous waste.

On skin

Flush acid off skin with lots of water.

5.8 · Charging

Let it cool, keep the vent clear, and never open a charger

Charging

Use the team's battery chargers, at 6 A or less per battery. A battery straight off the robot cools until the case isn't warm, about 15 minutes, before it charges. Don't cover the vent on top, and keep air moving around the charger.

Never open it

Nobody opens a charger or a power supply, even unplugged. A charger acting up is a mentor call, and usually a replacement.

Hot and bulged

A battery on the charger that's hot and bulging is damaged. Unplug the charger from the wall instead of pulling the battery's connector, which can spark. Leave the battery where it is, keep people back, and get a mentor.

5.8 · Battery Beak

Match batteries read Good and at least 110%

Readings

The Beak (our handheld tester) shows Status and Charge. Good and 110% or more is ready for a match. Fair means higher internal resistance than normal: practice and testing only. A healthy battery under 110% just needs charging.

Testing right

Test after it rests about 15 minutes off the charger, so surface charge doesn't fool the reading. Wait at least 5 seconds between tests so the Beak doesn't overheat. If it reads Bad or Charge Battery, recharge and retest. Still Bad: out of service.

What it can't see

The Beak measures internal resistance and charge. It can't see a crack, a bulge, or a loose lug, so inspect the battery too.

5.8 · Internal resistance

Internal resistance is the voltage drop divided by the extra current

The formula

Internal resistance = (change in voltage) ÷ (change in current). The Beak does this by testing at two loads; you can do it by hand.

Worked example

12.60 V at 1 A and 12.15 V at 19 A. Change in voltage: 0.45 V. Change in current: 18 A. 0.45 ÷ 18 = 0.025 ohms = 25 mΩ (multiply ohms by 1,000).

What's good

WPILib calls under 15 mΩ ideal and suggests keeping batteries over 20 mΩ out of matches.

5.8 · Storage

Store lead acid charged and cool; store lithium packs about half charged

FRC batteries

Store them fully charged, and top them up regularly or keep them on a maintenance charger. Keep them climate controlled, not in a hot container. Left drained, lead acid sulfates: crystals harden on the plates and permanently cut capacity. They lose about 0.1 V a month sitting. An old charger with no maintenance mode overcharges and boils off electrolyte.

Lithium tool packs

Ryobi, Makita, Milwaukee: charge each on its own brand's charger, with air around it. Not in a hot car or the sun. Store spares so their terminals can't touch loose metal like keys or a binder ring. Swollen, cracked, or damaged goes to a mentor. For long storage, about half charged in a cool place.

5.8 · Fire

A smoking pack means shout, make sure the mentor knows, and get out

Get out

Yell "fire," make sure the mentor knows, and get out. The mentor or the experienced student leader handles the extinguisher. Everyone else leaves and stays out.

One exception

If the fire is between you and the only exit, you fight it to get out.

Why

A venting lithium-ion cell can flare up, burn at about 500°C (932°F), give off toxic smoke, and reignite after it looks out. Don't pick it up, unplug it, or go near it.

Words in this guide

ABS
A tough plastic that handles heat well. It gives off fumes when printed, so it needs an enclosed, ventilated printer.
Anderson connector
The flat, genderless battery connector used on FRC robots. Either half plugs into the other.
AWG
American Wire Gauge: the number for a wire's size. A smaller number means a thicker wire that can carry more current.
breaker
A switch that cuts power. It trips on its own when too much current flows, protecting the wires. The robot's main breaker is also how you turn the whole robot off.
continuity
An unbroken path for electricity. A multimeter beeps when it finds one.
crimp
To squeeze a metal connector onto a wire with a crimping tool so it holds tight and conducts.
FRC
FIRST Robotics Competition: the high school robotics program the Rambots compete in.
heat shrink
Plastic tubing that shrinks tight around a connection when heated, to cover and protect it.
PDH
Power distribution hub: the board that takes power from the battery and sends it out to every motor controller and device on the robot.
PETG
A tough 3D printing plastic that bends a little before it breaks and handles more heat than PLA.
PLA
The easiest 3D printing plastic. It prints at low temperatures and is stiff, but it softens in heat, like a hot car.
solder
Metal melted with a hot iron to join wires or parts electrically.
washer
A flat ring under a screw head or nut that spreads the load and protects the surface.
