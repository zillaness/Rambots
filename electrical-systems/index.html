THE BASICS OF ELECTRICITY
ATOMS · ELECTRONS · CONDUCTORS · INSULATORS · CHARGE · VOLTAGE · CURRENT · RESISTANCE · POWER

⚛ ATOMS: THE BUILDING BLOCKS

All matter is made of atoms. An atom has three types of subatomic particles:

  • Protons — in the nucleus, carry a positive (+) charge. The number of protons defines what element the atom is (copper = 29 protons).
  • Neutrons — also in the nucleus, carry no charge. They add mass and help hold the nucleus together.
  • Electrons — orbit the nucleus in shells (energy levels), carry a negative (−) charge. Equal in number to protons in a neutral atom.

Opposite charges attract; like charges repel. The attraction between the positive nucleus and the negative electrons keeps electrons in orbit. The outermost shell (valence shell) electrons are the ones that matter most for electricity.

What is an electron vs an ion?

  • An electron is a subatomic particle — tiny, nearly massless (9.1×10⁻³¹ kg), carrying exactly one unit of negative charge. Electrons can travel freely through metallic conductors because metals have loosely bound valence electrons.
  • An ion is an entire atom (or molecule) that has gained or lost one or more electrons, giving it a net charge. A sodium ion (Na⁺) is a whole sodium atom missing one electron. Ions are massive compared to free electrons — they move much more slowly and require an ionic medium (like a liquid electrolyte) to move at all.

COPPER ATOM — THE ELECTRICIAN'S FAVORITE

🔬 ELECTRONS vs IONS — WHY THEY'RE FUNDAMENTALLY DIFFERENT CHARGE CARRIERS

Electron conduction (metals / wires): In a copper wire, the outermost valence electron of each copper atom is so loosely bound that at room temperature it has already escaped from its parent atom and wanders freely through the metal lattice. These are called free electrons or the electron sea. When you apply a voltage, these free electrons all drift in the same direction (slowly — drift velocity is only mm/second), but because there are roughly 8.5 × 10²⁸ of them per cubic meter in copper, even a tiny drift velocity produces enormous current.

The "signal" (the electric field effect) propagates at nearly the speed of light through the wire — like a pipe already full of water where pressure at one end immediately affects the other end. The electrons themselves move slowly; the electromagnetic wave moves fast.

Speed of light in vacuum: c = 299,792,458 m/s ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s (186,282 miles/sec)
Electrical signal in copper wire (VoP ≈ 0.66): ≈ 2 × 10⁸ m/s (200,000 km/s)
Electron drift velocity at 1A in 1mm² copper: ≈ 0.074 mm/s
The signal travels 2.7 billion times faster than the electrons carrying it. Electrons move slower than a snail (~50 mm/s). The electromagnetic wave is what does the work.

The propagation speed in a wire is slower than in vacuum because the insulation material has a dielectric constant greater than 1, which slows the electromagnetic wave. The velocity of propagation (VoP) — typically printed on cable datasheets — expresses this as a fraction of c: PVC-insulated wire ≈ 0.66c, Cat6 Ethernet ≈ 0.64c, air-dielectric coax ≈ 0.99c.

⚡ Footnote — Fiber Optic: Fiber optic cable carries signals as photons (light pulses) through a glass core rather than electrons through metal. Light in glass travels at ~0.67c (the glass slows it slightly via its refractive index of ~1.5). Fiber is completely immune to EMI — photons don't care about magnetic fields — and provides galvanic isolation (no electrical connection at all). It's used in long-distance telecom, industrial ethernet in noisy environments, and medical equipment. Not currently common in FRC, but some high-end industrial servo systems use fiber optic encoder cables to keep switching noise from corrupting position data over long cable runs.

Ion conduction (electrolytes / batteries): In a battery's electrolyte (sulfuric acid in lead-acid, lithium salt solution in Li-ion), the charge carriers are entire ions — atoms or molecules carrying a charge. In lead-acid, sulfate ions (SO₄²⁻) and hydrogen ions (H⁺) move through the liquid. Ions are thousands of times heavier than electrons (a proton is ~1836× heavier than an electron), move much more slowly, and require a liquid or specially doped solid medium.

This is crucial: Inside a battery, ion flow carries the charge through the electrolyte. Outside the battery (through the wires and robot), electron flow carries the charge. The battery's job is to convert between these two modes at each electrode — the chemical reaction at the electrode surface is the handoff point between ionic and electronic conduction.

VOCABULARY — CATIONS & ANIONS

Cation — an ion with a net positive charge (the atom has lost one or more electrons). Cat-ions are PAWsitive! 🐾 (Cats have paws. Cations have positive charge. You're welcome — you'll never forget this.)

Anion — an ion with a net negative charge (gained one or more electrons). Memory trick: Anion = Negative. Anions are attracted toward the positive electrode in an electrochemical cell.

In a lead-acid battery electrolyte (dilute H₂SO₄), there are two ion species doing the work:

  • H⁺ (hydrogen ion / proton) — a cation. Moves toward the negative electrode during discharge.
  • SO₄²⁻ (sulfate ion) — an anion. Moves toward the positive electrode during discharge.

Together they complete the internal circuit while electrons do the work through the external wire. Neither ion can exist in solid metal — they need the liquid electrolyte as their medium. This is why the separator must allow ion flow but block electron flow (see battery section §21).

🔌 WHAT MAKES A GOOD CONDUCTOR?

A material conducts electricity well when it has large numbers of free charge carriers available at room temperature. This comes down to electron shell structure:

  • Metals have 1–3 valence electrons in their outermost shell, and these electrons are delocalized — they don't belong to any specific atom but form a "sea" shared across the whole material. Apply any electric field and they drift immediately.
  • The fewer valence electrons, the more loosely bound they tend to be, and the better the conductivity. Copper (1 valence e⁻), silver (1 valence e⁻), and gold (1 valence e⁻) are all excellent conductors for this reason.
  • The metallic crystal structure also matters — regularly arranged atoms allow the electron sea to flow freely without scattering. Defects, impurities, and temperature all increase scattering and raise resistance.

Why temperature affects resistance: As temperature rises, atoms vibrate more violently, scattering electrons more frequently. This is why copper resistance increases with temperature — which is why motors and wires heat up during high current and their resistance increases, slightly reducing current (a self-limiting effect called positive temperature coefficient, or PTC).

🚫 WHAT MAKES A GOOD INSULATOR?

Insulators have no free charge carriers. Their valence electrons are tightly bound in covalent or ionic bonds, with a large "band gap" — a large energy barrier that prevents electrons from becoming free. You'd need enormous electric fields (far beyond anything in an FRC robot) to force conduction.

Common insulators: rubber, PVC plastic, glass, ceramic, wood (dry), air, diamond, PTFE. These all share the property that their electrons are essentially locked in place at room temperature.

Semiconductors are in between — at room temperature they're poor conductors, but adding small amounts of specific impurities (doping) or applying heat/light creates some free carriers. Silicon doped with phosphorus or boron becomes a semiconductor — the basis of every transistor and microchip. We'll cover this in depth in §22.

🌡 WHY GOOD ELECTRICAL CONDUCTORS TEND TO BE GOOD THERMAL CONDUCTORS

This is not a coincidence — it's explained by the same physics. In a metal, heat is also transported primarily by free electrons. When you heat one end of a copper rod, electrons at that end gain kinetic energy and rapidly diffuse toward the cooler end, carrying energy with them. This is far more efficient than the lattice vibration (phonon) mechanism that dominates heat transport in insulators.

The Wiedemann-Franz Law formalizes this: the ratio of thermal conductivity to electrical conductivity is approximately constant for metals at a given temperature (the Lorenz number). A better electrical conductor = proportionally better thermal conductor.

This is why: you use copper wire (good conductor) but also use copper heat spreaders in electronics. Aluminum is used in both power lines and heatsinks. And why you don't use plastic (insulator) for either electricity or heat dissipation.

⚡ VOLTAGE (V) — ELECTRIC POTENTIAL

Voltage is the difference in electric potential — the "pressure" pushing electrons. Think of it as the height difference between two water reservoirs; gravity (voltage) drives water (current) to flow when connected.

No voltage difference = no current flow, just like no height difference = no water flow. FRC Robot: 12V nominal; drops under load; some subsystems regulate to 5V or 3.3V.

〰 CURRENT (A) — FLOW RATE OF CHARGE

Current is how many electrons pass a point per second. 1 Ampere = 6.24×10¹⁸ electrons/second. Like the flow rate (gallons/minute) in a water system.

Current causes heat (P=I²R). Too much current through too-small a wire → fire. This is what fuses and breakers protect against. Stalling a Kraken X60 can pull over 350A instantaneously.

🔴 RESISTANCE (Ω) — OPPOSITION TO FLOW

Resistance opposes current flow and converts electrical energy into heat. Every conductor has some resistance — the goal is to minimize it in the power path. Bad connections, undersized wire, and corrosion all add resistance and cause voltage drop under load.

⚡ POWER (W) — ENERGY PER SECOND

Power = Voltage × Current. A Kraken at 12V drawing 50A = 600W. Power does the work — spins motors, lights LEDs. Your robot has a finite energy budget (the battery). High-power mechanisms drain it fast; smart power management wins matches.

Where the analogy breaks down:

  • Water is compressible / has inertia at high flow rates in ways that don't map cleanly. Electrical "inertia" (inductance) has no water counterpart.
  • Electrons don't "pile up" the way water can pool. Current in a series circuit is identical at every point simultaneously.
  • AC has no clean water analogy — water doesn't reverse 60 times per second usefully.
  • Electromagnetic effects — a wire carrying current creates a magnetic field. No water pipe equivalent.
  • Speed of signal vs speed of carrier — the electric field propagates at ~0.7c; the electrons drift at mm/s. Water pressure and water flow rate travel at similar speeds.
  • Quantum effects dominate at small scales (transistors) — water has no quantum tunneling equivalent.

Use the analogy to build intuition, but don't over-extend it. It fails most badly when discussing AC, inductance, capacitance, and semiconductor behavior.

RESISTORS — CONTROLLING CURRENT FLOW

Ω WHAT RESISTORS DO

A resistor is a passive component that opposes current flow according to Ohm's Law (V=IR). It converts electrical energy into heat. Uses: limit current to an LED, create voltage dividers for sensor inputs, set bias points for transistors, pull signal lines to a defined voltage level (pull-up/pull-down resistors).

Key specs: Resistance value (Ω, kΩ, MΩ), tolerance (±1%, ±5%), and power rating (0.125W, 0.25W, 0.5W, 1W…). Power rating is critical — exceed it and the resistor burns up. P = I²R; calculate dissipation before choosing a resistor.

Types:

  • Carbon film — cheap, ±5% tolerance, general purpose (the standard brown cylindrical resistor)
  • Metal film — better accuracy (±1% or better), lower noise, preferred for precision circuits
  • Wirewound — high power handling (1W–100W+), used in power supplies and motor braking circuits
  • SMD chip resistors — surface mount, tiny packages (0402, 0603, 0805), used on all modern PCBs
  • Thermistor (NTC/PTC) — resistance changes with temperature; used as temperature sensors
  • Potentiometer / trimmer — variable resistor; used for user-adjustable controls and calibration

CAPACITORS — STORING CHARGE, FILTERING NOISE

⚡ WHAT CAPACITORS DO

A capacitor stores electrical energy in an electric field between two conductive plates separated by an insulator (the dielectric). Unlike a battery which stores energy chemically, a capacitor stores it electrostatically and can release it almost instantaneously.

Q = C × V     E = ½CV²
Q = charge (Coulombs), C = capacitance (Farads), V = voltage, E = energy stored (Joules)

Key behaviors:

  • Capacitors block DC (once fully charged, no DC flows through them) but pass AC (alternating charge/discharge)
  • They resist changes in voltage — the voltage across a capacitor cannot change instantaneously
  • Larger capacitance = more charge stored = longer time to charge/discharge at a given current

Critical FRC application — decoupling caps: Motor controllers switching at 20kHz create voltage spikes on the power supply. Capacitors placed close to the power input "absorb" these spikes, preventing them from propagating to sensitive electronics. Every motor controller datasheet recommends decoupling capacitors on the supply rails.

INDUCTORS — STORING ENERGY IN MAGNETIC FIELDS

〰 WHAT INDUCTORS DO

An inductor (also called a coil or choke) is a coil of wire, usually wound around a magnetic core. It stores energy in a magnetic field when current flows through it. Key behavior: an inductor resists changes in current — it cannot change current instantaneously, just as a capacitor cannot change voltage instantaneously. They are in a sense dual components.

V = L × (dI/dt)     E = ½LI²
V = induced voltage, L = inductance (Henries), dI/dt = rate of current change, E = stored energy

Inductive kick — the dangerous property: When current through an inductor is suddenly interrupted (e.g., a relay switches off a motor), the magnetic field collapses and the inductor tries to maintain current — generating a large voltage spike in the opposite direction. This "inductive kick" or "flyback" can easily reach hundreds of volts and destroy transistors, MOSFETs, and relay contacts. This is why flyback diodes (also called freewheeling diodes) are placed across every motor, relay coil, and solenoid.

Inductor types: Air-core (small value, used in RF), ferrite bead (suppress high-frequency noise on power lines), toroidal (efficient power inductors in switching regulators), common-mode choke (filters EMI from differential signals).

FRC relevance: Motor windings ARE inductors. The inductance of a motor winding is why PWM-switched current is smoothed into near-DC average current — the motor's own inductance filters the switching pulses. Also: the solenoid valves on the pneumatic hub have inductive coils — the REV Pneumatic Hub has built-in flyback protection for this reason.

⚠️ THE FLYBACK DIODE — PROTECTING YOUR CIRCUITS

Any time you switch a relay coil, motor, or solenoid, the inductive kick can destroy the transistor/MOSFET doing the switching. The solution: place a diode in reverse bias across the coil. Under normal operation, the diode is reverse biased and doesn't conduct. When the coil is switched off and the inductive kick creates a reverse voltage spike, the diode becomes forward biased and provides a safe path for the current, clamping the voltage spike to ~0.7V above supply.

TRANSFORMERS — CHANGING VOLTAGE LEVELS

🔄 WHAT A TRANSFORMER DOES

A transformer uses electromagnetic induction to transfer energy between two electrically isolated circuits while changing the voltage. It consists of two (or more) coils of wire wound on a shared magnetic core. Transformers only work on AC — DC produces a static magnetic field that does not induce a changing voltage in the secondary coil.

Vs/Vp = Ns/Np    Is/Ip = Np/Ns
V = voltage, I = current, N = number of turns, p = primary, s = secondary
Power is conserved: Vp × Ip ≈ Vs × Is (ideal transformer)

Step-down transformer: More turns on primary than secondary → lower output voltage, higher current. Your phone's USB-C wall adapter contains a transformer stepping 120VAC down. The little black box is mostly transformer + rectifier.

Step-up transformer: More turns on secondary → higher voltage, lower current. Used to step 13.8kV from substations up to 345kV for long-distance transmission, then step back down near homes.

Galvanic isolation: Transformers provide complete electrical isolation between primary and secondary — they share no conductive path, only a magnetic field. This is essential in medical equipment, isolated power supplies, and audio equipment where ground loops must be broken.

Why this matters for FRC: The Voltage Regulator Module (VRM) and the Radio power supply on an FRC robot are switching regulators — they use high-frequency transformer action (with MOSFETs doing the switching) to convert 12V DC down to 5V or 12V regulated output. Modern DC-DC converters are essentially transformers operated at 100kHz–2MHz where the transformer can be tiny (a few millimeters) rather than the large 60Hz transformers in power supplies.

Battery charger: Your FRC battery charger plugs into 120VAC and outputs 14.4V DC. Inside: transformer steps down voltage → bridge rectifier converts AC to DC → regulation circuit holds exact output voltage. This chain (transformer → rectifier → filter → regulator) is the canonical AC-to-DC power supply architecture.

ℹ️
This is why Tesla/AC beat Edison/DC for power transmission: Transformers can step AC up to very high voltages (reducing current, reducing I²R losses in transmission lines) and step back down near the consumer. DC cannot be transformed this way without complex electronics — in 1887, those didn't exist. More on this in the next section.
DIODES — ONE-WAY VALVES FOR ELECTRICITY

→ WHAT A DIODE DOES

A diode is the simplest semiconductor device — a P-N junction that allows current to flow freely in one direction (forward bias) and blocks it in the other (reverse bias). Like a check valve in a water pipe.

Forward bias: Anode (+) to cathode (−). The P-N junction potential barrier is overcome at the forward voltage drop (~0.6–0.7V for silicon, ~0.2–0.3V for Schottky, ~1.8–3.3V for LEDs). Current flows freely above this threshold.

Reverse bias: Current is blocked (leakage current is negligible, typically nanoamps). If reverse voltage exceeds the breakdown voltage (PIV), the diode conducts in reverse — destructively for rectifier diodes, but controllably for Zeners.

LIGHT EMITTING DIODES — FROM INDICATOR TO ILLUMINATION

💡 HOW AN LED ACTUALLY MAKES LIGHT

An LED is a p-n junction diode with one critical difference from a rectifier diode: the semiconductor material is chosen so that when electrons cross the junction (recombining with holes), they release their energy as photons (light) rather than heat. The energy of the photon — and therefore its color — is determined by the bandgap of the specific semiconductor material.

This is direct bandgap emission: E = hf = hc/λ. Larger bandgap → higher-energy photons → shorter wavelength → blue/violet end of spectrum. Smaller bandgap → lower energy → longer wavelength → red/infrared.

LEDs are extraordinarily efficient light sources because they convert electrical energy directly to photons without the intermediate step of heating a filament to incandescence. A modern white LED can produce 200+ lumens per watt; a tungsten incandescent manages about 15 lm/W — most of the energy goes out as heat.

🌈 RGB LEDs — THREE IN ONE

An RGB LED is simply three separate LED dies (red, green, blue) in a single package sharing a common terminal. By varying the current (and therefore brightness) of each channel, the full visible color spectrum can be approximated through additive color mixing:

  • R+G+B at full brightness → white (or near-white)
  • R+G (blue off) → yellow/amber
  • R+B (green off) → magenta/pink
  • G+B (red off) → cyan
  • Any combination → ~16 million colors with 8-bit PWM per channel

Common anode vs common cathode:

  • Common cathode (−): All three GND leads tied together. Apply +V to red, green, or blue anode to light that color. Drive anodes HIGH = on. Most common in discrete RGB packages.
  • Common anode (+): All three + leads tied together (connect to +V). Drive cathodes LOW = on. Less intuitive but sometimes used in circuit designs where sinking current is easier.

Forward voltage mismatch matters: Red has Vf ≈ 2.0V; blue/green have Vf ≈ 3.2V. In a common anode RGB LED at 5V, the current-limiting resistors for each channel must be calculated separately. Using one resistor for all three channels produces incorrect brightness ratios and potential damage to the lower-voltage channels.

R = (Vsupply − Vf) ÷ Idesired
At 5V, 20mA: Rred = (5−2.0)/0.020 = 150Ω    Rblue = (5−3.2)/0.020 = 90Ω

🔵 ADDRESSABLE RGB LEDs — NEOPIXEL / WS2812B

Addressable RGB LEDs (WS2812B, SK6812, APA102) embed a tiny controller IC directly inside each LED package alongside the R, G, B dies. Each LED on a strip has its own address and can be set to any of 16.7 million colors independently by sending serial data through a single wire.

How WS2812B / NeoPixel works: Data enters at DIN (data in). The first pixel reads the first 24 bits (8R, 8G, 8B) as its own color command, then passes all remaining data to DOUT → next pixel's DIN. A reset pulse (>50µs low) latches all pixels simultaneously. You can chain hundreds of pixels on one GPIO pin.

💡 COB LEDs — CHIP ON BOARD

Chip On Board (COB) LEDs are constructed by mounting multiple bare LED semiconductor dies directly onto a PCB or aluminum substrate without individual packaging. The dies are wire-bonded to the board and encapsulated together under a single lens or phosphor layer. The result is a dense, high-power light source that appears as one large glowing area rather than individual points.

Why COB over individual LEDs?

  • Thermal performance: Heat spreads across the whole substrate simultaneously rather than concentrating at individual chip packages. COB on aluminum core PCB can dissipate 10–100W without hotspots.
  • Uniform light output: No visible individual LED points, no pixelation. The phosphor coating over all dies creates one smooth light source — essential for photography/studio lighting.
  • High lumen density: A 50mm diameter COB can produce 5,000–10,000 lumens. Comparable discrete LED arrays would need a much larger PCB.
  • Lower cost per lumen at high power: Packaging costs are eliminated; you pay for silicon area and substrate, not hundreds of individual packages.

Where you find COB LEDs: LED flashlights (single COB at the end), grow lights, video studio lights, automotive headlights (projector-style), LED streetlights, industrial high-bay fixtures, and cinema-grade lighting. The "warm glow" lighting you see in high-end architectural installations is usually COB with carefully tuned phosphor.

vs SMD LEDs: Surface-mount SMD LEDs (2835, 5050, 3528 package numbers are their dimensions in 0.1mm) are individually packaged and are what you find on LED strips. COB provides higher power density; SMD provides flexibility for strip/matrix configurations.

ℹ️
LED STRIP PACKAGE SIZES: The cryptic numbers on LED strips are dimensions in 0.1mm. A "5050" LED is 5.0mm × 5.0mm and contains three dies (great for RGB). A "2835" is 2.8mm × 3.5mm — single die, higher efficiency, better for white lighting. A "3528" is 3.5mm × 2.8mm — single die, older standard. 60/m, 144/m, etc. refers to LED density per meter of strip.

⚡ RELAYS — ELECTRICALLY CONTROLLED SWITCHES

A relay uses a small control signal (typically 5V/12V coil) to mechanically switch a larger power circuit. An electromagnet (the coil) attracts an armature, which moves a set of contacts. This provides complete electrical isolation between the control and power circuits — your 5V Arduino can switch a 240VAC circuit safely through a relay.

Key relay specs: Coil voltage (5V, 12V, 24V), coil current (50–200mA typically), contact rating (10A/250VAC typical), and contact configuration (SPDT, DPDT).

Relay types:

  • Electromechanical relay (EMR): Physical moving parts. Audible click. Can switch AC and DC. Slow (5–15ms operation time). Contact wear over millions of cycles.
  • Solid State Relay (SSR): No moving parts — uses optoisolator + TRIAC/MOSFET inside. Silent, fast (<1ms), no contact wear. More expensive. Output is AC-only (most common type) or DC-switched.

⚠ Always use a flyback diode across the relay coil — the coil is an inductor, and switching it off causes an inductive kick. Without a diode, this spike destroys the driver transistor. See inductor section.

FRC use: Modern FRC robots rarely use relays (Spike relay modules are now legacy). Motor controllers (TalonFX, SparkMAX) replaced relay-based motor control. However, custom boards sometimes use relays to control high-current/high-voltage accessories (lights, pneumatic compressors in older designs).

INTEGRATED CIRCUITS — CIRCUITS ON A CHIP

💻 WHAT IS AN INTEGRATED CIRCUIT?

An integrated circuit (IC) contains thousands to billions of transistors, resistors, capacitors, and diodes fabricated on a single silicon chip. The invention of the IC (Jack Kilby, Texas Instruments, 1958) enabled modern electronics — without it, a smartphone would require a building-sized collection of discrete components.

Linear (analog) ICs:

  • Op-Amp (operational amplifier): Amplifies the difference between two inputs. Foundation of analog signal processing, filters, comparators, instrumentation amplifiers. Used on many sensor PCBs.
  • Voltage regulator (LM7805, L78xx): Maintains a fixed output voltage regardless of input variation. Every electronics system needs voltage regulation.
  • Comparator: Outputs HIGH or LOW based on which of two input voltages is larger. Used in sensor threshold detection.

Digital ICs:

  • Logic gates (74HC series): AND, OR, NOT, NAND, NOR, XOR. The building blocks of all digital logic.
  • Flip-flops: 1-bit memory elements; chains of them make registers and counters.
  • Shift registers (74HC595): Expand output pins — send 8 bits serially, get 8 parallel outputs. Used to drive many LEDs from 3 GPIO pins.
  • Multiplexers (74HC4051): Select one of N inputs to route to one output. Used for analog sensor selection.

Mixed-signal ICs:

  • ADC (Analog-to-Digital Converter): Converts continuous analog voltage to digital number. Every sensor reading goes through one.
  • DAC (Digital-to-Analog): Opposite — converts digital number to analog voltage.

Complex ICs (FRC-relevant):

  • Microcontroller (ATmega, STM32, RP2040): CPU + memory + peripherals on one chip. The RoboRIO uses an ARM Cortex-A9 processor chip. Motor controllers (TalonFX, SparkMAX) use STM32 microcontrollers.
  • FPGA (Field Programmable Gate Array): Reconfigurable digital logic fabric. The RoboRIO contains a Xilinx FPGA for real-time I/O processing.
  • IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit): 3-axis gyro + accelerometer on one chip (e.g., ADIS16470 used in the Pigeon 2.0).
  • CAN transceiver (MCP2551, SN65HVD230): Converts microcontroller logic levels to CAN bus differential signals.
  • Gate driver (DRV8301): Sits between microcontroller and power MOSFETs; provides enough current/voltage to drive MOSFET gates quickly in motor controllers.

⚡ ASIC — APPLICATION SPECIFIC INTEGRATED CIRCUITS

An ASIC is a chip designed and manufactured to perform exactly one specific task. Unlike a CPU (which can run any program) or FPGA (which can be reprogrammed), an ASIC's logic is permanently etched in silicon. This makes it extraordinarily fast and power-efficient for its one purpose — but completely useless for anything else.

Examples: Bitcoin mining ASICs (run SHA-256 hashing billions of times/second), Google TPU (Tensor Processing Unit — matrix multiply for AI), Apple Neural Engine, the PPU and SPC chips in the original SNES, custom Wi-Fi chips in routers.

Crypto mining and the ASIC arms race: Bitcoin mining started on CPUs (2009), moved to GPUs (better at the parallel hashing required), then in 2013 ASICs appeared. A dedicated SHA-256 ASIC mines Bitcoin millions of times faster than a GPU while using a fraction of the power. Once ASICs entered the market, GPU Bitcoin mining became uneconomic overnight. Ethereum deliberately designed its mining algorithm (Ethash) to resist ASICs — keeping GPU miners viable until the "Merge" in September 2022 when Ethereum switched to Proof of Stake and eliminated mining entirely. The GPU market crashed shortly after as the mining demand evaporated.

AMD + Xilinx: In February 2022 AMD completed its $49 billion acquisition of Xilinx, the FPGA company co-founded by Ross Freeman (who invented the FPGA concept) in 1984. Xilinx was the #2 FPGA maker behind Intel/Altera. AMD rebranded Xilinx products as "AMD FPGAs and Adaptive SoCs." Notably, the RoboRIO 2.0 (used in FRC since 2022) is built around the Xilinx Zynq-7020 SoC — combining an ARM Cortex-A9 dual-core processor with FPGA fabric on a single chip. Every RoboRIO 2.0 in FRC contains what is now officially an AMD chip.

🎮 FPGAs IN RETRO GAMING — HARDWARE RECREATION vs SOFTWARE EMULATION

FPGAs have found an enthusiastic home in retro gaming preservation, and the difference between FPGA-based recreation and software emulation is directly related to everything we've learned about how digital logic works.

Software emulation (RetroArch, MAME, PCSX2, Dolphin): A modern x86 CPU runs software that interprets the instructions of the original processor. Each original instruction is translated into multiple modern instructions. The emulator simulates the behavior of the hardware mathematically — it doesn't be the hardware, it pretends to be. Timing is approximated, not exact. This works well for most games, but edge cases, audio timing, and cycle-accurate behavior can differ from original hardware.

FPGA hardware recreation (MiSTer project, Analogue brand): An FPGA is programmed with a "core" — a hardware description of the original system's logic gates, down to the exact behavior of the CPU, PPU (graphics chip), audio chips, and memory timing. The FPGA is the hardware, cycle-accurately. Every pixel renders on the exact same clock cycle as original hardware. Audio timing, bus behavior, and even hardware glitches are reproduced precisely.

Why it matters:

  • Competitive/speedrunning players require exact input timing — FPGA = no input lag differences from original hardware
  • Some games depend on hardware bugs that emulators paper over but FPGAs reproduce faithfully
  • Arcade preservation: original PCBs rot and capacitors fail. FPGA cores keep arcade hardware playable forever

AC vs DC
ALTERNATING CURRENT · DIRECT CURRENT · WHY IT MATTERS

〰 ALTERNATING CURRENT (AC)

AC reverses direction of electron flow periodically, described by a sine wave. In North America, the grid runs at 120V / 60Hz — it completes 60 cycles per second. Europe uses 230V / 50Hz.

Why AC for the power grid? Transformers can easily step AC voltage up (to reduce transmission losses over long distances) and step it back down. This was the decisive advantage in the "War of Currents" (Edison's DC vs Tesla/Westinghouse's AC, ~1880s). Tesla/AC won.

AC uses: Wall outlets, building power, AC motors (industrial), power transmission lines. Your battery charger's plug-in side is AC.

Danger: AC is more dangerous at the same voltage because it causes muscle tetanus — the alternating current prevents your muscles from releasing, making it harder to let go of a live conductor.

→ DIRECT CURRENT (DC)

DC flows in one direction — from the negative terminal through the circuit to the positive terminal (conventional current is defined the other way: + to −, but electrons actually flow − to +). Voltage is constant (not a sine wave).

Why DC for robots? Batteries produce DC. Electronics (processors, sensors, motor controllers) all require DC. DC is easily regulated to precise voltages (12V → 5V → 3.3V) with switching regulators.

DC uses: Batteries, solar panels, USB, computer internals, LED lighting, all FRC robot systems. Your battery charger's output side is DC.

The FRC robot is 100% DC — 12V from the battery, distributed throughout. Never connect AC to robot components.

CONVERTING AC ↔ DC — RECTIFIERS & INVERTERS

⚡ AC → DC: THE FULL BRIDGE RECTIFIER

We covered the full bridge rectifier circuit in §3, but let's connect it to the big picture. The four-diode bridge works because diodes are one-way valves. On the positive half of the AC cycle, current flows through two specific diodes to the load in one direction. On the negative half-cycle, current flows through the other two diodes — but arrives at the load in the same direction. Both half-cycles are "flipped" to the same polarity. The result is pulsating DC.

A filter capacitor across the output charges up on the peaks and discharges slightly between them, smoothing the pulses into near-constant DC. Larger capacitance = smoother output. This is why big electrolytic capacitors are found in every power supply.

A voltage regulator IC (LM7805, etc.) then holds the output at a precise fixed voltage regardless of input variation or load current changes. This three-stage chain — transformer → bridge rectifier → filter cap → regulator — is the fundamental architecture of almost every linear DC power supply ever made.

FRC battery charger chain: 120V AC wall → transformer (step down to ~18V AC) → full bridge rectifier → filter cap → smart charging circuit → 14.4V DC to battery.

🔄 DC → AC: THE INVERTER

Going the other direction — converting DC to AC — requires an inverter. This is not mysterious: it uses the same H-bridge MOSFET circuit we described in the motor control section, except instead of driving a motor, it synthesizes an AC waveform.

By switching the top and bottom MOSFETs alternately at the desired AC frequency (e.g., 60Hz for grid-compatible output), the H-bridge produces a square wave. With filtering, this becomes a sine wave. For three-phase output (like what a BLDC motor controller produces), three H-bridges operating 120° out of phase create the three-phase AC waveform.

Variable Frequency Drive (VFD): Industrial motors (the big 3-phase AC induction motors running factory equipment, HVAC systems, elevators, electric cars) are controlled by VFDs — essentially large motor controller inverters that synthesize any frequency and voltage of 3-phase AC from a DC bus. A Tesla's drive inverter is a $2,000+ VFD the size of a shoebox, doing the same MOSFET switching as an FRC motor controller but at 200kW.

The symmetry: Rectifier (AC→DC) uses diodes. Inverter (DC→AC) uses controlled switches (MOSFETs). Together they explain how the same DC battery in an electric car can both be charged from AC grid power and power an AC induction motor.

💡 FROM MERCURY VASE TO VOICE COMMAND — 145 YEARS OF ELECTRIC LIGHT

🏰 CRAGSIDE ESTATE, 1880 — THE WORLD'S FIRST ELECTRICALLY LIT HOME

In December 1880, Cragside Estate in Northumberland, England became the first house in the world to be lit by domestic incandescent lamps powered by hydroelectricity. The owner, Lord William Armstrong — Victorian industrialist, inventor, and arms manufacturer — partnered with Sir Joseph Swan, who had just demonstrated his incandescent light bulb the previous year, to illuminate his home in a way no one had ever seen.

There was one small problem: the wall socket and the light switch had not been invented yet.

Swan's bulbs were installed inside four ornate Chinese cloisonné vases in the library — converted from their original kerosene lamp role. Armstrong and Swan's solution for turning them on and off was extraordinary: a mercury contact switch ingeniously built into the stand on which each vase rested.

How it worked: The metallic base of each stand was connected to the power supply. In the center sat a small insulated cup filled with liquid mercury. A bare wire ran down from the bulb inside the vase, through a hole in the vase bottom, and hung down toward this cup. The copper body of the cloisonné vase served as the return path, carrying current back to the base.

To turn the light on: place the vase on the stand. The hanging wire dips into the electrified mercury, completing the circuit. The bulb illuminates. To turn the light off: lift the vase. Wire leaves mercury. Circuit broken. Darkness.

The cloisonné enamel exterior of the vase provided just enough insulation that a person could handle a live lamp without electrocution — a detail that suggests Armstrong and Swan understood the safety problem, even if their solution looks alarming to modern eyes.

Swan vs Edison — the parallel invention: Joseph Swan demonstrated a working carbon-filament incandescent bulb in Newcastle on January 27, 1879. Thomas Edison demonstrated his version in Menlo Park, New Jersey on December 31, 1879 — nearly a year later. Swan was first to demonstrate; Edison was first to achieve a commercially viable long-lasting filament and build the infrastructure (generators, distribution, meters) to sell electricity as a utility. Edison sued Swan for patent infringement in the UK. Rather than fight it out, they merged their British businesses into the Edison and Swan United Electric Light Company — "Ediswan" — in 1883. Their lightbulb boxes bore both names.

Cragside as a Victorian smart home: Lord Armstrong was decades ahead of his time. Cragside also featured hydraulic machinery powered by water pressure — a hydraulic lift, a hydraulic spit for the kitchen, and a hydraulic ram to pump water to the house. He installed central heating, an intercom system between rooms, and an early form of power distribution throughout the house. The man was, quite literally, building a proto-smart home in the 1880s using mercury vases as switches. It is a National Trust property today; you can visit the original vases.

⚡ THE WAR OF CURRENTS — AND TOPSY THE ELEPHANT

⚔️ EDISON vs TESLA/WESTINGHOUSE — THE WAR OF CURRENTS (1880s–1890s)

Thomas Edison built America's first commercial electrical grid in 1882 using direct current (DC) at 110V. DC was safe and simple — but it couldn't be transformed to higher voltages, meaning transmission lines lost enormous power over distance. Every customer needed to live within about a mile of a power station.

Nikola Tesla (working with George Westinghouse) championed alternating current (AC): transformers could step AC up to thousands of volts for long-distance transmission with minimal loss, then step it back down for safe household use. One power station could serve an entire city.

Edison stood to lose his entire DC infrastructure investment. His response was a remarkable PR campaign: he began publicly electrocuting animals using AC current to demonstrate how dangerous it was — dogs, calves, horses — staging these as "Westinghoused" demonstrations. He lobbied (unsuccessfully) for AC to be used in the electric chair, so it would be associated with death.

The campaign ultimately failed. AC's economic advantages were overwhelming. By 1892, Westinghouse had won the contract to light the Chicago World's Fair using AC. The War of Currents was over — AC won, and powers nearly every home and factory on Earth today.

The most notorious episode came on January 4, 1903, at Luna Park, Coney Island, New York. A Circus elephant named Topsy had killed three handlers over the years (one had tried to feed her a lit cigarette). Park management decided she needed to be put down. Edison's company — with a film crew present — proposed electrocution using 6,600 volts AC. Topsy was fed carrots laced with cyanide, copper sandals were placed on her feet, and 6,600V AC was applied. She died in approximately 10 seconds. The Edison Manufacturing Company filmed the event and distributed it as a short film titled "Electrocuting an Elephant."

The episode became a symbol of the ruthlessness of the DC lobby's campaign. It is simultaneously a genuine historical curiosity, a piece of early cinema, and a grim footnote in engineering history.

AMERICAN WIRE GAUGE · WHY THE NUMBERS ARE BACKWARDS · CONSTRUCTION TYPES

📏 THE STRANGE HISTORY OF AWG

AWG = American Wire Gauge, standardized in 1857 by the Brown & Sharpe company. The system seems backwards because of how wire was made: wire is produced by drawing (pulling) a thick rod through progressively smaller dies.

The AWG number represents the number of drawing steps from the original rod diameter. More draws = smaller wire = higher AWG number. A 24 AWG wire has been drawn 24 times; a 4 AWG wire only 4 times. Larger number = more draws = thinner wire.

The system is mathematically precise: every 6 AWG steps doubles or halves the cross-sectional area. Every 3 AWG steps changes diameter by roughly √2 (≈1.41×). Going from 10 AWG to 4 AWG doubles the cross-section and thus halves the resistance per foot.

Diameter (inches) = 0.005 × 92^((36 − AWG)/39)
Every 6 AWG steps = 2× cross-sectional area
Every 3 AWG steps = √2 change in diameter

🌀 STRANDED WIRE

Construction: Many thin wires twisted together to form a single conductor. More strands = more flexible.

Why use it:

  • Resists vibration fatigue (strands distribute stress)
  • Better for robots where wires flex during movement
  • Easier to route through tight areas
  • More forgiving of repeated bending

🤖
FRC STANDARD — Use stranded wire for virtually everything on the robot. The robot vibrates, twists, and flexes constantly. Solid wire will work-harden and break.

— SOLID WIRE

Construction: A single solid cylindrical conductor with no stranding.

Why use it:

  • Lower resistance for same gauge (slightly)
  • Easier to terminate in screw clamps (no fraying)
  • Better for in-wall home electrical (NEC code often requires it)
  • Works well in breadboards and prototyping
  • Cheaper to manufacture

Disadvantages: Inflexible — repeated bending causes work hardening and eventually fractures the conductor. Terrible for anything that moves. Harder to route through conduit around corners.

🚫
DO NOT USE on FRC robots — Solid wire on a moving robot WILL eventually crack and fail at the worst possible moment.

⚠ ALUMINUM LOOKS LIKE TINNED COPPER — AND THAT IS DANGEROUS

Both aluminum wire and tinned copper wire are silver-grey in color. They can look nearly identical in a parts bin, on a spool with a worn label, or on a robot being inspected after someone else built the harness. This is not a minor cosmetic confusion — it is a fire hazard.

Why the substitution fails: Aluminum is rated at ~61% of copper's conductivity per unit area. If you install aluminum 12 AWG wire on a circuit where copper 12 AWG is specified and the breaker is sized for copper ampacity (~20–25A), the aluminum wire will overheat and potentially ignite before the breaker trips — because the breaker is calibrated for copper's higher current-carrying capacity, not aluminum's.

Aluminum's second failure mode — oxide creep: Aluminum instantly forms a hard, electrically resistive aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) layer when exposed to air. Unlike copper oxide (which is somewhat conductive), aluminum oxide is essentially an insulator. At any screw terminal or crimp connection, this oxide layer creates high resistance that generates heat. Worse, aluminum "creeps" — it slowly flows under clamping pressure over months. Connections that were tight when made gradually loosen, increasing resistance further. Loose + oxidized = arcing. This combination caused thousands of residential fires in the 1960s–70s when aluminum wiring became common in US home construction.

⚡ VOLTAGE RATING vs INSULATION THICKNESS

Insulation must be rated for the voltage it will see. FRC robot wiring is 12–24V, so almost any automotive or standard hook-up wire is massively over-rated for voltage.

Higher temperature ratings don't just mean "survives hot environments" — they also mean higher ampacity. A 10AWG wire rated to 60°C might be rated for 30A; the same wire with 90°C insulation might be rated for 40A because the insulation allows the conductor to run hotter before degrading.

Always check the wire's rated ampacity, not just the gauge, when sizing for high-current applications.

TYPES OF WIRE
SIGNAL vs POWER · COAXIAL · MULTI-CONDUCTOR · FLAT FLEX

POWER WIRE

Designed to carry significant current from a power source to a load. Key specs: gauge (AWG), insulation temperature rating, ampacity, stranding type.

  • Battery cable (6 AWG): Carries up to 120A from battery to main breaker and PDH. Must be extremely low-resistance — any resistance here causes voltage drop under load.
  • Motor wire (10–14 AWG): Carries motor current from motor controllers to motors.
  • Branch circuit wire (18–22 AWG): Powers lower-current devices — servos, solenoids, sensors with power needs.

Power wires carry high current and generate significant EMI (electromagnetic interference). Run them away from sensitive signal wires when possible.

SIGNAL WIRE

Carries data or control information, not significant power. Current is milliamps or less. Noise immunity and signal integrity matter more than ampacity.

  • Analog signal (22–28 AWG): 0–5V or 0–3.3V analog sensor outputs (potentiometers, ultrasonic, pressure sensors).
  • Digital signal (22–26 AWG): PWM, digital inputs/outputs, encoders, limit switches.
  • CAN bus (22–24 AWG): Differential pair, 1Mbps communication between devices.
  • I²C / SPI (24–28 AWG): Short-distance board-to-board communication.
  • RS-232 / UART (22–26 AWG): Serial communication to sensors, GPS modules, etc.

Signal wires are susceptible to noise from nearby power wiring, motor switching, and EMI from motor controllers. Shielding and separation help.

SHIELDING, TWISTED PAIRS & ETHERNET
EMI · DIFFERENTIAL SIGNALING · CAT5e/6 · WHY TWISTING WORKS

🛡 WHY SHIELDING?

Electromagnetic interference (EMI) is produced by any device that switches current rapidly — especially brushless motor controllers (which switch at 10–20kHz). This interference can corrupt signal wires running nearby.

How shielding works: A conductive braid or foil wrapped around signal conductors intercepts external EMI and routes it to ground instead of the signal wire. The shield must be grounded at one end only (both ends creates a ground loop, which introduces new noise).

Types of shielding:

  • Foil (STP) — Aluminum foil wrap. 100% coverage, light, less flexible. Used in Cat6A.
  • Braid — Woven copper braid. 85–95% coverage, more flexible, better for RF.
  • Spiral/serve — Wound copper tape. Most flexible, lowest coverage (~95%), used in audio cables.
  • Combination — Foil + braid. Maximum protection, used in military/industrial spec wire.

〰 TWISTED PAIRS — THE CLEVER SOLUTION

Twisting two wires together is a cheap, elegant solution to EMI without metal shielding. It works on the principle of differential signaling:

The transmitter sends two opposite-polarity versions of the signal (Signal and /Signal). The receiver reads only the difference between them. Any EMI noise that couples into the pair couples equally into both wires (common-mode noise). When the receiver subtracts one wire from the other, the common-mode noise cancels out perfectly.

Twisting ensures both wires in the pair run the same path through the EM field, guaranteeing equal noise pickup. Untwisted wires would pick up different amounts of noise, preventing cancellation.

Twist rate matters: More twists per inch = more immune to interference. Higher-category Ethernet cables have higher twist rates.

🌐 ETHERNET CABLE — T568A vs T568B, CROSSOVER CABLES & FRC WIRING

Structure of Cat5e/6: 4 twisted pairs (8 conductors total), each pair with a different twist rate to reduce crosstalk. Pairs are color-coded: Orange/Orange-White, Green/Green-White, Blue/Blue-White, Brown/Brown-White. The pairs are twisted together, and the whole assembly is jacketed. The twist rates are intentionally different for each pair so that even if two pairs physically run parallel inside the jacket, their crosstalk interference averages out.

Two wiring standards exist for how the 8 conductors map to the 8 pins of an RJ45 connector: T568A and T568B. They are electrically identical — both terminate the same four pairs to the same functional pins. The only difference is that the orange and green pairs are swapped between the two standards.

This swap has no electrical effect on a straight-through cable connecting two different types of devices (a computer to a switch). But it is the key to understanding crossover cables.

  • Virtually all pre-made patch cables sold at electronics stores
  • Most IT departments, corporate networks, data centers
  • All FRC robot wiring — RoboRIO, radio, driver station

  • Some school districts and universities that standardized on T568A in the early 1990s and haven't rewired
  • Some international installations — T568A is the preferred standard in several countries outside North America
  • Legacy infrastructure — if you're troubleshooting an old building network and the wall jacks don't match your patch cables, T568A vs T568B mismatch is a common culprit
  • New construction following ISO/IEC 11801 international standard, which favors T568A
⚠️
MIXING A AND B WITHIN AN INSTALLATION IS FINE — as long as both ends of each cable are the same standard. A T568A wall jack connected by a T568A-terminated cable to a T568A patch panel works perfectly. The problem arises only when one end of a cable is A and the other is B — which is exactly what a crossover cable is (by design), and exactly what an accidental mispinning is (a wiring error).

✂ CROSSOVER CABLES — A SOLVED PROBLEM YOU SHOULD STILL KNOW

A crossover cable has T568B on one end and T568A on the other. Because the only difference between A and B is swapping the orange and green pairs — which correspond to the TX and RX signal pairs respectively — a crossover cable physically swaps the transmit wires on one end to the receive wires on the other.

Why that matters: Standard Ethernet devices have two types of ports:

  • MDI (Medium Dependent Interface) — used on end devices (computers, printers). Pin 1/2 = TX (transmit), pin 3/6 = RX (receive).
  • MDIX (MDI-Crossover) — used on switches and hubs. The internal pinout is already crossed — pin 1/2 = RX, pin 3/6 = TX. This is why a straight-through cable works between a computer (MDI) and a switch (MDIX): the switch internally does what a crossover cable does externally.

Before auto-negotiation, if you wanted to connect two similar devices — computer to computer, or switch to switch — their TX pins would both connect to TX pins on a straight-through cable, and they'd try to transmit to each other rather than receive. A crossover cable fixed this by physically routing one device's TX to the other device's RX.

ℹ️
AUTO-MDIX — WHY CROSSOVER CABLES ARE MOSTLY HISTORY Since IEEE 802.3ab (1000BASE-T, Gigabit Ethernet, 1999), the Auto-MDIX feature was standardized. The hardware automatically detects whether a crossover is needed and switches its internal TX/RX wiring accordingly. Any modern switch, router, computer, or network-capable device made after roughly 2003 supports Auto-MDIX. You can plug a straight-through cable between two computers or two switches and it just works. Crossover cables are essentially a legacy curiosity at this point — but understanding why they existed reveals how Ethernet's TX/RX pairs actually function.

CRIMP CONNECTORS, SPLICES & TERMINATIONS

🔧 WHAT IS CRIMPING?

Crimping is mechanically deforming a metal sleeve (the crimp connector) around a wire to create a gas-tight electrical connection. Done correctly, a crimp deforms both the connector barrel and the wire strands, creating metal-to-metal contact with no air gaps. This prevents oxidation at the contact point — the leading cause of connection degradation over time.

The key to a good crimp:

  • Correct wire gauge for the connector
  • Proper crimper (not pliers — never use pliers for electrical crimp connectors)
  • Correct die for the connector type
  • Wire fully inserted before crimping
  • One crimp per barrel — don't crimp twice in different spots

Pull test: After crimping, grasp the wire firmly and pull. A good crimp should hold 15–20 lbf on 18 AWG wire. If the wire pulls out or the insulation sleeve slides, redo it.

✂️ TYPES OF SPLICES

A splice is any junction where two or more wires are joined together to carry current continuously.

Butt Splice (Crimp): The most common in FRC. Two wire ends inserted from opposite sides of a cylindrical metal barrel and crimped. Use insulated versions or add heat shrink. Available in solder-filled versions that heat shrink and solder simultaneously.

Western Union Splice (Lineman's): Wires stripped, wrapped tightly around each other in opposite directions, then soldered. The mechanical wrap provides strength even if solder fails. Old-school but reliable for fixed installations.

Tap Splice / T-Splice: Branches one wire off a continuous run without cutting the main wire. Used when you need to add a connection mid-run. Insulation displacement connectors (IDC) do this without stripping.

Solder Splice: Wires stripped, tinned, overlapped, and soldered together, then covered with heat shrink. Excellent if done correctly; requires proper technique.

Wire Nut / Marrette: Twist-on plastic cap used in building wiring. NOT appropriate for robot use — they can unscrew from vibration.

⚠️ SPLICE BEST PRACTICES FOR FRC

  • Always cover splices with adhesive-lined heat shrink tubing (waterproof grade)
  • Never leave bare metal exposed — especially in the robot frame where shorts can occur
  • In high-vibration areas, crimp splices are more reliable than solder splices (vibration can crack solder joints)
  • Never splice main power wires (6 AWG battery cables) with butt splices — use proper lugs and inline connectors
  • Document all splices in your wiring diagram — hidden splices cause debugging nightmares
  • Test continuity with a multimeter after every splice before applying power
  • Heat shrink should have 3:1 or 4:1 shrink ratio for complete coverage over connectors

🔥 WHAT IS SOLDER?

Solder is a fusible metal alloy that melts at relatively low temperatures and bonds to metals, creating an electrical and mechanical connection. It is not a glue — it forms a metallurgical bond with the base metals (copper wire, PCB pad) through intermetallic compound formation.

🧪 WHAT IS FLUX?

Flux is a chemical cleaning agent that removes the oxide layer from metal surfaces and prevents re-oxidation during soldering. Without flux, solder beads up and doesn't wet the surface — it won't bond properly.

Types of flux:

  • Rosin (R, RMA, RA): Natural pine resin. Most common. R = non-activating (mildest), RMA = mildly activated, RA = fully activated (most aggressive). Residue is non-conductive but should be cleaned for inspection.
  • No-clean flux: Leaves minimal residue that doesn't need cleaning. Not truly "no need to clean" — just "no need for electrical reasons." May still need cleaning for conformal coating.
  • Water-soluble flux: Very aggressive, extremely active cleaning. Must be cleaned completely with DI water — residue is conductive and corrosive.

FRC recommended: No-clean rosin-core solder + liquid or paste no-clean flux for additional joints. Do not use water-soluble flux on robots unless you have a proper cleaning setup.

✅ WHAT MAKES A GOOD SOLDER JOINT?

Visual characteristics of a GOOD joint:

  • Smooth, shiny surface (lead solder) or slightly matte but smooth (lead-free)
  • Concave meniscus — solder wets up the wire and feathers to a thin edge on the pad
  • Clearly shows the shape of the wire/component under it
  • No excess solder blobs or bridging to adjacent pads
  • Wire/component does not move during or after cooling

Visual characteristics of a BAD joint:

  • Cold joint: Dull, grainy, lumpy appearance. Solder did not flow properly — usually caused by insufficient heat or moving the joint while cooling.
  • Dry joint: Looks like solder just sits on surface without wetting. No metallurgical bond.
  • Bridging: Solder connects two pads that should be separate. Causes shorts.
  • Blob/excess: Too much solder, possibly hiding a cold joint underneath.
  • Tombstoned SMD component: Surface-mount component stood up on one end during reflow.

🔧 HOW WAGO / LEVER NUTS WORK

  • Re-usable — can open and remove wire without damage
  • Accepts solid or stranded wire (some models require ferrules for fine stranded)

⚡ HOW FUSES WORK

A fuse is a sacrificial overcurrent protection device. It contains a metal element (alloy chosen for its melting point) in series with the circuit. When current exceeds the rated value, the element heats up (P = I²R) and melts, opening the circuit.

Fuses are: Single-use (must be replaced after blowing), very fast-acting, available in many forms (glass tube, blade, ATC, maxi, MIDI, ANL).

Advantage: Very fast clearing time — some fuses open in microseconds, protecting expensive electronics from current spikes.

FRC Use: MIDI fuses (older PDP for main bus), inline fuses for accessories, 20A/30A blade fuses for some systems. The older CTRE PDP used 40A snap-action circuit breakers on motor channels.

🔄 BIMETALLIC (THERMAL) BREAKERS

A circuit breaker is a resettable overcurrent protection device. The most common type uses a bimetallic strip — two metals with different thermal expansion rates bonded together.

When excessive current flows, the strip heats up. Because the two metals expand at different rates, the strip bends, tripping a mechanical latch and opening the contacts. Once cooled, the breaker can be reset manually.

The Snap Action breakers on the PDH/PDP work this way. Critically: they are thermally dependent — if the breaker is already warm (ambient temp, previous trip), it will trip at a lower current. This is why robots can "thermally derate" during long or intense matches.

The 120A main breaker is also a thermal/magnetic breaker — magnetic trip for instantaneous overcurrents (short circuits), thermal trip for sustained overloads.

♻️ SELF-RESETTING (PTC) FUSES

PTC = Positive Temperature Coefficient thermistor used as a resettable fuse (also called "polyfuse" or "polyswitch").

Under normal current, PTC resistance is very low. If current exceeds the hold current, heating causes the PTC polymer to swell, dramatically increasing resistance — effectively opening the circuit. When the fault is removed and the device cools, resistance returns to normal.

Advantages: Resets automatically without replacement, protects circuits that cannot be easily accessed. Used in USB ports, battery protection circuits, computer ports.

Disadvantages: Slower than fuses, have a "hold current" vs "trip current" gap, still pass some current when tripped, cannot handle as much current as proper breakers.

FRC Use: Some VRM (Voltage Regulator Module) outputs have PTC protection. Radio power is often PTC-fused at the VRM/PDH.

🔬 THE MULTIMETER — ESSENTIAL FRC TOOL

A digital multimeter (DMM) is the single most important diagnostic tool on an FRC team. Learn to use every function.

DC Voltage (VDC): Measure battery voltage (should be 12.0–12.9V resting, drops under load). Check 5V rail from VRM. Verify voltage at end of long wire run (should be close to source).

Continuity Mode (🔊 beep): Most valuable for wiring diagnosis. Beep = connected. Probes on two points — if it beeps, current can flow between them. Check: is ground connected all the way? Is there an accidental short between + and −? Is the wire actually making contact in the connector?

Resistance (Ω): Measure resistance of a wire run to check for excess resistance. A good 10 AWG run of 6 feet should be <10mΩ. Measure contact resistance of a connector. Identify partial shorts.

DC Current (A): Requires breaking the circuit to insert the meter in series. Useful for measuring device current draw. Many meters use a clamp-on CT for current without breaking the circuit.

Diode test: Check LEDs (they should light up), verify diode orientation.

CONTINUITY IS YOUR BEST FRIEND — When a device isn't working, before touching code, check continuity from the PDH channel, through the wire, to the device. Most electrical problems are mechanical (bad connection, broken wire, loose terminal).

📡 BONUS: CAN BUS IN FRC

CAN (Controller Area Network) is a differential serial communication protocol used to link motor controllers, PDH, pneumatic hubs, and the RoboRIO on FRC robots. It was designed for automotive use (robust to noise, multi-master).

Physical Layer: Two wires — CAN-H (yellow) and CAN-L (green). The difference between them encodes the data bits. Differential signaling makes it immune to common-mode noise from motor switching.

Topology: A daisy-chain bus (not star), from RoboRIO → Device 1 → Device 2 → … → last device. The last device must have a 120Ω termination resistor across CAN-H and CAN-L. The RoboRIO has an internal 120Ω terminator. Without proper termination, reflections cause communication errors.

CAN BUS — DEEP DIVE
PROTOCOL · FRAMES · TOPOLOGY · TERMINATION · TROUBLESHOOTING · FRC DEVICES

📡 WHAT IS CAN BUS?

CAN (Controller Area Network) was developed by Bosch in 1983 and first appeared in production cars in 1991. It was designed specifically for environments with severe electrical noise — a moving vehicle with motors, ignition coils, and alternators. That's exactly why it's perfect for robots.

CAN is a multi-master, message-based protocol. Any device on the bus can transmit at any time; if two transmit simultaneously, the one with the lower (higher-priority) ID wins via bitwise arbitration — the losers back off and retry automatically. No device needs to ask "can I talk?" like older protocols.

Unlike point-to-point protocols (UART, SPI, I²C over long distances), all CAN devices share the same two wires — CAN-High (CAN-H) and CAN-Low (CAN-L). A message sent by one device is received by every device on the bus simultaneously. Devices filter messages by ID to decide if they should act on them.

FRC uses CAN 2.0B — the extended frame format supporting 29-bit message IDs at 1 Mbps. REV and CTRE devices all communicate over this standard.

⚡ CAN DIFFERENTIAL SIGNALING

CAN uses differential signaling — the same principle as twisted-pair Ethernet. Rather than measuring voltage on one wire relative to ground, the receiver measures the difference between CAN-H and CAN-L.

Two states exist on the bus:

  • Recessive (logic 1): Both CAN-H and CAN-L are at the same voltage (~2.5V). The difference = 0V. This is the "idle" state of the bus.
  • Dominant (logic 0): CAN-H is driven to ~3.5V, CAN-L to ~1.5V. Difference = 2.0V. Any device can assert a dominant bit; only all devices being silent produces recessive.

Because the receiver only cares about the difference, any noise that affects both wires equally (common-mode noise from a nearby motor controller switching at 20kHz) cancels perfectly. This makes CAN extremely robust in a robot environment.

🔗 CAN BUS TOPOLOGY — THE DAISY CHAIN

CAN requires a linear bus topology — also called a daisy chain. This is not a star, not a ring, not a tree. Deviations from a linear topology cause signal reflections that corrupt data, especially at 1 Mbps.

Correct linear topology:

RoboRIO ──[Y/G]──> TalonFX #1 ──[Y/G]──> TalonFX #2 ──[Y/G]──> SparkMax #1 ──[120Ω]
RoboRIO has internal 120Ω termination at one end.
Last device has 120Ω resistor across CAN-H and CAN-L at the other end.

What to avoid:

  • Star topology: One central hub with multiple branches. Creates reflections at every branch point. Never do this.
  • Long stubs: A "stub" is a short branch off the main line. Keep stubs <30cm (12"). REV and CTRE passthrough connectors help minimize stubs.
  • Missing terminator: Without 120Ω at the far end, signal reflections bounce back and corrupt frames, causing sporadic errors.
  • Too many splices: Each splice adds impedance discontinuity. Route the bus cleanly through each device's passthrough connector.

How to verify termination with a multimeter:

ℹ️
MULTIMETER TERMINATION CHECK
With robot power OFF, place probes across CAN-H and CAN-L anywhere on the bus. A correctly terminated bus reads ~60Ω (two 120Ω resistors in parallel). Too high = missing terminator. Open circuit = broken bus. Too low = short or extra terminator. This is the fastest way to verify CAN termination before even turning on the robot.

🔥 WHAT IS HEAT SHRINK TUBING?

Heat shrink tubing is a polymer tube that, when heated to its activation temperature, shrinks radially (in diameter) to conform tightly around whatever is inside it. It does not shrink significantly in length. The shrinking is caused by cross-linking in the polymer during manufacturing — when heated, the memory of the cross-linked structure pulls the tube back to a smaller diameter.

Activation temp: Typically 90–120°C (194–248°F) for standard polyolefin. Use a heat gun, not an open flame — flames cause uneven heating, scorching, and can ignite the insulation.

The "shrink ratio" tells you how much smaller the tube gets. A 2:1 tube shrinks to half its original inner diameter. A 3:1 tube shrinks to one-third. Always choose tubing that fits loosely over the un-shrunk connection and shrinks down tightly over it.

Sizing rule of thumb: Choose a pre-shrink inner diameter at least 20–30% larger than the largest part of the connection. After shrinking, it should grip firmly with no gaps.

  • Always slide heat shrink on BEFORE making the connection — you can't put it on after you've crimped or soldered the end. This is the #1 beginner mistake.

  • Start shrinking from the middle out to avoid trapping air bubbles.
  • For connectors, shrink from the wire end toward the connector body, allowing air to escape from the open connector end.

  • Use adhesive-lined heat shrink on all battery connections, Anderson contacts, and any outdoor-facing connection.
  • Match colors to wire color: red heat shrink on positive, black on negative — this maintains your color coding through the splice.

🧵 WIRE MANAGEMENT: SLEEVING & LOOM

Protective sleeving organizes and protects wire bundles:

Braided expandable sleeving (techflex): Flexible woven sleeve that expands to accept wires. Excellent for robot wiring harnesses — allows branching at any point and is re-openable. No tools to apply. Does not provide waterproofing.

Split loom / conduit: Corrugated plastic tube with a split down the side. Snap wires in anywhere. Very abrasion-resistant. Common in automotive wiring. Good for protecting wires running along robot frame rails.

Spiral wrap: Plastic spiral that winds around a bundle. Flexible, allows easy branch-outs, re-openable. Less protective than loom but easier to route.

Cable ties (zip ties): Secure bundles at regular intervals (every 150–200mm) to prevent sagging, chafing, and catching on robot mechanisms. Use UV-resistant black for durability. Always cut the tail flush — sharp zip tie tails cut fingers and skin.

⚠️ CHAFE & ABRASION PROTECTION

Moving robots chafe wires against frame members, gears, and other moving parts. A chafed wire will eventually short — usually at the worst possible moment.

Prevention strategies:

  • Route wires away from all moving mechanisms with at least 25mm clearance
  • Use grommets wherever wires pass through metal holes — sharp metal edges cut insulation over time
  • Secure wires so they cannot swing into moving parts
  • Use cable clamps (not zip ties alone) to anchor wires that carry significant tension from moving assemblies
  • Where contact is unavoidable, use abrasion-resistant sleeving (nylon braid or split loom) over that section
  • Add a service loop: a small intentional loop of extra wire at moving joints to allow motion without tension on the conductor
🚨 INSPECT ALL WIRING AFTER EVERY MATCH — Check for chafed, pinched, or loose wiring. Many match-ending failures start as mechanical wire damage that got worse over multiple matches.

📐 SCHEMATIC DIAGRAMS vs WIRING DIAGRAMS

These two terms are often confused — they show the same system but in fundamentally different ways:

Schematic (circuit diagram): Uses standardized symbols to show the electrical relationships between components. Does not show physical location, actual wire routing, or connector types. Everything is abstracted. Lines = electrical connections. Used for design, analysis, and understanding how a circuit works.

Wiring diagram: Shows the physical connections between real components — connector types, wire colors, routing paths, and physical locations. Used by technicians for assembly and troubleshooting. Less useful for understanding circuit theory, more useful for actually building it.

For FRC: You need both — a schematic to understand and design your electrical system, and a wiring diagram (or at minimum a labeled harness map) to document what's actually on the robot. The FRC wiring documentation template (available on firstinspires.org) is a good starting wiring diagram format.

📋 HOW TO READ A WIRING DIAGRAM — STEP BY STEP

Step 1 — Find the power source. Every diagram starts with power. Identify the battery (or power supply), the positive terminal, and the ground. Follow the positive line to find the first protection device (main breaker/fuse).

Step 2 — Trace the power distribution. Follow from the breaker/fuse to the distribution point (PDH/PDP). Note how power splits to different circuits. Each branch has its own protection device — identify each one's rating.

Step 3 — Follow each branch circuit. Pick one branch (e.g., drive motor 1). Follow the + wire from the PDH through its breaker, through the motor controller, to the motor. Follow the − wire back through the motor controller to the PDH ground. This complete loop is one circuit.

Step 4 — Identify signal paths. Now look for the thin/low-current signal wires. PWM signal from RoboRIO to motor controller. CAN bus chain. Sensor wires. These are separate from the power circuit but control it.

Step 5 — Check for ground connections. All grounds should ultimately connect back to battery negative. Check that sensor grounds, motor controller grounds, and logic grounds all share a common reference. Floating grounds cause mysterious behavior.

Step 6 — Identify connector types. A good wiring diagram labels each connector. If you see "AP45" you know it's a 45A Anderson Powerpole. "JST-PH 3-pin" tells you exactly what connector and tool you need.

  • Connector type at each junction
  • PDH channel assignments (what's on channel 1, 2, 3...)
  • CAN device IDs and bus topology order
  • Breaker rating for each PDH channel

ELECTRICAL DIAGNOSIS
SYSTEMATIC TROUBLESHOOTING · MULTIMETER TECHNIQUES · COMMON FRC FAILURES · BROWNOUTS
💡
THE GOLDEN RULE OF ELECTRICAL DIAGNOSIS Most electrical failures are mechanical — a loose connector, a broken wire, a bad crimp, a frayed strand touching something it shouldn't. Before suspecting software, sensors, or component failure: check the physical connections. 80% of robot electrical problems are found by wiggling connectors and looking at wire routing.

🔬 SYSTEMATIC TROUBLESHOOTING — THE PROCESS

1. Define the symptom precisely. "The robot doesn't work" is useless. "The left drive motor doesn't respond to joystick input but the DriverStation shows enabled" is specific. The more precise your symptom description, the faster you'll find the cause.

2. Identify what's working and what's not. Does anything on the robot work? Is the RoboRIO communicating? Are CAN devices appearing? Is the fault on one device or everything? Isolation is key.

3. Eliminate variables systematically. Work from power source outward: Battery → Main Breaker → PDH → Branch circuit → Motor Controller → Motor. At each stage, verify the expected voltage is present before moving forward.

4. Swap and compare. If you have a second working robot or spare components, swap suspect components to confirm failure. This is faster than trying to measure a controller's internal state.

5. Use the tools. Multimeter for voltage and continuity. DriverStation for system status. Phoenix Tuner / REV Hardware Client for CAN device health. RoboRIO web dashboard for system diagnostics.

6. Document what you find. When you find and fix a failure, write it down. Recurring failures are a pattern — patterns indicate a design problem that needs a permanent fix, not just repeated repairs.

⚡ VOLTAGE MEASUREMENT TECHNIQUE

How to check if a device is getting power:

Set your multimeter to DC Volts (20V range or auto). Black probe to battery negative / chassis ground. Red probe to the positive input terminal of the device being tested. You should see ~12V (will vary based on battery state and load).

Voltage drop test: Measure voltage at the battery terminals, then measure voltage at the device. The difference is the voltage drop across the wiring and connections between them. Under no-load, this should be less than 0.1V. Under full load, up to 0.5V is acceptable; more indicates excessive resistance in the power path.

Voltage divider / sensor output: Analog sensors output 0–5V or 0–3.3V. Measure between signal wire and sensor ground. If reading is stuck at 0V, check power to sensor and sensor ground. Stuck at max voltage might indicate sensor is disconnected from signal (pull-up resistor dominating).

Vdrop = Vbattery − Vdevice
Keep this < 0.5V under full load for good wiring health

🚨 UNDERSTANDING BROWNOUTS — THE FRC NEMESIS

A brownout occurs when battery voltage drops so low (~6.8V on the RoboRIO input) that the RoboRIO's power supply can no longer maintain stable 5V or 3.3V rails. When this happens, the RoboRIO forces all outputs to 0 — motors go neutral, solenoids release — as a protective measure. The robot goes "limp" for a moment even though it stays enabled.

Brownout causes:

  • Simultaneous stall of multiple drive motors (e.g., pushing hard against a wall)
  • Shooting mechanism spinning up under full load
  • Weak or partially discharged battery
  • High resistance in main power path (corroded Anderson connector, undersized battery cable)
  • Too many high-current devices running at once

Diagnosing brownouts:

  • DriverStation logs show voltage vs time — look for voltage dips below 7V
  • "ROBOT VOLTAGE" in DriverStation drops sharply at the moment of brownout
  • RoboRIO has a brownout LED/indicator
  • Review PDH current data (available via CAN logging) to identify which channel pulled the most current

Solutions:

  • Use a fresh, fully charged battery
  • Check and replace corroded Anderson SB50 connectors
  • Implement software current limiting on motor controllers
  • Ramp motor setpoints gradually instead of jumping to 100%
  • Stagger startup of multiple high-current devices
  • Verify main power wiring is 6 AWG with good connections

HOW BATTERIES WORK

⚗️ WHAT IS A BATTERY? (THE CHEMISTRY)

A battery is an electrochemical device that converts stored chemical energy into electrical energy through a controlled chemical reaction. The key insight: electrons don't travel through the electrolyte — they travel through your external circuit (the robot). Ions travel inside the battery to complete the circuit.

Every battery consists of one or more electrochemical cells, each containing:

  • Anode (negative electrode): The material that is oxidized (gives up electrons) during discharge. In lead-acid: sponge lead (Pb).
  • Cathode (positive electrode): The material that is reduced (accepts electrons) during discharge. In lead-acid: lead dioxide (PbO₂).
  • Electrolyte: An ion-conducting medium between electrodes. In lead-acid: sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) diluted in water. Ions move through this; electrons cannot.
  • Separator: A porous physical barrier that prevents the anode and cathode from touching (short circuit) while allowing ions to pass.

The difference in chemical potential between anode and cathode materials determines the cell voltage. For lead-acid, each cell produces approximately 2.1V at rest.

⚡ THE LEAD-ACID DISCHARGE REACTION

When you connect a load (close the circuit), spontaneous chemical reactions occur at both electrodes simultaneously:

Anode (−): Pb + SO₄²⁻ → PbSO₄ + 2e⁻
Lead is oxidized — releases electrons into the external circuit. Lead sulfate (PbSO₄) forms on the anode plate.
Cathode (+): PbO₂ + 4H⁺ + SO₄²⁻ + 2e⁻ → PbSO₄ + 2H₂O
Lead dioxide is reduced — accepts electrons from the external circuit. Also forms PbSO₄ and water.
Overall: Pb + PbO₂ + 2H₂SO₄ → 2PbSO₄ + 2H₂O
Both plates become PbSO₄. Electrolyte becomes more dilute (H₂SO₄ is consumed). This is discharge.

On charging, the reaction reverses: The charger forces electrons backward, converting PbSO₄ back to Pb (anode) and PbO₂ (cathode), and regenerating H₂SO₄ in the electrolyte. This is why lead-acid is rechargeable — the reaction is highly reversible.

Why batteries "die" permanently: If discharged too deeply or left discharged too long, PbSO₄ crystals harden and grow — called sulfation. Hardened sulfate cannot be converted back by normal charging. This permanently reduces capacity and increases internal resistance.

📦 AGM vs FLOODED LEAD-ACID

The FRC battery is an AGM (Absorbed Glass Mat) type, not a traditional flooded lead-acid battery.

Flooded (wet cell): Plates submerged in liquid sulfuric acid. Can spill if tipped. Vents hydrogen gas (explosive). Requires periodic water topping. Common in car starter batteries.

AGM (FRC standard): The sulfuric acid electrolyte is absorbed and held in a fiberglass mat separator — there is no free liquid. This means:

  • Can operate in any orientation without spilling
  • Sealed — no acid spill hazard
  • Pressure-relief valve handles any gas build-up
  • Recombines evolved hydrogen and oxygen back to water internally (valve-regulated)
  • Lower self-discharge rate than flooded
  • Higher vibration resistance — critical for robots

AGM batteries also accept higher charge currents than flooded types, making them faster to recharge between matches.

⚡ CAPACITY: Ah, C-RATE, AND REALITY

The FRC battery is rated 18 Ah — but what does that actually mean?

Ampere-hours (Ah) is energy capacity. An 18 Ah battery can theoretically deliver 18A for 1 hour, or 1A for 18 hours, or 9A for 2 hours — before being fully discharged.

C-rate is how fast you draw that capacity. "C20" means discharging over 20 hours (18Ah ÷ 20hr = 0.9A). The 18Ah rating is at the C20 rate — extremely slow.

The FRC reality is harsh: A robot drawing 40A average during a 150-second match consumes 1.67 Ah per match. But because it's discharged so quickly (high C-rate), lead-acid batteries deliver less capacity under high loads. At a 40A draw (≈2.2C), effective capacity drops to perhaps 10–12 Ah — and voltage sags significantly.

Peukert's Law: Lead-acid capacity decreases under higher discharge rates. This is why brownouts happen when multiple motors stall simultaneously — the battery can't deliver that current without significant voltage drop.

Vterminal = VOC − (I × Rinternal)
At 100A load with 20mΩ internal resistance: V = 12.6 − (100 × 0.020) = 10.6V

🔋 BATTERY CARE & FAILURE MODES

Sulfation (most common failure): Leaving a battery deeply discharged allows PbSO₄ to crystallize permanently. Always charge immediately after use. Never store a discharged battery.

Over-discharge: Discharging below 10.5V damages plates. The RoboRIO brownout circuit protects electronics but doesn't prevent battery damage if the robot is run too long on a weak battery.

Overcharging: Charging above 14.8V on a sealed AGM battery causes electrolysis of water, producing hydrogen and oxygen gas that vents out. Repeated overcharging dries out the AGM mat and destroys the battery. Always use a smart charger rated for AGM chemistry.

Grid corrosion: Age and heat corrode the lead alloy grid structure of the plates, increasing internal resistance even without sulfation.

Signs of a failing battery:

  • Resting voltage below 12.0V even after full charge
  • Voltage collapses quickly under even moderate load
  • Internal resistance >50 mΩ on Battery Beak or equivalent tester
  • Battery runs warm during charging at normal rates
  • Case bulging (gas pressure building up internally)
  • Robot brownouts that didn't occur with a fresh battery
🚨 REPLACE, DON'T RISK IT — A failing battery at competition causes robot brownouts, inconsistent performance, and potential match losses. Batteries typically last 3–4 seasons of FRC competition use. When in doubt, replace it.

🧱 THE SEPARATOR — HOW IT BLOCKS ELECTRONS BUT PASSES IONS

The separator is one of the most elegant components in a battery. Its job is seemingly impossible: allow ions to pass freely while completely blocking electrons. Here's how it works:

Physical structure of an AGM separator: In an FRC AGM battery, the separator is a mat of ultrafine borosilicate glass fibers — essentially a sponge with pores just a few micrometers across. The sulfuric acid electrolyte is held within these pores by capillary action (surface tension). The mat physically separates the lead anode plate from the lead dioxide cathode plate, preventing them from touching and short-circuiting.

Why ions pass through: Ions (H⁺, SO₄²⁻) are charged atoms/molecules dissolved in the liquid acid. They move through the liquid electrolyte that saturates the separator's pores, carried by chemical potential gradients and electric field forces. The liquid-filled pores provide a continuous ionic path from one electrode to the other.

Why electrons cannot pass through: Electrons cannot travel through liquid water or acid — they require a metallic conductor (a continuous lattice of atoms sharing a free-electron sea). The glass fiber separator and the aqueous electrolyte within it have no such metallic lattice. Any electron that tried to enter the electrolyte would immediately be captured by a water molecule or ion. The only path for electron flow is through the external circuit — your robot's wiring.

The bandgap explanation: More precisely, liquid water has a very large electronic bandgap — the energy required to free an electron from a water molecule is far greater than what battery voltages can provide. So electrons simply cannot enter the electrolyte medium. Meanwhile, ions (entire charged atoms) are already dissolved in the electrolyte and move freely through it.

This electron-blocking property is fundamental: It's what forces current to flow through the external circuit (your robot). If electrons could flow through the electrolyte, the battery would immediately short itself internally and discharge all at once, converting all energy to heat. The separator's blocking of electrons is what makes batteries useful.

External circuit: e⁻ flow (wire) → does work → returns
Internal (electrolyte): ion flow completes the circuit
Electrode surface: chemical ↔ electrical handoff
ℹ️
IN SHORT: The separator is porous to the medium (liquid acid) in which ions are dissolved, but is a solid insulator to direct electron flow. Ions move through liquid; electrons move through metal. The separator provides one but not the other.
CELLS vs BATTERIES — SERIES & PARALLEL CONFIGURATIONS

🔋 CELL vs BATTERY — THE DISTINCTION

A cell is a single electrochemical unit — one anode, one cathode, one electrolyte, producing one fixed voltage determined by its chemistry. A battery is technically a collection of cells arranged to achieve desired voltage and capacity.

In common usage, "battery" and "cell" are used interchangeably, but the distinction matters when designing power systems. The FRC 12V battery contains 6 lead-acid cells in series. Your phone's "battery" is a single lithium-ion cell. A Tesla battery pack contains thousands of individual 18650 or 4680 cells.

Cell voltage is determined by chemistry alone: Lead-acid = ~2.1V/cell, NiMH = ~1.2V/cell, LiCoO₂ = ~3.6V/cell, LiFePO₄ = ~3.2V/cell. You cannot change this by design — it's physics. To get higher voltage, you wire cells in series. To get higher capacity, you wire cells in parallel.

➕ SERIES vs ⊞ PARALLEL WIRING

Series (S) — adds voltage: Connect + of one cell to − of the next. Total voltage = sum of all cell voltages. Capacity stays the same as one cell.

Vtotal = n × Vcell
Capacity unchanged

FRC battery: 6S1P → 6 × 2.1V = 12.6V, 18Ah

A 4S LiPo: 4 × 3.7V = 14.8V

Parallel (P) — adds capacity: Connect all + together, all − together. Voltage stays the same as one cell. Capacity multiplies.

Ahtotal = n × Ahcell
Voltage unchanged

2× 18Ah in parallel = 36Ah at same voltage

C-RATE, Ah vs Wh, AND VOLTAGE SAG IN FRC

⏱ C-RATE — THE MISSING CONTEXT FOR Ah RATINGS

The C-rate describes how quickly a battery is charged or discharged relative to its capacity. A rate of 1C means discharging the entire capacity in 1 hour. 0.5C means 2 hours. 10C means 6 minutes.

Current (A) = C-rate × Capacity (Ah)
For an 18Ah battery: 1C = 18A, 5C = 90A, 0.05C = 0.9A

Why the C20 (0.05C) rating on the FRC battery? The 18Ah rating is measured by discharging at 0.9A over 20 hours. This is extremely gentle. Lead-acid batteries suffer from Peukert's effect — under higher discharge rates, internal reactions can't keep up, and you get less usable energy out.

🔋 Ah vs Wh — WHY FRC USES AMP-HOURS

Energy is properly measured in Watt-hours (Wh) — it accounts for both voltage and current (Wh = V × Ah). But for FRC batteries, we almost always talk in amp-hours. Why?

Watt-hours is the right unit when voltage varies widely (comparing a 3.7V LiPo to a 12V SLA). But in FRC, everything operates from the same 12V battery. The voltage does change — it drops from ~12.7V fully charged to ~10.5V depleted — but within a single match, it's approximately constant. When comparing batteries of the same nominal voltage, Ah is proportional to Wh and is simpler to reason about operationally.

More practically: current is what trips breakers and stalls motors, not power. A motor controller cares about amps. A PDH breaker trips on amps. When you're asking "can this battery sustain this motor during a match?", you're asking about current capacity, not watt-hours. So Ah is the operationally relevant unit for FRC teams.

Wh = Ah × Vnominal
FRC battery: 18 Ah × 12V = 216 Wh (theoretical rated energy)

📉 VOLTAGE SAG — THE FRC NEMESIS, IN DETAIL

What is voltage sag? Every real battery has internal resistance (Rint). When current flows, that internal resistance causes a voltage drop inside the battery itself, reducing the terminal voltage available to the robot:

Vterminal = VOCV − (I × Rint)
VOCV = open-circuit voltage (resting)  |  I = current drawn  |  Rint = internal resistance

Real FRC example: Battery resting at 12.6V with Rint = 20mΩ. Six Kraken X60 drive motors each drawing 30A = 180A total draw.

Vterminal = 12.6 − (180 × 0.020) = 12.6 − 3.6 = 9.0V

At 9V, the RoboRIO input voltage has sagged below 8V after the distribution wiring, and a brownout occurs. Worse: a worn battery with Rint = 60mΩ sags to just 1.8V at the same draw — essentially dead.

Voltage sag is not the same as state of charge. A fully charged battery with high internal resistance sags more than a partially charged healthy battery. This is why internal resistance testing (Battery Beak / CBA) is more useful than a voltage test for competition battery selection.

Sources of voltage sag in the FRC robot:

  • Battery internal resistance (dominant for old/damaged batteries) — increases with age, sulfation, temperature
  • Anderson SB50 contact resistance — corroded or arced contacts can add 10–50mΩ
  • 6 AWG main power cables — typically 3–5mΩ per foot. At 6 feet round-trip = ~30mΩ
  • PDH bus bar resistance — minimal but non-zero
  • Motor controller output stage resistance — a few mΩ per controller
  • Motor winding resistance — becomes significant at stall

How to minimize voltage sag:

  • Use fresh, low-resistance batteries (<15mΩ)
  • Keep main power cable runs as short as possible
  • Inspect and replace corroded Anderson connectors
  • Implement current limits in motor controller code
  • Use software ramp rates — avoid instantaneous 0→100% commands
  • Stagger high-current startup sequences
  • Never run multiple subsystems at stall simultaneously (drive + shooter + intake at full stall = disaster)
🤖 KRAKEN X60 IN FRC: Modern FRC drivetrains use 4 Kraken X60s for drive (4× TalonFX motor controllers, 6000+ RPM free speed). In a swerve drive, add 4 more Krakens or Kraken X44s for steer (the X44 is a smaller, lower-power variant). Each Kraken X60 stalls at ~366A. Even with current limiting, 4-8 active Krakens represent a massive instantaneous current demand. Voltage sag management is a real design constraint for competitive swerve robots.

⚠ WHY SHORTING A BATTERY IS EXTREMELY DANGEROUS

💥 THE PHYSICS OF A SHORT CIRCUIT

A short circuit is simply a path of near-zero resistance connecting a battery's terminals. The math makes the danger obvious. From Ohm's Law:

Ishort = VOCV ÷ Rtotal
Rtotal = Rinternal + Rshort path
FRC battery: V = 12.6V, Rint = 15mΩ, short via screwdriver (≈1mΩ):
I = 12.6 ÷ 0.016 = 787 AMPS

Now apply the heating law — P = I²R:

Pbattery = 787² × 0.015 = 9,284 W
In the battery alone: 9.3 kilowatts — the equivalent of 15 electric kettles boiling simultaneously, inside a sealed lead-acid case.
Pscrewdriver = 787² × 0.001 = 619 W
A metal screwdriver blade would reach incandescence and weld itself to the terminals in milliseconds.

What actually happens in a short circuit:

  • Metal conductors glow red/white-hot and may vaporize or catch fire
  • The battery case temperature spikes — electrolyte boils, generating hydrogen gas
  • Pressure builds inside the sealed AGM case; the relief valve may vent acid vapor
  • In severe shorts, the case can rupture, spraying hot dilute sulfuric acid
  • Sparks at the point of contact can ignite hydrogen gas — explosive
  • Even a brief short before breaking the connection can destroy the battery permanently

The only thing limiting the current is the battery's internal resistance — which is why high-quality, low-resistance batteries are more dangerous when shorted. A healthy 15mΩ FRC battery is more dangerous to short than a degraded 60mΩ one.

🚨
SAFE BATTERY HANDLING — CORRECT CONNECTION ORDER

Connecting: Positive (+) first, Negative (−) last.  Disconnecting: Negative (−) first, Positive (+) last.

Why this order? Before the negative is connected, the circuit is open — no current can flow regardless of what the positive terminal accidentally touches. Adding the negative as the very last step completes the circuit, so it is the highest-risk moment. At that instant, your positive terminal is live. If any conductive tool bridges from the positive terminal (or anything connected to it) to the now-grounded robot frame, the circuit is complete and you have a short.

The same logic in reverse explains removing negative first: breaking the negative removes the return path, so the positive terminal is no longer dangerous even if it contacts the frame.

Practical rules: Use insulated tools. Keep unused terminals covered with tape or terminal boots. Never set a battery where a dropped wrench could bridge both terminals. Make your final connection deliberately — do not rush the negative.

THE MULTIMETER — WHY "MULTI"?

📏 ONE TOOL, MANY MEASUREMENTS

A multimeter earns its name because it replaces what were historically several separate instruments: the voltmeter, the ammeter, and the ohmmeter. Early electrical work required three distinct instruments in three different housings. Around the 1920s, manufacturers realized these could share a single movement and display if you added a mode selector switch. The "multi-meter" was born.

Modern digital multimeters (DMMs) go further, often measuring: DC voltage, AC voltage, DC current, AC current, resistance, continuity (with audible beep), diode forward voltage, capacitance, frequency, duty cycle, temperature (with thermocouple probe), and even transistor gain (hFE). The better the meter, the more it can do — but even a $15 meter handles the FRC essentials.

The central component — historically: Early analog meters used a moving coil galvanometer: a coil of wire suspended in a magnetic field, with a needle attached. Current through the coil creates a magnetic force that deflects the needle proportionally. Adding resistors (for voltage), shunts (for current), and an internal battery with bridge circuit (for resistance) created the multimeter. Digital meters replaced the galvanometer with an analog-to-digital converter (ADC) but the underlying measurement principles are identical.

✅ MEASURING VOLTAGE — ALWAYS SAFE

A voltmeter is placed in parallel (across) the component or source being measured. It has a very high input impedance — typically 10 MΩ on a standard DMM.

Imeter = V ÷ Rinput
12V ÷ 10,000,000Ω = 1.2 microamps
The meter draws essentially zero current from the source.

Because the meter draws negligible current, it doesn't disturb the circuit and can't damage the battery. This is called a high-impedance or non-loading measurement.

What you're actually reading: When measuring a battery at rest, you read the open-circuit voltage (OCV) — the battery's chemical potential with no significant load. A fully charged FRC battery reads 12.7–12.9V. This is NOT the voltage you'll see during a match under load (voltage sag reduces it).

AC voltage: The meter's internal ADC samples the AC waveform thousands of times per second. A True RMS meter correctly computes the root-mean-square of the waveform regardless of shape. A basic "average-responding" meter only reads correctly on pure sine waves — it gives wrong answers on the PWM-shaped waveforms in motor circuits. For FRC diagnostic work, True RMS matters when measuring at motor controller outputs.

⚠️ MEASURING CURRENT — DANGEROUS IF MISUSED

An ammeter is placed in series with the circuit — you break the circuit and insert the meter in the gap so all current flows through it. It has extremely low internal resistance (a precision shunt resistor, typically milliohms) to minimize voltage drop across the meter.

Why the low resistance is dangerous if misused: If you accidentally connect an ammeter in parallel across a battery (the same way you'd use a voltmeter), the meter's milliohm shunt becomes a near-short circuit.

I = 12V ÷ 0.001Ω = 12,000 A
The meter's shunt, leads, and internal fuse see the full short-circuit current of the battery.

The result: the meter's internal fuse blows instantaneously (if the meter has one rated appropriately), the probe leads may arc and melt, and the meter itself may be destroyed. FRC batteries can deliver enough current to vaporize meter probe wires.

The meter's protection fuse: Most quality DMMs have a fuse protecting the current input — often 200mA on the mA port and 10A or 20A on the A port. These fuses are fast-blow types, but even a fast-blow fuse takes a finite time to open — enough for a momentary arc. Always check that your meter fuse is intact before trusting current readings. Replacement fuses must match the original specs exactly.

Safe current measurement in FRC: Use a clamp meter (Hall-effect current sensor that clips around a wire without breaking the circuit) for high-current measurements. The DriverStation and motor controller CAN telemetry report current directly — use those tools instead of breaking power circuits.

🚫 MEASURING RESISTANCE — NEVER ON LIVE CIRCUITS

An ohmmeter works by injecting a small known current from the meter's own internal supply (typically a 9V battery), measuring the resulting voltage drop across the unknown resistance, and applying Ohm's Law internally: R = V/I.

Runknown = Vmeter measures ÷ Imeter injects
The meter's test current is typically 0.1 to 1 mA
The meter's test voltage is 0.3 to 3V depending on range

What happens when you connect an ohmmeter to a live battery:

The battery's own voltage (12.6V) is orders of magnitude larger than the meter's 0.3–3V test signal. The ADC inside the meter sees a large external voltage completely dominating its tiny test signal. The measurement is entirely meaningless — you might read near-zero (the battery looks like a good conductor to the meter's tiny signal), you might get an error code, or you might get a random number. Additionally, the large external voltage can damage the meter's measurement circuitry or blow the ohmmeter protection components.

The rule is absolute: always de-energize and discharge capacitors before measuring resistance. For wiring continuity on the robot, disconnect the battery first. Then use continuity mode (the beeper) or resistance mode freely.

Why this matters for batteries specifically: A battery IS a voltage source — you can never turn it off. You can never use a standard ohmmeter to measure a battery's internal resistance. This seems to create an unsolvable problem... until you understand how the Battery Beak works.

THE BATTERY BEAK — MEASURING WHAT AN OHMMETER CANNOT

🔋 THE CLEVER SOLUTION: USE CURRENT, NOT AN OHMMETER

The Battery Beak (and the West Mountain Radio CBA — Computerized Battery Analyzer) solve the "you can't measure a battery with an ohmmeter" problem elegantly: instead of injecting a test signal, they use the battery's own voltage to drive a known current, then measure the voltage difference.

This is just Ohm's Law applied in a circuit the battery is part of — which is exactly valid, because R_internal is a real resistor in series with the battery's ideal voltage source:

Rint = (VOCV − Vload) ÷ Iload
VOCV = open-circuit voltage (voltmeter reading, no load)
Vload = voltage under a known, controlled load current
Iload = the measured current being drawn

Step-by-step process:

  1. Connect the Battery Beak to the battery terminals (Anderson SB50 connector)
  2. The device measures VOCV with its own high-impedance voltmeter — no current flows yet
  3. It switches in an internal precision load resistor — a known, calibrated resistance — drawing a specific current (typically 10–30A)
  4. It simultaneously measures Vload (the terminal voltage under load) and Iload (the actual current)
  5. It computes: Rint = (VOCV − Vload) / Iload
  6. Displays the result in milliohms

Worked example:

VOCV = 12.65V, Vload = 12.05V at Iload = 30A
Rint = (12.65 − 12.05) ÷ 30 = 0.60 ÷ 30 = 0.020Ω = 20 mΩ
This is a healthy FRC battery. Anything >50mΩ is degraded; replace it.

📊 WEST MOUNTAIN RADIO CBA — DISCHARGE CURVE ANALYSIS

The CBA (Computerized Battery Analyzer) goes further than a simple internal resistance spot check. It performs a full controlled discharge test that reveals the battery's true capacity and health:

  1. Connects to battery and records VOCV
  2. Applies a constant controlled current load (user sets this — typically 5A for a long test, 15–30A for a realistic FRC simulation)
  3. Logs voltage continuously as the battery discharges
  4. Stops at a configurable cutoff voltage (10.5V for FRC SLA)
  5. Plots a voltage vs. capacity (Ah) discharge curve and reports total capacity delivered

What you learn from the discharge curve:

  • A steep initial voltage drop = high internal resistance
  • A flat mid-discharge plateau = healthy cell chemistry
  • An early "knee" where voltage collapses = reduced capacity (sulfation)
  • Total Ah reported vs. rated 18Ah = capacity retention percentage

A battery that reads 12.7V on a voltmeter can fail this test spectacularly — showing only 9Ah of real capacity and collapsing to 10.5V at just 10A load. The discharge test reveals what a voltage reading cannot.

🔄 BRUSHED DC MOTOR

A brushed DC motor has four key components: a permanent magnet stator (the stationary outer shell), an armature (the rotating coil — also called the rotor), a commutator (a segmented copper ring on the shaft), and brushes (carbon/copper pads that press against the commutator).

The brushes make sliding electrical contact with the commutator. As current flows through the armature coil, the magnetic field of the coil interacts with the permanent magnet field, creating a torque force (Lorentz force: F = IL × B). The commutator mechanically switches which coil segments are energized as the rotor turns, always keeping the current in the right direction to sustain rotation.

Advantages: PRO Simple control (just apply voltage) PRO Cheap PRO No position feedback needed

Disadvantages: CON Brushes wear out CON Brushes arc and create EMI CON Limited RPM CON Less efficient than brushless CON Requires periodic maintenance

FRC Examples (legacy): CIM motor, mini-CIM, RS-775 — all brushed DC. Still used for light tasks. Controlled via Talon SRX or Victor SP.

⚡ BRUSHLESS DC (BLDC) MOTORS — HOW THEY ACTUALLY WORK

In a BLDC motor, the roles are reversed from a brushed motor: the permanent magnets are on the rotor (which spins), and the electromagnetic coils are on the stator (which is fixed). There are no brushes or commutator — the electronic speed controller (ESC/motor controller) takes on the job the commutator did mechanically, using electronics to switch which stator coils are energized.

BLDC motors are 3-phase motors: they have three sets of coils (called phases U, V, W or A, B, C), arranged 120° apart. The ESC energizes them in sequence, creating a rotating magnetic field that the permanent magnet rotor chases. Spin the field faster → rotor spins faster.

Why "DC"? The power source is DC (your 12V battery). The motor controller internally converts this to 3-phase AC-like waveforms (actually switched PWM). The name BLDC distinguishes it from traditional AC induction motors and from brushed DC motors — it's powered by DC but commutated electronically to produce 3-phase behavior. The "DC" technically refers to the power supply, not the internal motor phases.

Advantages over brushed:

  • No brushes to wear out — much longer lifespan
  • Higher efficiency (no brush friction loss, no arcing loss)
  • Higher power density — more torque per gram
  • No brush arcing = less EMI
  • Higher RPM capability (no mechanical commutator limit)
  • Better thermal performance (heat in stator, easier to cool)

〰 3-PHASE POWER — WHY THREE PHASES?

Three-phase power delivers power to the motor more smoothly than single-phase. With a single-phase supply, power delivery pulses — it goes through zero twice per cycle (120 times per second for 60Hz AC). With three phases offset by 120°, there's always at least one phase near its peak, so total power delivery is nearly constant.

In an industrial AC grid, 3-phase power comes from actual alternating current generators with three sets of windings 120° apart. In FRC, the motor controller converts DC (battery) into 3-phase using six power MOSFETs in an H-bridge configuration, rapidly switching to synthesize the three phases electronically.

How speed is controlled in BLDC: The motor controller doesn't change the peak voltage to the motor — it changes the frequency and timing of phase switching. Faster phase rotation → faster rotor rotation. This is called Field-Oriented Control (FOC) in advanced controllers like the Kraken's TalonFX.

📍 ROTOR POSITION SENSING — HOW BLDC KNOWS WHERE IT IS

Hall Effect Sensors: Three Hall-effect sensors embedded in the stator, spaced 120° apart. A Hall sensor outputs a voltage when a magnetic field passes through it (the Hall effect: a magnetic field deflects moving electrons sideways in a conductor, creating a transverse voltage). As the rotor's permanent magnets spin past the sensors, each sensor fires a digital pulse. The pattern of three sensor outputs gives 6 unique position states per electrical cycle, which is enough for basic commutation. Simple and robust but coarse resolution (6 states per cycle).

Rotary Encoders: Higher-resolution position sensing. An encoder uses an optical or magnetic disc with fine markings. As the shaft rotates, a sensor counts pulses — typically 2048 pulses per revolution for FRC encoders. This gives precise position and velocity information. The TalonFX in Kraken/Falcon has a built-in 2048 CPR (counts per revolution) magnetic encoder integrated into the motor itself — no external encoder needed. The SparkMAX uses a 42 CPR encoder built into the NEO.

Back-EMF (sensorless): When a BLDC motor spins, it acts as a generator in addition to a motor — it produces a back-electromotive force (back-EMF) on the phases that aren't currently powered. The ESC measures this back-EMF voltage on the floating phase to determine rotor position. This requires the motor to already be spinning (back-EMF is zero at standstill), so sensorless controllers need a startup routine. Popular in RC/drone ESCs where encoder wires would add complexity and weight. FRC motor controllers with built-in encoders are superior — they work from standstill and with closed-loop control.

Absolute encoders (Kraken X60): Unlike incremental encoders that count pulses and lose position on power-off, the Kraken uses an absolute magnetic encoder that always knows its exact position — even after power cycling. Critical for swerve drive steer modules, which need to return to the correct angle without homing every startup.

📶 PWM AND HOW MOTOR CONTROLLERS WORK

PWM = Pulse Width Modulation. Instead of varying voltage (which is inefficient and hard to do cleanly), motor controllers keep the voltage at a fixed level and switch it on and off very rapidly. The duty cycle — the percentage of time the signal is "on" versus "off" — determines the average power delivered.

Vavg = Vsupply × Duty Cycle %
50% duty cycle at 12V → 6V average to motor
100% duty → full 12V | 0% duty → 0V (motor off)

PWM is efficient because the switching transistors (MOSFETs) are either fully on (almost zero resistance, low heat) or fully off (no current). They don't spend time in the lossy linear region. A typical FRC motor controller switches at 10–20 kHz (10,000–20,000 times per second) — fast enough that the motor inductance smooths the current into a near-DC average.

Traditional PWM signal from RoboRIO: A 50Hz signal where 1ms = full reverse, 1.5ms = neutral, 2ms = full forward. The RoboRIO's PWM output headers send this signal to older motor controllers (Victor SP, Spark). Modern CAN-based controllers (TalonFX, SparkMAX) receive setpoints over CAN instead of a PWM signal.

H-Bridge — the key circuit in a motor controller: To drive a brushed DC motor in both directions, you need an H-bridge: four switching transistors (MOSFETs) arranged so any combination of two can be switched to send current in either direction through the motor.

For 3-phase BLDC, there are three H-bridges (or 6 MOSFETs total), one per phase. The controller switches them in the correct firing sequence based on rotor position feedback.

💡 USING CURRENT AS A SENSOR — GAME PIECE DETECTION

Here's a clever FRC trick that ties together everything we know about amps, volts, and watts (or as the classic mnemonic goes: "Watt are amps and volts? ...Watts ARE volts times amps!").

When an intake motor is spinning freely — no game piece — it draws very little current. The motor has low load, low torque demand, and runs near free-speed. But when a game piece enters the intake and provides mechanical resistance, the motor slows down slightly. With less back-EMF, more current flows. The motor controller reports this current increase over CAN.

Why the physics works: Recall P = V × I. With voltage roughly constant (12V from the battery), a higher mechanical load on the motor requires more power → more current. The motor controller's internal current sensor (all modern FRC controllers report this) sees this spike instantly. You can threshold on this in your robot code:

if (intakeMotor.getOutputCurrent() > 20A) → gamePieceDetected = true
Free-spin current: ~3–5A  |  Loaded (piece held): 15–30A

Advantages of current-based detection:

  • No additional sensor hardware needed
  • Works regardless of game piece position in the mechanism
  • Detects game pieces that physical sensors might miss (odd angles, transparency)
  • Uses information already available from the motor controller via CAN

Tips for reliability: Add a short time delay (e.g., 100ms) to avoid false positives from startup transients. Apply a moving average filter to smooth out current noise. Set your threshold just above the normal no-load running current with some margin.

🔄 NEMA STEPPER MOTORS — HOW THEY WORK IN FRC

Stepper motors move in fixed discrete steps. The most common step angle is 1.8° per step, giving 200 full steps per revolution. Half-stepping (400 steps/rev) and microstepping (up to 51,200 steps/rev with a good driver) allow much finer resolution by partially energizing adjacent coils.

Bipolar vs Unipolar:

  • Bipolar (4-wire): Two coils, current reverses direction in each coil to step. Higher torque, requires an H-bridge driver (A4988, DRV8825, TMC2209). The standard in modern FRC and robotics use.
  • Unipolar (6 or 8-wire): Center-tapped coils. Simpler control (current only flows one direction), but half the copper is active at any time → lower torque. Less common in new designs.

🏭 NEMA AC MOTORS — BRIEF MENTION

NEMA also defines frame sizes for AC induction motors under the NEMA MG 1 standard — this is a separate, much older system used for industrial motors. Frame designations look like 56, 143T, 182T, 213T, where the number encodes the shaft height and bolt pattern. The T suffix indicates the current T-frame standard (since 1964).

These are the large motors on pumps, fans, compressors, conveyor belts, and HVAC equipment — typically 1/4 HP to hundreds of HP, powered by 120V/240V single-phase or 208V/480V three-phase AC. You are unlikely to encounter them in FRC competition, but you will find them everywhere in manufacturing, facilities maintenance, and the shop equipment (drill press, lathe, band saw) in your build space.

🌍 IEC MOTOR FRAMES — BRIEF MENTION

Outside North America, motors follow the IEC 60034 standard (International Electrotechnical Commission). IEC frame sizes are designated by numbers like IEC 56, IEC 71, IEC 90, IEC 112, IEC 132. The number is the shaft center height in millimeters — a clear, intuitive system.

NEMA and IEC frames are not interchangeable — different bolt patterns, shaft diameters, and mounting dimensions. In international FRC competitions or when sourcing parts from overseas suppliers, verify which standard a motor uses before designing a mount. Many modern suppliers list both NEMA and IEC equivalents for cross-reference.

For stepper motors specifically, NEMA frame naming is used worldwide regardless of IEC/NEMA geography — you'll see NEMA 17 and NEMA 23 steppers on datasheets from Chinese, European, and US manufacturers alike, because the stepper motor industry adopted NEMA naming globally before IEC had a competing stepper standard.

COAST, BRAKE, AND REGENERATIVE BRAKING

🌊 COAST MODE

In coast mode, the motor controller disconnects all three phase outputs — none of the MOSFETs are conducting. The rotor spins freely with only bearing friction slowing it down. The motor is electrically disconnected from the supply.

Coast is appropriate when you want smooth, gradual deceleration without jerking the mechanism — for example, a flywheel spinning down slowly, or a drivetrain that should glide to a stop. The motor's own back-EMF has nowhere to go and dissipates only in the motor winding resistance.

FRC setting: motor.setIdleMode(IdleMode.kCoast) in SparkMAX or talonFX.setNeutralMode(NeutralModeValue.Coast) in TalonFX. Many teams set drive motors to coast for a floaty feel; mechanisms that must hold position need brake.

🛑 BRAKE MODE

In brake mode, the motor controller shorts all three motor phases together through the low-side MOSFETs. The motor becomes its own load — any motion generates back-EMF, which drives current through the short circuit (the motor's own winding resistance), dissipating energy as heat and creating a braking force proportional to speed.

This is resistive braking, not regenerative — the energy is burned as heat in the motor windings, not returned to the battery. It's fast, predictable, and requires no special hardware. The harder the motor tries to spin, the more it resists itself.

When to use it: Elevator mechanisms (must stop quickly), intake arms (hold position against gravity), shooters (stop wheel fast between shots). Brake mode stresses motor windings slightly more thermally, but for FRC durations this is not significant.

FRC setting: IdleMode.kBrake / NeutralModeValue.Brake

♻️ REGENERATIVE BRAKING

In regenerative braking, the motor is operated as a generator — the mechanical kinetic energy of the spinning/moving robot is converted back to electrical energy and returned to the battery. This is real energy recovery, not heat dissipation.

How it works: When a BLDC motor's rotor spins faster than the commanded speed (or when braking), back-EMF exceeds supply voltage. The motor controller uses the upper MOSFETs to route this back-EMF current back into the DC bus. The battery's chemistry then absorbs this reverse current (charging). The current flow through the battery's internal resistance opposes the motion — creating braking force.

In FRC: Modern motor controllers (TalonFX, SparkMAX) perform limited regenerative braking automatically when the motor is driven against load (controlled deceleration). The energy returned to the 12V battery per match is small — FRC robots are lightweight and matches are short. But in an elevator or high-inertia flywheel, regen significantly extends battery life compared to pure resistive braking.

In EVs: Regenerative braking in a Tesla recovers 15–25% of energy normally lost as heat in friction brakes, dramatically extending range. The physics is identical to FRC — the motor controller becomes a generator controller.

AC INDUCTION MOTORS — YOUR CAR, YOUR WASHING MACHINE, YOUR FACTORY

🏭 HOW AC INDUCTION MOTORS WORK

AC induction motors are the most widely used motor type in the world — they run the vast majority of industrial equipment, appliances, HVAC systems, and many electric vehicles. Like BLDC, they have a stator with 3-phase coils. Unlike BLDC, the rotor has no permanent magnets and no electrical connection at all — just conductive bars (like a squirrel cage) or conductive loops.

The rotating magnetic field produced by the 3-phase stator coils induces currents in the rotor bars (by Faraday's law — a changing magnetic field through a conductor induces a current). These induced currents create a magnetic field in the rotor that is attracted to the rotating stator field, dragging the rotor around. The rotor always spins slightly slower than the stator field — this difference is called slip, and it's what allows induction to occur. A synchronous motor (like a BLDC at a fixed speed) has no slip.

🚗 ELECTRIC VEHICLES — "AC MOTOR FROM DC BATTERY"

The most common question about EVs: "If the battery is DC, how do you power an AC motor?" The answer: an inverter (the same MOSFET H-bridge concept from motor controllers, scaled up enormously).

Tesla Model 3 rear motor: Uses a Permanent Magnet Synchronous Motor (PMSM) — this is essentially a very large BLDC motor, identical in concept to an FRC Kraken X60 but producing 283 hp. The drive inverter in the car converts the 350V DC battery pack into 3-phase AC at whatever frequency and amplitude is needed to produce the desired speed and torque. Field-Oriented Control (FOC) algorithms run in real time, doing exactly what the TalonFX does in your robot, just at 200kW instead of 500W.

Older Tesla Model S front motor: Used an AC induction motor (Nikola Tesla's design, hence the brand name) — no permanent magnets, squirrel cage rotor, powered by an inverter converting battery DC to 3-phase AC. Simpler rotor, slightly less efficient at partial load but extremely robust.

The takeaway: The motor controller on your FRC robot and the $15,000 drive inverter in a Tesla are doing the same fundamental job: converting DC from a battery into controlled 3-phase power for a motor, using switched MOSFETs, encoder feedback, and FOC algorithms. The FRC version just has less power and fits in your hand.

🤖 DC IS A MISNOMER FOR BLDC? Technically yes. BLDC motors have AC in their phases — the "DC" refers to the battery/supply being DC. The motor controller synthesizes 3-phase AC internally. "DC brushless motor" really means "a brushless motor powered by a DC supply, with internal inverter." The alternative name Permanent Magnet Synchronous Motor (PMSM) is more technically accurate and is used in engineering literature. FRC just calls them "brushless motors."

SEMICONDUCTORS, TRANSISTORS & MOSFETs
SEMICONDUCTORS · P/N DOPING · TRANSISTORS · MOSFETs · HOW MOTOR CONTROLLERS WORK AT THE SILICON LEVEL

⚛ WHAT IS A SEMICONDUCTOR?

Recall from §1: conductors have free electrons (metal), insulators have none (plastic/rubber). Semiconductors sit in between — at room temperature they conduct poorly, but their conductivity can be precisely controlled.

The most important semiconductor is silicon (Si). Silicon has 4 valence electrons and forms a crystal lattice where each atom shares electrons with 4 neighbors (covalent bonds). At absolute zero, silicon is a perfect insulator — all electrons are locked in bonds. At room temperature, thermal energy breaks a few bonds, creating a small number of free electrons and "holes" (missing electrons that act like positive charges). Pure silicon has very few free carriers — it's a poor conductor but not an insulator.

The magic — DOPING: Adding tiny amounts of impurity atoms changes everything:

  • N-type (Negative carriers): Add phosphorus (5 valence electrons). The extra electron has no bond partner → free to move. The material now has an abundance of free electrons as majority carriers.
  • P-type (Positive carriers): Add boron (3 valence electrons). The missing electron creates a "hole" — a positive carrier. Holes flow in the opposite direction to electrons.

By placing N-type and P-type silicon adjacent to each other, you create a junction with remarkable properties: a diode (current flows one way only), or the basis for a transistor.

🔀 WHAT IS A TRANSISTOR?

A transistor is a semiconductor device that uses a small signal to control a large current — an electrically controlled switch or amplifier. Invented at Bell Labs in 1947, it is the fundamental building block of all modern electronics. Your phone has roughly 15 billion transistors. A motor controller has dozens to hundreds.

The classic bipolar junction transistor (BJT) has three terminals: Base, Collector, Emitter. A small current into the Base "opens the gate" allowing a much larger current to flow from Collector to Emitter. Current gain (β) is typically 50–300× — 1mA into the base allows 100–300mA through the collector-emitter path.

As a switch: BJT fully off (open) → no current flows (motor off). BJT fully on (saturation) → maximum current flows (motor on). PWM rapidly switches between these two states.

BJTs are current-controlled. MOSFETs are voltage-controlled — which is why MOSFETs dominate modern power electronics: they require almost no current to control, meaning the microcontroller driving them loses almost no energy in the control signal.

🔌 MOSFET — METAL-OXIDE-SEMICONDUCTOR FIELD-EFFECT TRANSISTOR

The MOSFET is the workhorse of power electronics. Every FRC motor controller uses a set of MOSFETs (typically 6 per 3-phase controller). The name breaks down as:

  • Metal: The gate electrode (actually polysilicon in modern devices, but originally metal)
  • Oxide: A thin layer of silicon dioxide (glass!) that insulates the gate from the channel
  • Semiconductor: The silicon substrate through which current flows
  • Field-Effect: The gate controls current by creating an electric field, not by passing current through itself
  • Transistor: Three terminals — Gate, Drain, Source

How it switches: In an N-channel enhancement MOSFET (most common in low-side switches):

  • Gate voltage at 0V relative to Source → channel is off, almost no current flows (like an open switch)
  • Apply ~10V to Gate → electric field inverts the P-type channel region, creating a conductive N-type path → current flows freely from Drain to Source (switch closed)

The gate draws almost zero current (the oxide is a near-perfect insulator), so a 3.3V microcontroller GPIO can switch hundreds of amps through a motor — with appropriate gate driver circuitry to ensure fast switching.

On-resistance (RDS(on)): When fully on, a MOSFET has a small but nonzero resistance (typically 1–10 mΩ for power MOSFETs). At 100A, even 5mΩ = 0.5V drop = 50W of heat. FRC motor controllers use large, low-RDS(on) power MOSFETs and heat sinks to manage this.

INTRO TO PRINTED CIRCUIT BOARDS (PCBs)

🟩 WHAT IS A PCB?

A Printed Circuit Board (PCB) is a board that mechanically supports and electrically connects electronic components using conductive traces etched from copper sheets laminated onto a non-conductive substrate. It replaces the tangle of individual wires you'd otherwise need to connect components.

The substrate is most commonly FR4 — a fiberglass-reinforced epoxy laminate. FR4 is rigid, thermally stable, and an excellent electrical insulator. The copper layer on top (and/or bottom and inner layers) starts as a solid sheet — then acid or laser etching removes the copper everywhere except where you want your traces.

Anatomy of a PCB layer stackup (2-layer, most common for FRC custom boards):

  • Top copper layer: Signal traces, component pads
  • Soldermask: Green (or other color) protective coating — prevents solder bridges between traces. The "green board" color is the soldermask, not the board itself.
  • Silkscreen: White text/markings showing component references (R1, U2, J3), polarity markers, and logos
  • FR4 substrate
  • Bottom copper layer: Ground planes, additional signal routing
  • Bottom soldermask

SMD vs THT COMPONENTS

Through-Hole Technology (THT): Component leads pass through holes in the PCB and are soldered on the opposite side. Mechanically very strong. Easy to hand-solder. Good for high-current connectors, large capacitors, and anything that needs mechanical stress tolerance. The RoboRIO, Talons, and SparkMAX all use THT for their terminal connectors.

Surface-Mount Technology (SMT/SMD): Components sit on the PCB surface with leads or pads that solder directly to the top copper layer. No holes needed. Components are miniaturized — a 0402 resistor is 1mm × 0.5mm. Enables very dense, small boards. Requires reflow oven or skilled hand soldering. Most modern electronics are primarily SMT.

FRC Custom Boards: If you're designing your first custom PCB, use THT or large SMD packages (1206 resistors, SOT-23 transistors). Avoid tiny packages until you have proper reflow equipment.

🏭 HOW A SEMICONDUCTOR CHIP IS MADE — FROM SAND TO SILICON

Computer chips start as ordinary silicon dioxide — sand. Silicon (Si) is the second most abundant element in Earth's crust. The process of turning it into a functioning processor is one of the most complex manufacturing feats in human history, involving tolerances smaller than a virus.

1 — Purification: Quartz sand (SiO₂) is reduced to metallurgical-grade silicon (~98% pure), then further purified to electronic-grade silicon (99.9999999% pure — nine nines). Even a few atoms of impurity per billion can ruin transistors.

2 — Crystal growth (Czochralski process): Pure silicon is melted at 1,414°C and a seed crystal is slowly pulled upward while rotating, growing a single-crystal silicon ingot (boule) up to 300mm (12") in diameter and 1–2 meters long. The entire boule is one continuous crystal — no grain boundaries that would cause defects.

3 — Wafer slicing & polishing: The boule is sliced into wafers ~0.7mm thick using diamond wire saws. Wafers are polished to atomic-level flatness — variations of less than 1 nanometer across a 300mm surface.

4 — Photolithography: The magic step. A photosensitive material (photoresist) is applied to the wafer. A mask (like a photographic negative) containing the circuit pattern is projected onto the wafer using UV or EUV light. Where light hits, the photoresist becomes soluble. Chemical etching then removes material, carving out the circuit pattern. This is repeated dozens of times with different masks to build up layers.

5 — EUV (Extreme Ultraviolet Lithography): For features below 7nm, standard UV light has a wavelength too long to accurately project tiny features. EUV uses 13.5nm wavelength light — generated by firing a tin droplet with a laser until it becomes plasma, then collecting the emitted EUV light with specialized mirrors. The machines that do this are made by a single Dutch company: ASML. Each machine weighs ~180 tons, costs $150–400 million, and requires 40 shipping containers to transport. There is no alternative supplier on Earth for EUV — it is arguably the single most geopolitically critical industrial machine ever built.

6 — Doping, deposition, metallization: Ion implantation adds controlled impurities to create N-type and P-type regions. Chemical vapor deposition builds up metal interconnect layers (copper, now cobalt and ruthenium). A modern chip has 10–15+ layers of metal wiring.

7 — Testing & dicing: Completed wafers contain hundreds to thousands of dies. Each die is electrically tested before dicing. The wafer is cut into individual chips using diamond-tipped saws or laser dicing.

8 — Packaging: The bare die is mounted in a package (the green rectangle with pins you see), wire-bonded or flip-chip connected to the package substrate, and encapsulated. Modern advanced packaging (TSMC CoWoS, Intel Foveros, AMD 3D V-Cache) stacks multiple dies using microscopic copper pillars called Through-Silicon Vias (TSVs).

✂️ CHIP BINNING — WHY THE "SAME" CHIP COMES IN DIFFERENT SKUs

No two chips off a wafer are identical. Defects, microscopic variations in doping, and crystal irregularities mean some dies run faster, cooler, or at lower voltage than others. Binning is the process of testing each chip and assigning it to a product tier based on measured performance.

A wafer of AMD Ryzen 7 dies might be tested and sorted into: Ryzen 9 (fastest, lowest voltage — premium price), Ryzen 7 (good performance), Ryzen 5 (a few cores disabled due to defects — still sold), Ryzen 3 (more cores disabled, lower max frequency), and reject (fails testing entirely — scrapped or sold for embedded use).

Why this is economically brilliant: A single production line produces all product tiers simultaneously. A wafer with many defects still yields profitable chips at lower tiers. A perfect wafer yields premium chips at the highest price. Average die yield directly determines profitability — getting 80% yield vs 60% yield at 7nm can mean hundreds of millions in profit difference.

The "golden sample": Enthusiasts know that some cheap CPUs are premium dies "underperforming" due to conservative factory settings. "Silicon lottery" refers to the luck of getting a chip from the fast end of the yield distribution — it will overclock further than spec. Some binned chips with "disabled" cores can have those cores re-enabled with software modifications (AMD Phenom II Black Edition days).

THE COMPANIES THAT BUILT THE DIGITAL WORLD

🔵 INTEL — THE INCUMBENT GIANT

Founded 1968 by Gordon Moore (of Moore's Law) and Robert Noyce (co-inventor of the integrated circuit), both veterans of Fairchild Semiconductor. Intel's first product was SRAM memory. In 1971 they produced the Intel 4004 — the first commercially available single-chip microprocessor. The 8086 (1978) established the x86 architecture that still runs most of the world's PCs and servers today.

Intel dominated the PC era through the 1990s–2000s with Pentium, Core, Xeon. Their "tick-tock" cadence (alternating new process node and new microarchitecture each year) seemed unstoppable. But they stumbled: the 14nm process node arrived in 2014 and Intel remained on 14nm++ variants until 2019, while TSMC and Samsung advanced to 7nm and 5nm. AMD, built on TSMC's process, closed the performance gap dramatically.

Key acquisitions: Acquired Altera (FPGA maker) in 2015 for $16.7B; acquired Mobileye (autonomous driving vision) 2017 for $15.3B. Tried to acquire Tower Semiconductor (Israeli foundry, 2022) — blocked by China regulators 2023.

Intel fabs: Fab 28 and Fab 38 in Kiryat Gat, Israel; major fabs in Arizona, Oregon, Ireland. Building new Ohio fabs under the US CHIPS Act. CEO Pat Gelsinger (2021–2024) launched IDM 2.0 strategy to compete as a foundry.

🔴 AMD — THE COMEBACK KID

Advanced Micro Devices founded 1969 by Jerry Sanders. Started as a second-source manufacturer of Intel chips (Intel actually required a second-source for IBM PC contracts in 1982). AMD's cross-license agreement with Intel has been contentious and litigated for decades but gave AMD perpetual rights to produce x86 chips.

The Athlon (1999) was AMD's first genuinely competitive Intel-beating processor. Opteron (2003) introduced 64-bit x86 extensions (AMD64 / x86-64) — Intel had to adopt AMD's own 64-bit extension for their chips, calling it EM64T. AMD also pioneered dual-core CPUs.

AMD acquired ATI Technologies (GPU maker) in 2006 for $5.4 billion, giving them both CPU and GPU divisions. Years of financial struggle followed (2012-2017). Then Lisa Su became CEO in 2014, and the Zen architecture launched in 2017 revitalized AMD completely. Ryzen, EPYC server chips, and Radeon GPUs became genuinely competitive or best-in-class.

AMD acquired Xilinx (FPGAs) in 2022 for $49B, and Pensando (data center networking) for $1.9B. AMD is now the only company with competitive CPU, GPU, and FPGA product lines simultaneously. The RoboRIO 2.0's Xilinx Zynq chip is now technically an AMD product.

🟢 NVIDIA — FROM GAMING TO AI DOMINANCE

Founded 1993 by Jensen Huang, Chris Malachowsky, and Curtis Priem. Their first product (NV1, 1995) was a failure. The RIVA 128 (1997) was their first success. The GeForce 256 (1999) coined the term "GPU" and introduced hardware transform and lighting.

NVIDIA's CUDA parallel computing platform (2006) was visionary — it allowed scientists and engineers to use GPU shader cores for general-purpose parallel computation, not just graphics. This laid the groundwork for the AI revolution a decade later, because AI training is fundamentally matrix multiplication — exactly what GPU shader arrays excel at.

NVIDIA tried to acquire ARM for $40 billion in 2020 — blocked by regulators worldwide in 2022 on competition grounds. ARM IPO'd in 2023.

Post-ChatGPT (2022), demand for NVIDIA H100/H200 AI training GPUs became essentially infinite. Governments and hyperscalers (Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta) bought $billions of GPUs. NVIDIA's market cap briefly exceeded $3.3 trillion in 2024, making it briefly the most valuable company in the world. Jensen Huang became the most important figure in tech seemingly overnight.

GPU architecture names: Tesla → Fermi → Kepler → Maxwell → Pascal → Volta → Turing → Ampere → Hopper → Blackwell. Consumer = GeForce. Data center AI = H-series/B-series. Workstation = Quadro/RTX Pro.

🟡 TSMC — THE WORLD'S MOST CRITICAL FACTORY

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company, founded 1987 by Morris Chang. TSMC pioneered the pure-play foundry model — a company that manufactures chips designed by others, with no competing products of its own. This allowed fabless semiconductor companies (AMD, NVIDIA, Apple, Qualcomm, etc.) to exist without billion-dollar fabs.

TSMC manufactures chips for Apple, AMD, NVIDIA, Qualcomm, Broadcom, and hundreds of other companies. They produce ~90% of the world's most advanced semiconductor nodes (<10nm). A disruption to TSMC — earthquake, political event, blockade — would halt global electronics production within months. This is why Taiwan's status is one of the most sensitive geopolitical issues on Earth.

TSMC is building fabs in Arizona (multiple sites, first chips 2024), Kumamoto, Japan (partnered with Sony, already producing), and considering Germany. These are partly TSMC-driven expansion and partly host-government pressure to reduce concentration risk in Taiwan.

🔷 ASML — ONE COMPANY, ONE MACHINE, ALL THE CHIPS

ASML (Advanced Semiconductor Materials Lithography), Dutch company founded 1984 as a joint venture of ASM International and Philips. They make the photolithography machines that pattern chips. For advanced nodes (sub-7nm), ASML is the sole supplier on Earth of EUV (Extreme Ultraviolet) machines.

EUV machines cost $150–400M each, contain over 100,000 parts, require 40 shipping containers, and represent 30+ years of physics research. The US has pressured the Netherlands to restrict ASML from selling EUV (and now even older DUV) machines to China — one of the most powerful technology export controls in history. China is spending billions trying to develop domestic alternatives; they are currently unable to manufacture chips below ~28nm without ASML equipment.

ARM — THE ARCHITECTURE IN EVERY POCKET

ARM (Advanced RISC Machines) was founded 1990 as a spin-off of Acorn Computers in Cambridge, UK — a partnership with Apple and VLSI Technology. ARM doesn't manufacture chips; it designs CPU architectures and licenses them. Every chip maker pays ARM a royalty to use their instruction set and CPU designs.

ARM is in essentially every smartphone ever made (Apple A-series, Qualcomm Snapdragon, Samsung Exynos, MediaTek Dimensity). Also: network routers, embedded microcontrollers (Cortex-M), server chips (AWS Graviton, Ampere Altra), Apple Silicon Macs, and now even Windows PCs (Qualcomm Snapdragon X Elite).

Architecture families: Cortex-A (application processors, high performance), Cortex-R (real-time), Cortex-M (microcontrollers — including the STM32 in FRC motor controllers, the RP2040 in Raspberry Pi Pico). The RoboRIO 2.0's Zynq contains ARM Cortex-A9 cores. The upcoming SystemCore uses ARM Cortex-A76.

SoftBank acquired ARM for $32B in 2016. ARM IPO'd in 2023 (SoftBank retained majority). NVIDIA's $40B acquisition attempt was blocked 2022.

QUALCOMM & BROADCOM

Qualcomm (founded 1985, San Diego): Dominant in smartphone SoCs (Snapdragon series). ARM-based. World's leading 5G modem supplier. Acquired Nuvia (custom ARM core startup) 2021 for $1.4B, yielding the Oryon CPU cores in Snapdragon X Elite. Tried to acquire NXP Semiconductors for $44B in 2018 — blocked by China in 2019. Also makes WiFi/Bluetooth chips (many FRC radios use Qualcomm chipsets internally).

Broadcom (formed by merger of Broadcom Corp and Avago Technologies in 2015 for $37B): Makes networking ASICs, storage controllers, and crucially — the Broadcom BCM2XXX SoC inside every Raspberry Pi. Acquired CA Technologies ($18.9B), Symantec Enterprise ($10.7B), and VMware ($69B in 2023 — one of the largest tech acquisitions ever). Essentially controls much of enterprise networking and virtualization infrastructure.

Legacy consolidations: The semiconductor industry has seen relentless M&A: Broadcom+Brocade, Marvell+Cavium, NXP+Freescale, Microchip+Atmel (the company that made the original Arduino ATmega chips), ON Semi+Fairchild (original birthplace of the integrated circuit itself).

🌍 SEMICONDUCTOR GEOPOLITICS

Taiwan & TSMC: ~90% of the world's most advanced chips come from an island 100 miles from China, which claims sovereignty over it. The US CHIPS and Science Act (2022) allocated $52 billion for domestic semiconductor manufacturing. Taiwan's political status is the most important single geopolitical variable in global technology.

South Korea: Samsung Semiconductor (memory + foundry) and SK Hynix together supply ~70% of global DRAM and ~40% of NAND flash. South Korea is as critical to memory chips as Taiwan is to logic chips.

Intel in Israel: Intel's Fab 28 and Fab 38 in Kiryat Gat employ ~11,000 people. Intel has invested over $25 billion in Israel since the 1970s. Israeli engineers have been responsible for key Intel microarchitectures including the Core 2 Duo and Sandy Bridge. Intel Fab 34 in Kiryat Gat uses Intel 7 (10nm) process.

China: Spending hundreds of billions to achieve semiconductor self-sufficiency. SMIC is China's most advanced foundry but cannot access EUV. Export controls prevent them from reaching <14nm at scale. Huawei's Kirin chips (made by SMIC) are a significant domestic effort but remain behind TSMC's leading edge.

Netherlands/ASML: A single Dutch company's export decisions determine which nations can manufacture advanced chips. The US-Netherlands-Japan export control agreement (2023) restricts the most advanced DUV and all EUV equipment from China.

CPU ARCHITECTURES — x86, ARM, RISC-V

x86 — THE LEGACY GIANT

x86 was born with the Intel 8086 (1978) and extended to 32-bit with the 386 (1985, "IA-32"). AMD extended it to 64-bit in 2003 with AMD64 (also called x86-64 or EM64T). Intel had to adopt AMD's 64-bit extension — one of the few times Intel followed AMD's lead.

x86 is a CISC (Complex Instruction Set Computer) architecture. A single x86 instruction can do multiple operations. Internally, modern x86 CPUs translate complex instructions into micro-operations (µops) — they behave like RISC internally while presenting a CISC interface to software.

Who holds x86 licenses: Intel (originator), AMD (1982 cross-license, perpetual), VIA Technologies / Zhaoxin (Chinese x86 clone, licensed), Hygon (Chinese joint venture using AMD Zen architecture, contested). No new x86 licenses have been issued in decades — this duopoly is protected.

x86 runs Windows and Linux server workloads. Despite ARM encroachment, x86 software compatibility (decades of compiled code) keeps it dominant in PCs and servers for now.

ARM — RISC WINS THE MOBILE WAR

ARM uses a RISC (Reduced Instruction Set Computer) philosophy: simple instructions that execute in one cycle. The original 1985 Acorn RISC Machine (ARM1) was notable for extraordinary power efficiency — critical for battery-powered devices.

ARM's license model comes in two flavors: architecture license (design your own cores using the ARM ISA — Apple, Qualcomm, Amazon do this) and core license (use ARM's pre-designed Cortex-A/M/R cores directly — most other chip makers). This flexibility enabled ARM's ubiquity.

ARM now runs nearly every smartphone, most tablets, modern laptops (Apple MacBooks, Qualcomm Windows PCs, Chromebooks), AWS cloud servers, and microcontrollers everywhere including FRC motor controllers. The power/performance efficiency advantage over x86 for mobile use cases proved decisive.

ARMv8 / AArch64 is the current 64-bit ARM architecture. Apple's M-series chips are ARMv8.6-A, custom-designed by Apple's silicon team.

RISC-V — THE OPEN STANDARD

RISC-V ("RISC five") is an open, royalty-free instruction set architecture developed at UC Berkeley starting 2010. Unlike x86 (Intel/AMD duopoly) and ARM (license fees), RISC-V is governed by RISC-V International (non-profit, headquartered in Switzerland to be geopolitically neutral).

Anyone can design, manufacture, and sell RISC-V chips without paying royalties. This is extremely attractive to China (ARM export controls are a concern), embedded systems designers, and academia.

Current uses: Western Digital uses RISC-V cores in their HDD controllers. SiFive makes RISC-V CPUs. NVIDIA uses RISC-V for management processors inside their GPUs. Google's Tensor chips contain RISC-V security cores. India is investing heavily in domestic RISC-V chip design. The ESP32-C3 (very common in DIY robotics) uses a RISC-V core.

RISC-V is not yet competitive with x86 or ARM in high-performance computing, but its momentum in embedded and specialized applications is significant. It may be the architecture of the next decade in places where ARM licenses are unavailable or undesirable.

🍎 APPLE SILICON — THE GREAT MIGRATION (PowerPC → Intel → ARM)

Apple's processor journey is one of the most significant architectural transitions in computing history, and they've done it twice.

PowerPC era (1994–2006): Apple switched from Motorola 68k CPUs to the PowerPC architecture — developed jointly by Apple, IBM, and Motorola (the AIM alliance). PowerPC was a high-performance RISC architecture used in the Power Mac G3, G4, and G5. IBM's G5 ran hot and power-hungry; Apple couldn't make a thin laptop with it. Steve Jobs announced at WWDC 2005 that Apple was switching to Intel.

Intel era (2006–2020): The Intel Mac transition was remarkably smooth — Rosetta translation software ran PowerPC binaries on x86, and eventually all software was rebuilt natively. MacBooks became popular among developers partly because they ran Mac OS X, Windows, and Linux simultaneously. But Apple was always at Intel's mercy for roadmap timing and power efficiency.

The foundation — iPhone A-series (2010–2020): Apple quietly began designing their own ARM chips with the A4 in the original iPhone 4 (2010). Each generation (A5, A6…A14) demonstrated Apple's custom ARM designs outperforming anything Qualcomm or Samsung made. By the A12 Bionic (2018) it was clear Apple had world-class CPU and GPU design capability in-house.

Apple Silicon era (2020–present): At WWDC 2020, Apple announced the Mac transition to Apple Silicon. The M1 (late 2020) was a revelation. Built on TSMC 5nm, the M1 outperformed most Intel laptops in both performance and battery life. The key innovation: Unified Memory Architecture (UMA) — the CPU, GPU, Neural Engine, and media engines all share the same high-bandwidth memory pool (LPDDR5 with bandwidth up to 68 GB/s), eliminating the memory copy bottleneck between CPU and GPU that plagues discrete GPU systems.

M1 → M1 Pro/Max/Ultra → M2/M3/M4: The Pro and Max variants are M1 chips with more GPU cores and wider memory bandwidth. The Ultra is literally two M1 Max dies glued together using Apple's UltraFusion interconnect — they appear as one chip to software. This "tile" approach is essentially the same chiplet strategy AMD uses with EPYC.

Why it matters: Apple demonstrated that a vertically integrated company controlling its own OS, software, and silicon can produce hardware that pure hardware companies (Intel, Qualcomm) struggle to match. The entire industry took notice. Qualcomm hired engineers from Nuvia (founded by former Apple silicon architects) to attempt to replicate the approach on Windows.

MODERN CPU & GPU ARCHITECTURE

🧠 MODERN CPU DESIGN — CHIPLETS, CACHE & 3D STACKING

Monolithic vs Chiplet design: A monolithic CPU puts all cores on one die. As core counts grew, monolithic dies became too large (large dies = more defects = lower yield = higher cost). The solution: chiplets — multiple smaller dies connected by a high-speed interconnect on a common interposer/package. AMD's EPYC processors use up to 8 "compute chiplets" (CCDs) each containing 8 cores, connected via the Infinity Fabric interconnect. This allows AMD to mix and match chiplets, reuse known-good dies, and achieve effective yields that would be impossible with a monolithic design at the same scale.

Cache hierarchy:

LevelLocationSizeLatencyPurpose
L1Per core32–512 KB4–5 cyclesFastest; split into L1-I (instructions) and L1-D (data)
L2Per core256 KB–32 MB12–15 cyclesSecond tier; "victim cache" for L1 misses
L3Shared across core cluster or all cores4–128 MB+40–60 cyclesLast on-chip resort before main RAM (100+ cycle latency)

AMD 3D V-Cache: AMD stacks an additional 64–128 MB of SRAM cache directly on top of the compute chiplet using Through-Silicon Vias (TSVs) — microscopic copper pillars that connect the stacked dies electrically. The cache die sits physically on top of the CPU die, separated by only a few microns. This dramatically reduces cache miss penalties in games and scientific workloads — the Ryzen 7 5800X3D showed 15–40% gaming performance gains purely from the extra cache with no other changes.

🎮 GPU — PARALLEL PROCESSING & MULTI-GPU HISTORY

A GPU has thousands of small, simple shader cores optimized for parallel floating-point math — ideal for graphics (millions of pixels processed simultaneously) and AI/ML (matrix multiplication is embarrassingly parallel). An NVIDIA RTX 4090 has 16,384 CUDA cores; a high-end CPU has 16–32 complex cores. Different tools for different problems.

Multi-GPU history:

  • SLI (Scalable Link Interface, NVIDIA 2004–2020): Two or more NVIDIA GPUs connected by an SLI bridge for combined rendering. Frames were split between GPUs (alternate frame rendering or split-frame rendering). In practice, poor scaling, driver issues, microstutter, and not all games supporting it made SLI problematic. NVIDIA dropped consumer SLI support in 2020.
  • CrossFire (AMD, 2005–2017): AMD's multi-GPU equivalent. Same issues. Discontinued.
  • NVLink (NVIDIA, data center): High-bandwidth interconnect between data center GPUs (A100, H100) for AI training across multiple GPUs. Bandwidth up to 900 GB/s vs 64 GB/s for PCIe. This is the serious multi-GPU technology that actually works — used in HPC clusters and AI training rigs.

PhysX: NVIDIA acquired Ageia Technologies in 2008, who had invented a dedicated Physics Processing Unit (PPU) — a separate card for game physics simulation. NVIDIA folded PhysX into CUDA, allowing game physics to run on GPU shader cores. The dedicated PhysX card became obsolete almost immediately after the acquisition.

VRAM types: Consumer GPUs use GDDR6/GDDR7 (Graphics DDR). AI/data center GPUs use HBM (High Bandwidth Memory) — 3D-stacked DRAM on the same package as the GPU die, achieving 3–12× higher bandwidth than GDDR. HBM is why the H100 costs $30,000 — the HBM alone costs more than most consumer GPUs.

AI acceleration: NVIDIA's "Tensor Cores" (introduced Volta 2017) perform mixed-precision matrix math for AI training at dramatically higher throughput than standard CUDA cores. The H100 Tensor Core GPU delivers 3,958 TFLOPS of FP8 Tensor Core performance. Google's TPUs, Intel's Gaudi, and AMD's MI300X are alternative AI accelerators targeting the same market.

ARDUINO — THE MAKER REVOLUTION

📖 ARDUINO HISTORY

Arduino was born in 2005 at the Interaction Design Institute Ivrea (IDII) in Italy. Massimo Banzi, David Cuartielles, Tom Igoe, Gianluca Martino, and David Mellis created it as an inexpensive, beginner-friendly microcontroller board for students with no electronics background. Named after a bar in Ivrea where the founders met.

The core innovation wasn't the hardware (an Atmel ATmega microcontroller, already available) but the software ecosystem: a simplified IDE, a C++ wrapper library that hid low-level register manipulation, a massive library ecosystem, and a USB bootloader allowing programming without a separate programmer device. Arduino made embedded programming accessible to artists, educators, and hobbyists who had never touched a register.

Open-source hardware meant anyone could build compatible boards. A complicated legal dispute between founders led to a split into two entities "Arduino LLC" and "Arduino S.r.l." that was eventually resolved; the organization reunified in 2017.

Atmel ATmega: The ATmega328P chip in Arduino Uno has an 8-bit AVR RISC core, 32KB flash, 2KB RAM, runs at 16MHz, and costs ~$2. Microchip Technology acquired Atmel in 2016 for $3.56B — so modern ATmega chips are technically Microchip products.

RASPBERRY PI — THE SINGLE-BOARD COMPUTER

📖 RASPBERRY PI HISTORY

The Raspberry Pi Foundation was established in 2009 in Cambridge, UK by Eben Upton (then at Broadcom), Jack Lang, Alan Mycroft, Robert Mullins, and Pete Lomas. The mission: produce an affordable computer to improve computing education, inspired by the BBC Micro that had taught a generation of UK programmers in the 1980s.

The original Raspberry Pi Model B launched February 29, 2012 for $35. It sold 100,000 units on day one — crashing the servers. The Foundation had expected modest educational uptake; instead it triggered a global maker/hacker revolution. By 2023 the Foundation had sold over 60 million Raspberry Pis.

All Raspberry Pi boards use Broadcom SoCs (BCM2XXX series). The relationship with Broadcom is historic — Eben Upton worked at Broadcom during Pi's development, and Broadcom has provided chips at educational pricing. The Raspberry Pi CM5 inside SystemCore uses the same BCM2712 chip as the Raspberry Pi 5.

The Foundation's structure: A registered charity (Raspberry Pi Foundation) handles education and outreach. A separate commercial entity (Raspberry Pi Ltd) designs and sells hardware, donating profits to the charity. This unusual structure explains why they can sell hardware at component cost.
