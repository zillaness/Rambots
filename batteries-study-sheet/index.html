01

Tool batteries & the 18650

The workhorse cylindrical cell — and where its name comes from

The 18650 has been the standard rechargeable cylindrical cell for years. The name comes straight from the physical size: 18 mm diameter × 65.0 mm long, with the trailing "0" indicating a cylindrical form factor. The same naming pattern applies to all cylindrical cells.

Fig 118650 cell dimensions. The same three-part naming convention applies to all cylindrical cells: 21700 = 21mm × 70mm.

You'll find 18650s in old laptop battery packs (typically 6–9 cells wired together), flashlights, vape pens, e-bikes, and most major-brand power tool batteries. High-output tools have moved to the larger 21700 cell.

Cylindrical cell sizes

Tools and EVs are moving to bigger cells — more energy per cell, fewer welds per pack

Fig 2Cylindrical cells drawn to relative scale. The 4680 (used in Tesla Cybertruck) holds roughly 5× the energy of an 18650.

CellDimensionsTypical capacityWhere you'll find it

The trend toward bigger cells reduces complexity: fewer cells per pack → fewer welds, fewer BMS channels, simpler thermal management. Tesla's 4680 pack also uses the cell casing as structural load-bearing material ("structural battery pack").

Nominal voltage vs peak charge

Why "18V" and "20V Max" tool batteries are the same pack

A lithium-ion cell doesn't have a single fixed voltage. It varies continuously through the discharge cycle. Two reference points matter:

Fig 3Lithium-ion discharge curve. The cell starts at 4.2V fully charged, spends most of its useful life near 3.6–3.7V, then drops sharply near empty.

04

Series vs parallel

The foundation of every battery pack

Fig 4Series adds voltage. Parallel adds capacity. Real packs combine both — a "5S2P" pack has 5 in series (18V) then 2 strings in parallel (double capacity).

5S2P example using 3.6V / 3 Ah cells:
Voltage: 5 × 3.6 V = 18 V nominal
Capacity: 2 × 3 Ah = 6 Ah
Energy: 18 V × 6 Ah = 108 Wh total

High-performance batteries

Why brushless tool packs have extra contacts

"High Output," "FlexVolt," "M18 High Output" — these aren't just bigger standard packs. They differ in cell format (21700 instead of 18650), tuned chemistry for lower internal resistance, beefier internal busbars, thermistors, and critically — additional contacts for two-way communication with the tool.

Fig 5Through the data contacts, the brushless tool reads pack temperature, state of charge, cell health, and pack identity — throttles or refuses to fire if anything is wrong.

08

Battery Management System (BMS)

Watches every cell individually · balances during charge · cuts off if unsafe

A multi-cell pack is only as good as its weakest cell. If one drifts low while discharging or high while charging, the whole pack suffers — and an over-discharged or over-charged lithium cell is a fire risk.

Fig 6The BMS samples every individual cell via balance taps. During charging, it bleeds current from faster-charging cells so all finish at the same voltage. Without this, cells drift apart and pack capacity drops over time.

Never build a multi-cell lithium pack without a BMS. Hobby LiPo packs expose a balance lead so an external charger handles this. Tool packs and EVs do it internally and invisibly.

FIRST Robotics: Sealed Lead-Acid

Why FRC robots use AGM batteries, not lithium

The SLA family tree

Flooded lead-acid

Original. Lead plates in liquid acid. Spills if tipped, vents hydrogen gas. Still used in cars.

Gel cell

Acid thickened with silica gel. Sealed, spill-proof, deep-cycle capable. Slow charge only.

AGM — what FRC uses

Absorbed Glass Mat: electrolyte soaked into fiberglass mat between plates. Sealed, any orientation, very high surge current.

Key AGM specs

Nominal voltage2.0 V / cell · 12 V pack
Full charge2.25–2.30 V / cell
Cutoff1.75 V / cell
Energy density30–50 Wh/kg
Cycle life200–500
Self-discharge3–5% / month

Why AGM specifically for FIRST competition robots:

1
Brutal surge current

Drivetrain motors can stall at 100+ amps. AGM handles this without complaint — gel cells struggle here.

2
Safe for students

No fire risk. No spill risk. Survives charging mistakes that would destroy or ignite a lithium pack.

3
Any orientation

Robots flip and roll. The AGM is fully sealed — mounts upside-down, sideways, anywhere.

4
No BMS required

Six cells, two terminals, done. Far simpler electrical system than any lithium alternative.

5
Cheap & standardized

Every FRC team uses the same model. Easy to source, easy to replace, easy to borrow at competition.

6
Voltage sag = grace

Natural drop under heavy load inherently limits current. Lithium delivers full voltage until the BMS slams off — harder to handle gracefully.

AC vs DC

The fundamental distinction before we get to transformers

Fig 7DC is flat — constant voltage, one direction. AC reverses 60 times per second in the US (60 Hz). Most electronics need DC internally; AC must be converted first.

11

Transformers

Two coils on a shared iron core — voltage ratio = turns ratio — only works with AC

Fig 8Step-up transformer (9/5 = ×1.8). Reverse the ratio for step-down. The key: transformers only work with AC because they need a continuously changing magnetic field in the core. Pure DC just heats the primary coil.

TypeTurns ratioEffectExample use
Step-upSecondary has more turnsHigher V out, lower currentPower transmission lines (raises voltage to reduce losses)
Step-downSecondary has fewer turnsLower V out, higher currentWall-wart adapters, doorbells, phone charger bricks
12

Full-bridge rectifier

Converting AC to DC using four diodes — whichever way AC swings, output stays positive on top

Fig 9Four diodes route current so the output always has the same polarity regardless of which way the AC is swinging. The smoothing capacitor fills in the gaps to produce steady DC. Every wall-wart contains this circuit.

13

Buck converters

DC-to-DC step-down at 85–95% efficiency — no heat wasted like a linear regulator

Instead of burning the excess voltage as heat (what a linear regulator does), a buck converter rapidly switches a transistor on and off, stores energy in an inductor, and releases it at the target lower voltage. All the clever conversion happens in microseconds.

Fig 10Switch chops the input. Inductor smooths the current. Capacitor smooths the voltage. The duty cycle of the PWM signal sets the output voltage.

VariantDirectionExample use
BuckSteps voltage down (V out < V in)12V battery → 5V USB, 3.3V MCU
BoostSteps voltage up (V out > V in)3.7V LiPo → 12V fan, 5V USB from single cell
Buck-boostEither directionLaptop chargers, LED drivers
14

MOSFETs & BJTs — NPN vs PNP

The switching elements in buck converters, H-bridges, and motor controllers

Fig 11The emitter arrow direction is the memory aid: NPN arrow points out (Not Pointing iN); PNP arrow points in toward the base (Points iN to Pin). MOSFETs follow the same split: N-channel (low-side) and P-channel (high-side).

Motor H-bridges use combinations of both. Switching diagonal pairs alternately lets a motor run forward, reverse, brake, or coast. The same topology is in every ESC, FRC Victor SP, and Victor SPX.

15

Wireless charging

A transformer with an air gap — same physics, just the core is air instead of iron

Fig 12Wireless charging is transformer physics applied across an air gap. The Qi standard adds a digital handshake over the magnetic link so pad and device can agree on power level. Efficiency is lower (60–80%) because the air gap weakens coupling.

USB Power Delivery (USB PD)

Charger and device negotiate the exact voltage — the whole chain in one standard

Old USB was always 5V at limited current. USB PD lets charger and device hold a conversation over the CC (Configuration Channel) pins in the USB-C cable, agree on a voltage, then the charger reconfigures its internal buck converter to deliver it.
